The ceiling

Canva's maximum design size is 8000 × 3125 px — per axis. The minimum is 40 × 40. From the Canva Help Centre: if the custom size fields turn red, "it means the values are less than or exceed the size limits".

So your width can be 8,000. Your height stops at 3,125, full stop — and an infographic is the one format that is inherently tall, so this is the only design type where that ceiling bites. A proper long-scroll infographic wants four, five, six thousand pixels of height. You cannot type it.

There is no warning text explaining why, no suggestion, no offer to scale it for you. The field simply refuses the number.

The fix — design small, export big

A Canva design downloads as a PNG at between 0.5× and 3.125× its size. That multiplier is the escape hatch: the ceiling is on the canvas, never on the export. So work backwards from the pixels you actually want.

design size    ×  multiplier  =  output
450 × 3000        2×             900 × 6000
600 × 2400        2.5×          1500 × 6000
640 × 3125        3.125×        2000 × 9765

The consequence nobody checks: everything is half (or a third of) the pixels it will end up being, so every photo you place needs at least the multiplier × the resolution it looks like it needs. A photo that is exactly sharp at 450 wide will be soft at 900.

The three shapes — from Canva's own infographic page

Canva recommends three sizes, and they are not variations of each other:

  • Blog — 663 × 2000 px (a tall column)
  • Facebook — 1200 × 628 px (landscape)
  • LinkedIn — 1104 × 736 px (landscape)

There is no single artwork that is both a tall column and a landscape. So stop trying to make one. The tall one is your master — it is where the whole argument lives. The landscape ones are extracts: one section, one number, one claim, re-laid out to fill a wide frame.

Canva's Resize tool changes the canvas but does not re-think the layout, and a tall column squashed into a landscape box is unreadable every time. Three extracts from a ten-section infographic is plenty — and only the sections that work alone. Not the methodology. Not the conclusion that only lands if you read the eight before it.

The ten-second test almost nobody does

Zoom out until the whole thing fits on your screen at about the size of a postage stamp. That is roughly what a feed, a preview thumbnail or a search result shows.

  • Should survive: the shape of it, the colour blocks, and the biggest numbers.
  • All grey texture? The type is too uniform — the sizes are too close together and nothing is leading the eye. Go back and make the numbers bigger. Not the headings.
  • Then check the top two inches on their own. On most destinations that is the part that gets seen first, and sometimes the only part that gets seen at all. If your title and your best number are not both up there, move one of them.

Where the numbers come from

An infographic makes a claim look true whether or not it is — a number set in 72-point type with an icon next to it reads as established fact, and it will be screenshotted, reposted and separated from your source within a day.

So put the source on the image, with a date. A caption does not travel with a screenshot. Two lines of 8-point type is the difference between a reference and a rumour.

Seven checks before you export

  • The design fits inside 8,000 × 3,125
  • You know the multiplier that gets you the pixels you want
  • Every photo is at least that multiplier × the resolution it looks like it needs
  • It still reads at thumbnail size, and the numbers are what survive
  • The title and your best number are both in the top two inches
  • The sources are on the image, with a date
  • The landscape extracts are re-laid out, not squashed

The third is the one nobody checks until the export comes back soft — and by then the design is finished.
