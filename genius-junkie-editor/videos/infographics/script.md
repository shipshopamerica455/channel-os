# How to Make an Infographic in Canva

## Facts verified 2026-10-06

- **THE TRAP — Canva's maximum design size is 8000 × 3125 px, per axis.**
  Canva Help Centre, "Resize designs and size limits": minimum **40 × 40 px**,
  maximum **8000 × 3125 px**, and if the custom size fields turn red "it means
  the values are less than or exceed the size limits". The limit is per axis —
  width *and* height — so the 3,125 px ceiling applies to height.
  An infographic is the one format that is inherently tall, so this is the only
  design type where that ceiling bites. A long-scroll infographic cannot be
  built as a single page at full size.
- **THE FIX — design small, export big.** Same source: a design can be
  downloaded "in PNG format from 0.5 up to 3.125 times its size". So the way to
  get a 900 × 6000 px infographic is to build it at 450 × 3000 and export at
  **2×**. Counter-intuitive, and it is the opposite of what people do.
- **THE SECOND TRAP — Canva's own infographic page gives three different
  shapes.** From `canva.com/create/infographics`, verbatim: for blog posts
  "infographics should be around **663 x 2000 pixels**"; for Facebook "aim for
  **1200 x 628 pixels**"; for LinkedIn "go for **1104 x 736**".
  One is a tall column, two are landscape. The tall one is what everybody
  makes, and then posts to all three.

NOT CLAIMED: how any platform crops an image in its feed, what a specific
social algorithm rewards, engagement or click-through figures, or the price of
any Canva plan. None was checked against the platform's own page in this
session, so the script sends you to test your own destination instead.

## S01 — HOOK (~125 words)

> Canva will not let you make a tall infographic. Their size limit is eight
> thousand pixels by three thousand one hundred and twenty five, and that is
> per axis — so the second number is your height ceiling.
>
> Three thousand one hundred and twenty five pixels. For a format whose whole
> point is that it scrolls. Type a taller number into the custom size box and
> the fields turn red and refuse you.
>
> And then there is the shape problem, which is worse, because Canva causes it
> themselves. Their own infographic page tells you to use six sixty three by
> two thousand for a blog, twelve hundred by six twenty eight for Facebook, and
> eleven oh four by seven thirty six for LinkedIn. One tall column and two
> landscapes.
>
> There is a free infographic size card in the description. Let me build one
> properly.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free card is linked below — the size ceiling, the export arithmetic, and
> the three shapes on one page.
>
> Three principles.
>
> One: the height ceiling is three thousand one hundred and twenty five pixels,
> and you get round it with arithmetic, not by giving up. Design smaller,
> export bigger.
>
> Two: an infographic is not one artwork. It is one piece of information in
> however many shapes you need, and the tall one is the master.
>
> Three: everything in it has to be readable at the size people actually see
> it, which is almost never full size.
>
> One idea, several shapes, legible at a thumbnail.

## S03 — THE SIZE, AND THE CEILING (~160 words)

> Start with the canvas, because this is where you meet the wall.
>
> Custom size. Pixels. And the limits, from Canva's own help pages: minimum
> forty by forty, maximum eight thousand by three thousand one hundred and
> twenty five. Per axis. Type something outside that and the fields turn red —
> their words, "it means the values are less than or exceed the size limits".
>
> So your width can be eight thousand. Your height stops at three thousand one
> hundred and twenty five, full stop.
>
> Put that next to what an infographic actually is. Canva's own suggestion for
> a blog is six six three by two thousand. That fits. But a proper long-scroll
> infographic — eight or ten sections, the kind that gets shared — wants four,
> five, six thousand pixels of height. You cannot type it.
>
> Most people discover this halfway through, delete a section, and ship
> something thinner than they meant.

## S04 — DESIGN SMALL, EXPORT BIG (~155 words)

> Here is the way round it, and it is arithmetic.
>
> Canva lets you download a P N G at between nought point five and three point
> one two five times the design size. That multiplier is the escape hatch.
>
> So work backwards. Decide the pixels you actually want. Say nine hundred wide
> by six thousand tall — a proper long one. Six thousand is over the ceiling,
> so halve both numbers: four fifty by three thousand. Three thousand is under
> three thousand one hundred and twenty five, so Canva accepts it.
>
> Build the whole thing at four fifty by three thousand. Then at download,
> P N G, and set the size multiplier to two times. Out comes nine hundred by
> six thousand.
>
> Same artwork. The ceiling never applied to the file, only to the canvas.
>
> And the multiplier goes to three point one two five, so three thousand one
> hundred and twenty five of height can become nine thousand seven hundred and
> sixty five if you need it.

## S05 — WHAT GOES IN IT (~150 words)

> Now the content, and the discipline that separates an infographic from a
> poster with facts on it.
>
> One idea per section, and one number per idea. If a section needs two
> numbers, it is two sections.
>
> Work top to bottom as a single argument: the claim, then the evidence, then
> the consequence. People read an infographic in one scroll and stop the moment
> it stops making sense.
>
> Use Canva's charts for anything numeric — Elements, search chart, and it
> opens a small table you can paste a spreadsheet column straight into.
>
> And the typography rule that does most of the work: three sizes only. A
> section heading, a body size, and a number size that is much bigger than you
> think. The numbers are what people screenshot.
>
> Everything else — icons, rules, blocks of colour — is there to separate
> sections, not to decorate them.

## S06 — THE TRAP, DEMONSTRATED (~185 words)

> Let me show you the wall, because it does not explain itself.
>
> Custom size. Nine hundred wide. Six thousand tall. And look — the height
> field goes red the moment the number passes three thousand one hundred and
> twenty five. There is no warning text explaining why, no suggestion, no
> "would you like to scale this". It simply will not accept the number.
>
> Now the fix, live. Halve it. Four fifty by three thousand. Accepted
> immediately, because three thousand is under the ceiling.
>
> I build the entire infographic at this size. Everything is half the pixels it
> will end up being — which means, and this matters, every photo I place has to
> be at least twice the resolution it looks like it needs. A photo that is
> exactly sharp at four fifty wide will be soft at nine hundred.
>
> Then download. P N G. And here is the multiplier — set it to two.
>
> Nine hundred by six thousand. The file I was told I could not make.
>
> The ceiling is on the canvas, never on the export.

## S07 — THE THREE SHAPES (~155 words)

> Now the second problem, and Canva creates this one themselves.
>
> Their infographic page recommends three sizes. For a blog, six six three by
> two thousand. For Facebook, twelve hundred by six twenty eight. For LinkedIn,
> eleven oh four by seven thirty six.
>
> Read those again. The first is a tall column, three times higher than it is
> wide. The other two are landscape, wider than they are tall.
>
> They are not variations of each other. There is no single artwork that is
> both.
>
> So stop trying to make one. The tall one is your master — it is where the
> whole argument lives. The landscape ones are extracts: one section, one
> number, one claim, re-laid out to fill a wide frame.
>
> Canva's Resize tool will change the canvas for you, but it will not re-think
> the layout, and a tall column squashed into a landscape box is unreadable in
> every case I have tried.

## S08 — THE EXTRACTS (~145 words)

> So make the extracts deliberately, and make fewer of them than you think.
>
> Duplicate the design, resize to the landscape shape, then delete almost
> everything. One section survives. Re-lay it out as a headline, the single
> number, and one line of context.
>
> That is the post. The tall one is what it links to.
>
> Three extracts from a ten section infographic is plenty, and they should be
> the three sections that work alone. Not the methodology. Not the conclusion
> that only lands if you read the eight sections before it.
>
> And keep them visually of a family — same palette, same type, same number
> treatment — so somebody who sees the extract and then the full one recognises
> it as the same piece of work.
>
> One idea. Several shapes. That is the whole structure.

## S09 — WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE SMALL (~150 words)

> Before you export, the test almost nobody does, and it costs ten seconds.
>
> Zoom out until the whole infographic fits on your screen at about the size of
> a postage stamp. That is roughly what it looks like in a feed, or as a
> preview thumbnail, or in search results.
>
> What should survive at that size: the shape of it, the colour blocks, and the
> biggest numbers. If all you can see is grey texture, the type is too uniform
> — the sizes are too close together and nothing is leading the eye.
>
> Go back and make the numbers bigger. Not the headings, the numbers.
>
> And check the top two inches separately, because on most destinations that is
> the part that gets seen first and sometimes the only part that gets seen at
> all. If your title and your best number are not both up there, move one of
> them.

## S10 — THE EXPORT (~145 words)

> The export, and there are three things to get right.
>
> One: P N G, not J P G, if there is any flat colour or type — and an
> infographic is almost nothing but flat colour and type. J P G will fuzz the
> edges of your lettering.
>
> Two: the size multiplier, which we have already used. Nought point five to
> three point one two five. Set it so the output lands on the pixels you
> actually planned, not whatever the design happens to be.
>
> Three: if it is going anywhere near paper, P D F Print instead, which is
> three hundred D P I. A P N G built for screens will print soft at A3 no
> matter how big the multiplier was.
>
> And export the extracts separately, at their own sizes. Do not resize one
> export into another — you will be scaling pixels that have already been
> scaled once.

## S11 — WHERE THE NUMBERS COME FROM (~140 words)

> A word about the content, because an infographic makes a claim look true
> whether or not it is.
>
> That is the format's whole power and its whole risk. A number set in
> seventy two point type with an icon next to it reads as established fact. It
> will be screenshotted, reposted, and separated from your source within a day.
>
> So put the source on the infographic itself. A small line at the bottom —
> where the figure came from and when. Not a link in the caption, which does
> not travel with the image. On the image.
>
> And date it. Data goes stale, and an undated infographic circulates for years
> looking current.
>
> It costs you two lines of eight point type and it is the difference between a
> reference and a rumour.

## S12 — KEEPING THE FILE (~125 words)

> One habit, same as always.
>
> Keep the Canva design, the exported P N Gs at every size you made, and a
> plain text note of the numbers and where each one came from.
>
> Infographics get asked about. Somebody will query a figure six months from
> now, and "I think I read it somewhere" is not an answer when your name is on
> the image.
>
> Write down the design size and the multiplier too. When you want the same
> infographic at a different size next year, the thing you will not remember is
> whether you built it at four fifty by three thousand and doubled it, or built
> it full size.
>
> Three lines per infographic.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~110 words)

> Before you export.
>
> The design fits inside eight thousand by three thousand one hundred and
> twenty five. You know the multiplier that turns it into the pixels you
> actually want. Every photo in it is at least that multiplier times the
> resolution it looks like it needs. It still reads at thumbnail size, and the
> numbers are what survive. The title and your best number are both in the top
> two inches. The sources are on the image, with a date. And your landscape
> extracts are re-laid out, not squashed.
>
> Seven things. The third is the one nobody checks until the export comes back
> soft.
