## S02 — THE EIGHT NON-NEGOTIABLES (~155 words)

> Before design, the contents — because a beautiful invoice missing one of
> these still causes a payment delay. One: the word "Invoice", visible at
> a glance. Two: a unique invoice number — more on that soon. Three: your
> business details — name, address, email; and if you're tax registered,
> your tax or VAT number; check the rules where you live. Four: the
> client's details — the legal name of the company, not just "Dave".
> Five: the issue date AND the payment due date — a real calendar date,
> not just "net fourteen". Six: line items — what you did, quantity,
> rate, amount. Seven: the totals — subtotal, any tax, and the final
> amount due. Eight: how to pay — the exact account details or link.
> Miss any one of these and the polite version of what happens is an
> email; the expensive version is the end of the month.

missing-field ✕ flash.]

## S04 — HEADER + YOUR BRAND (~135 words)

> The header sets the tone in one second. Left side: your logo — or just
> your business name set in bold type; honestly, clean type beats a
> stretched low-res logo every time. Under it, small: address, email,
> phone, and your tax number if you have one. Right side, the working
> parts: the word INVOICE — large, spaced, confident, the biggest text
> on the page. Under it: the invoice number, the issue date, and the due
> date, labelled clearly. One accent colour, pulled from your logo with
> Canva's eyedropper, used only for the word Invoice, the table header,
> and the total. That's the entire branding strategy — one colour, used
> three times, and it reads as designed.

## S04b — THE NUMBERING SYSTEM (~155 words)

> The invoice number looks like bureaucracy — it's actually your payment
> tracking system, and one day, your tax record. Use a format that sorts
> itself: INV, dash, the year, dash, a three-digit counter. INV-2026-001,
> then 002, then 003. Three rules. Sequential, no gaps you can't explain
> — accountants and tax offices read missing numbers as missing income.
> Never reuse a number, even for a cancelled job — cancel it in your
> records and move on. And never start at 001 if it embarrasses you —
> nobody wants to be a client's first customer, so start at a believable
> number like 041 and keep counting. Keep a simple sheet — number,
> client, amount, date sent, date paid — and Friday afternoons, you scan
> one column and know exactly who owes you what. That column has paid
> for this video many times over.

[VISUAL: INV-2026-041 counter ticks; sequential row with a gap ✕; tracker
sheet with paid/unpaid column.]

## S05 — THE LINE-ITEM TABLE (~175 words)

> The table is where disputes are born or prevented, so build it for a
> reader who wants to say no. Four columns: description, quantity or
> hours, rate, amount. In Canva: Elements, search "table", insert, and
> strip the styling — no fills, light lines, generous row height, your
> accent only on the header row. Numbers right-aligned so the digits
> stack; descriptions left-aligned. Now the writing rule that gets you
> paid: describe outcomes, not activity. "Homepage redesign — desktop
> and mobile" is a line a manager approves without asking; "design
> work, twelve hours" is a line that gets queried. One line per
> deliverable, five to eight lines maximum — if the project was bigger
> than that, group it, and attach the detail separately if anyone asks.
> And leave the last row empty in your template — future you will
> always need one more line than planned.
>

## S06 — TOTALS THAT CANNOT BE MISSED (~140 words)

> The totals block, bottom right — where every reader's eye goes first,
> so make the journey effortless. Three lines, right-aligned: subtotal,
> then tax if you charge it, labelled with its rate — check what applies
> to you locally — and then the total. Now the design move: the final
> amount due gets a filled accent box, white bold type, the largest
> number on the page. Not underlined, not italic — boxed. There should
> be exactly one number a skimming client can quote back, and it's this
> one. If you take deposits, show the maths: total, minus deposit
> received, amount now due — three lines that prevent three emails.
> Everything else on the page can be quiet; the total is allowed to be
> loud.

## S07 — PAYMENT TERMS THAT WORK (~155 words)

> Now the part that changes when you get paid. First: a due date, not
> just terms. "Net thirty" means nothing to a busy client; "Due the
> fifteenth of September" is an instruction. Common practice is net
> fourteen for freelancers, net thirty for corporates — but shorter
> terms are normal now, and clients accept "due in seven days" far more
> often than freelancers dare to ask. Second: make paying frictionless.
> The exact bank details — account name, number, sort code or routing —
> or a payment link right on the invoice; every extra step a client
> needs costs you days. Third, the quiet deterrent: one line noting
> that overdue invoices may incur a late fee, phrased politely and
> according to your local rules — you'll almost never charge it, but
> its presence moves you up the payment pile.

[VISUAL: terms card Net-30 → real date morph; payment details block;
late-fee line stamps.]

## S08 — THE FOOTER: POLITE POWER (~120 words)

> The footer, the last thing they read. Three things live here. A thank
> you — "Thank you for your business" is fine; warm beats clever. A
> notes line for the human context: the PO number the client gave you,
> or "final invoice for the March project". And your payment terms
> repeated in one sentence. What does NOT go here: apologies. "Sorry to
> chase" and "whenever is convenient" cost real money — this is a
> business document, and its tone is friendly and certain. You did the
> work; the invoice's whole personality is: of course this will be
> paid.

[VISUAL: footer builds; apology phrases get struck through ✕; tone chip.]

## S08b — INTERNATIONAL CLIENTS (~115 words)

> Invoicing across borders? Three additions. State the currency
> explicitly — not just a symbol; "all amounts in US dollars" kills the
> most awkward email in freelancing. Say who covers transfer fees — one
> line: "payment fees are the payer's responsibility" — or a forty
> dollar wire fee quietly becomes your problem. And offer one sensible
> payment route for their region: a multi-currency account or a payment
> link often costs you two percent, but being easy to pay in someone's
> own currency is frequently the difference between net seven and net
> forty-five. International clients are wonderful — invoice them like
> you've done it before.

## S09 — EXPORT + THE SEND ROUTINE (~145 words)

> Export and send — where professionals separate themselves. Share,
> Download, PDF Standard — an invoice is read on screens; keep the file
> small and crisp. Name it so it files itself in the client's system:
> Invoice, the number, your business name — because "invoice-final.pdf"
> from forty suppliers is how invoices get lost. Send it as an
> attachment, not a link they'll lose — with a two-line email: what it's
> for, the amount, the due date. Subject line: Invoice, number, your
> name. And one habit that makes you look like a company: send it to
> the right person — ask once, early, "who should invoices go to?" —
> because the fastest way to be paid late is to invoice someone who
> can't pay you.
