# Business Presentation in Canva 2026 — the deck they open is not the deck you built

## Facts verified 2026-08-29 — Canva's own Help Centre

**THE TRAP — export to PowerPoint and Canva itself tells you it will change.**

From "Choose the right download file type for your Canva design"
(`canva.com/help/download-file-types`), verbatim:

> "Designs downloaded as PPTX or DOCX files may look different when opened in
> Microsoft Powerpoint or Microsoft Word."

> "You may need to make edits, or download and install the fonts you used in
> Canva to your device."

> "Animations and embedded videos are not yet supported in Microsoft PowerPoint
> and Microsoft Word. If your design includes animated elements and/or videos,
> they may not play as expected when downloaded as PPTX or DOCX."

So a business deck — the one design most likely to be **emailed to somebody
else** — is the one Canva is least able to hand over intact. The client opens
your .pptx, their machine does not have your font, PowerPoint substitutes it,
the line breaks move, and every animation you built is dead.

They do not know that happened. They just think your deck looks scrappy.

**THE HONEST OPTIONS (all three go in the video):**
1. **Send a PDF.** Fixed layout, fonts baked in. You lose animation and any
   video. For a deck someone reads on their own, this is almost always right.
2. **Send a link and present it yourself** from Canva. Everything works
   exactly as built. Requires you in the room, or on the call.
3. **They genuinely need an editable .pptx** — so design in a font they will
   already have, keep animation out of it, export, and then **open the file
   yourself** before it goes anywhere.

**Say plainly:** there is no export that gives you an editable PowerPoint AND
your Canva fonts AND your animations. Pick which one you are giving up.

## S01 — HOOK (~130 words)

> You build a really good deck in Canva. Clean type, a proper grid, everything
> lined up. You download it as a PowerPoint, you email it to the client, and
> what they open is not what you made. The font has been swapped for something
> generic, so your headlines now run onto two lines and push everything down.
> The animations do nothing. And here is the part that stings: they will never
> tell you. They will just quietly think your work is a bit scrappy. This is not
> a bug and it is not you doing it wrong — Canva's own help pages say it happens,
> in plain English, and almost nobody reads that page. Today we build a proper
> business deck, and we handle the handover so it survives the trip.

## S02 — THREE WAYS A DECK LEAVES CANVA (~150 words)

> Every deck you make leaves Canva by one of three doors, and each one costs you
> something different. Door one, a link — you present it yourself, straight from
> Canva, and everything works exactly as you built it. Costs you nothing, but it
> needs you there. Door two, a PDF — the layout is frozen and the fonts are
> baked in, so it looks identical on any machine on earth. You lose animation and
> anything that moves. Door three, a PowerPoint file — the only one that is
> editable by the person receiving it, and the only one that changes on the way.
> Most people pick door three by reflex, because a deck feels like it should be a
> PowerPoint. That reflex is what causes the problem.

## S03 — SETTING UP (~155 words)

> Create a design, and choose Presentation, sixteen by nine. That is nineteen
> twenty by ten eighty pixels, and it is the shape of every screen and projector
> you will meet. Do not use four by three unless someone has specifically asked
> for it. Now the part that makes everything after it faster: set up your
> margins before you place a single thing. Turn on rulers — that is View, then
> Show rulers — and drag guides in about eighty pixels from each edge. Nothing
> touches those edges. Every slide in the deck will use the same margin, and that
> single decision is what makes a deck look designed rather than assembled. The
> second decision: two font sizes and two only. One for headings, one for
> everything else.

## S04 — TITLE AND SECTION SLIDES (~145 words)

> A business deck has four kinds of slide and no more. Title, section, content,
> and closing. Build them once, reuse them forever. The title slide is the
> easiest to overwork — resist. The name of the thing, who it is for, the date.
> That is it. White space is doing the work. Section slides are the ones people
> skip and they are the reason long decks feel long. A section slide is a full
> bleed colour, a number, and three or four words. It costs you nothing and it
> tells the room where they are. If your deck is more than about ten slides, you
> want them. Add them now, before you have content to fit around.
>
> One more thing about the title slide, because it is where most decks lose the
> room before they have started. If there is a date on it, make it the date of
> the meeting, not the date you built it. A deck dated three weeks ago reads as
> recycled, whether it is or not.

## S05 — THE CONTENT SLIDE THAT DOES THE WORK (~200 words)

> This is the one you will build twenty times, so build it properly once. Left
> two thirds for the point, right third for the evidence. The point is a
> statement, not a label — not "Q3 results", but "Q3 revenue grew nineteen
> percent". Somebody reading the deck without you should get the argument from
> the headlines alone. Underneath it, no more than three supporting lines. If you
> need six, you need two slides. On the right, one piece of evidence: a chart, a
> number, a photograph. One. Two things fight each other and the audience picks
> neither. Now duplicate that slide — command D — and you have your second one.
> Change the words, keep the geometry. The temptation is to vary the layout to
> keep it interesting. Do not. A deck where every slide sits in the same place is
> a deck people can read at speed, and speed is the whole point when someone is
> deciding something.

→ **AD BREAK 1**

## S06 — THE HANDOVER TRAP (~185 words)

> Now the bit nobody covers. You are going to send this to someone. Watch what
> Canva says happens if you send it as a PowerPoint file — this is their own
> help page, not my opinion. First: designs downloaded as P P T X may look
> different when opened in Microsoft PowerPoint. Second: you may need to
> download and install the fonts you used in Canva onto your device — and note
> that "your device" means the recipient's device too, and they are not going to
> do that. Third: animations and embedded videos are not yet supported in
> Microsoft PowerPoint, so if your design has animated elements or video, they
> may not play as expected. Read those three together and you get the whole
> problem. The editable file is the one that changes. The file that does not
> change is the one they cannot edit. There is no export that gives you both, and
> pretending otherwise is how people end up embarrassed in somebody else's
> meeting.

## S07 — SO WHICH DOOR DO YOU PICK (~165 words)

> It depends on one question: does this deck need to be read, presented, or
> edited? If it is being read — sent ahead, forwarded, skimmed on a phone —
> send a PDF. Fonts baked in, identical everywhere, nothing to install. If you
> are presenting it, send nothing. Present from Canva, share the link afterwards,
> and let them look at the real thing. If they genuinely need to edit it, and
> sometimes they do, then design for that from the start: pick a font they will
> already have — Arial, Calibri, Georgia, something that ships with the
> operating system — keep animation out of the deck entirely, export the
> PowerPoint, and then open that file yourself and look at every slide before you
> send it. That last step takes two minutes and it is the difference between
> finding the problem and your client finding it.

## S08 — CHARTS AND NUMBERS (~150 words)

> One chart per slide, and the chart has a headline that says what it means. Not
> "Revenue by quarter" — that is a label, and a label makes the audience do the
> work. "Revenue grew every quarter except the one we changed pricing" is a
> headline. Same chart, and now they know what they are looking at. Use Canva's
> chart element rather than pasting a picture of one from a spreadsheet, because
> a pasted screenshot is at screen resolution and it will be soft on a projector.
> Strip the chart back: no gridlines you do not need, no legend if you can label
> the lines directly, no three-dimensional anything ever. And check the numbers
> in the deck against the source one final time before it goes. Nobody has ever
> been forgiven for a wrong number because the slide looked nice.

→ **AD BREAK 2**

## S09 — PRESENTING IT LIVE (~160 words)

> If you are presenting from Canva there are two things worth knowing. Presenter
> view gives you your notes and a timer on your screen while the audience sees
> only the slides — you get to it from Present, then Presenter view, and you
> need two screens for it to be any use. And if you are on a video call, share
> the window rather than the whole screen, so nobody watches you get a
> notification. Put your notes in the notes field rather than on the slide. A
> slide with your script on it is a slide the audience reads instead of listening
> to you, and you end up narrating something they finished thirty seconds ago.
> Two lines of prompt per slide is plenty. If you need more than that, you know
> the material less well than you think, and no amount of text on the screen
> fixes that.

## S10 — MAKING THE DECK REUSABLE (~150 words)

> Here is how this stops being a one-off job. Once you have your four slide
> types, delete the content and keep the shell. Save that as your own starting
> point — duplicate the whole design and name it something like Deck Template,
> then every future deck starts from a copy of that instead of a blank page. Put
> the brand colours in as a palette while you are there. What you have built is
> not a presentation, it is a system, and the second deck takes about a fifth of
> the time the first one did. If you work with other people, share that shell
> with them, because the fastest way to make everything look consistent is to
> make the consistent thing the easiest thing to start from.
>
> And keep one slide in the shell that you never use in a real deck: a page with
> your four layouts side by side, shrunk down. It is the fastest way to remember
> what the system is when you come back to it in three months and have forgotten
> every decision you made today.

## S11 — BEFORE YOU SEND IT (~145 words)

> Right before it goes, four checks. One: read only the headlines, top to
> bottom. Do they tell the story on their own? If they do not, the deck does not
> work without you in the room. Two: does anything touch the margins you set at
> the start? Three: if you are sending a PowerPoint, open the exported file and
> look at every slide — that is the check this whole video is about. Four: the
> numbers. Against the source, one more time. And name the file properly.
> Not "deck final version two". The company name, what it is, and the date, so
> that when they search their inbox in three weeks they can actually find it.
> That last one sounds trivial and it is the one people thank you for.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~115 words)

> Everything in one place. Sixteen by nine. Guides eighty pixels in, set before
> you design. Four slide types: title, section, content, closing. Two font sizes.
> Headlines that make a statement, not a label. One piece of evidence per slide.
> Charts with a headline and no gridlines you do not need. To hand it over:
> reading means PDF, presenting means a link, editing means a system font and no
> animation — and open the exported file yourself before it goes. Keep the empty
> shell as your template. And read the headlines alone before you send.

## S13 — THE PACK (~90 words)

> If you would rather start from a deck that is already set up, the kit has it.
> The editable Canva template at sixteen by nine with the margins and grid in
> place — title, section, three content layouts, a chart slide and a closing

## S14 — OUTRO (~80 words)

> The one thing to take away: the editable file is the one that changes, and
> Canva's own documentation says so. Decide whether your deck is for reading,
> presenting or editing before you export it, and the problem never happens. If
> you want the video that pairs with this one, it is on screen now. Thanks for
> watching, and I will see you in the next one.
