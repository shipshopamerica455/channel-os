# Canva for Teachers — the button that gives every student their own copy

## Facts verified 2026-08-19 — all from Canva's own Help Centre

**THE TRAP — the default share puts thirty students in one file:**

- A normal share link gives edit / comment / view access **to the same design**.
  Paste that into Google Classroom and every student opens the identical file.
- The classroom tools do the opposite. From "Share class assignments in Canva":
  > "From the editor, select the **Assign** button in the top corner, or select
  > the **Share** menu, then **Assign**."
- Schoology setup, verbatim: select "Link text and then **New design for each
  student**" — "they'll get their own editable copy of the design."
- Canvas setup, verbatim: "**Students will be given their own copy of the design
  that you share.** They can edit it and submit it back to you for review in
  SpeedGrader."

**The two expiries — both documented, both bite:**

- Assignment **link**: "The link remains active for **30 days**. You can
  deactivate it anytime by selecting **Deactivate link**."
- Assignment **code**: "**The code will expire in 4 hours.**"
  So a code generated the night before is dead by first period. Use the link.
- Students need a Canva account: "Students will need a Canva account to access
  assignments. If they don't have one, they'll be prompted to sign up."

**The submission loop (from the same article, student-side):**

- Student opens the assignment, completes it, selects **Send to teacher**.
- "After you send your assignment, **your design becomes read-only**."
- Teacher can request changes → student edits → **Resubmit**.
- "If you want to continue working on your ideas without affecting your
  submitted assignment, select **Make a copy**. Your original submission stays
  unchanged for your teacher to review."
- Teacher statuses on Classwork: **Not submitted · To review · Done ·
  Resubmission requested**.

**LMS routes:**

- Google Classroom: Assign → Assign in Google Classroom. "you can select **up to
  20 classes at once**". And: "To assign to different platforms (e.g. Canvas),
  **create a new copy of the design first**."
- Direct integrations listed by Canva: Canvas, Schoology, D2L, Moodle,
  Blackboard, Google Classroom, Microsoft Teams.

**ELIGIBILITY — say this honestly, a lot of the audience is not eligible:**

Eligible (must be *currently active* in the role):
- certified K-12 teacher from a formally accredited school, currently teaching
- certified K-12 school librarian · learning support assistant or teacher ·
  curriculum specialist
- certified teacher at a technical/vocational school serving primary/secondary
- plus K-12 schools, districts, departments of education as organisations

**NOT eligible**, verbatim from Canva's list:
- "Certified K-12 teachers, who are **not currently in teaching positions**"
- "**Public librarians** (e.g. city or county librarians)"
- "**Preschool or daycare** assistants"
- "**Colleges, Universities**, and other higher education institutions,
  including lecturers and staff" · college and university students
- "Continuing/Further education institutions" · "Adult education institutions"
- "Organizations providing **extracurricular activities** outside of school"
- "**Parents** of children who are in K-12 education"
- "**Homeschool educators** who are not licensed or currently employed with a
  K-12 institution"

Other verified specifics:
- "Canva Education is **free** for eligible teachers and students. It's
  available all over the world, and isn't restricted to any country."
- Verification: a verified educational email domain gets access right away;
  otherwise upload a document. "Teachers in Japan, Australia, Canada, New
  Zealand, the United Kingdom, the United States of America, South Korea, and
  Europe must only provide a copy of their **teaching license**."
- Students: K-12 age only, not college. "Eligible students can only get access
  **if their teacher invites them**."
- "Teachers need to get **parental consent for children below 13**."
- Canva states Education is "FERPA, COPPA, and GDPR compliant".
- Canva Education includes the premium feature set free, plus Affinity access.

**Not claimed here:** anything about Canva Campus pricing, or how any specific
school district's rollout works. Not documented well enough to state.

Target: ~1,955 words @ 3.70 w/s ≈ 8 min 48 s. Ad breaks after S05 and S09.

## S01 — HOOK (~135 words)

> Here is a thing that happens in about week two. You make a really nice
> worksheet in Canva. You copy the link, you paste it into Google Classroom, and
> thirty students open it. And they are all in the same file. Same one. Student
> four deletes a text box and it vanishes off everybody's screen at once,
> including the two who had already finished. That is not a bug, and it is not
> you being stupid. A normal share link shares one design. What you actually
> want is for every student to get their own copy, and there is a button that
> does exactly that. It is not the Share button, it is next to it, and most
> teachers never press it. Let me show you the whole thing properly.

## S02 — SAME FILE, OR THEIR OWN COPY (~150 words)

> This is the only idea in this video, so let us be clear about it. There are
> two completely different things you can do with a Canva design, and they look
> almost identical when you are setting them up. The first is sharing, which is
> what the Share button does. Edit, comment, or view — all three of those point
> at the same single design. That is right when you and another teacher are
> building a scheme of work together and you both want to see the same page. The
> second is handing out, which is what Assign does. Every student gets their own
> copy to work in, and their copy comes back to you when they are done. Sharing
> is for colleagues. Assigning is for students. Almost every classroom problem I
> have seen with Canva is somebody doing the first when they meant the second.

## S03 — CAN YOU ACTUALLY GET THIS (~165 words)

> Before you spend an evening on this, check you can have it, because Canva's
> eligibility list is narrower than people assume, and I would rather you find
> out now. Canva Education is free, it is worldwide, and it is not restricted by
> country. You qualify if you are currently active as a certified primary or
> secondary teacher at an accredited school, and also if you are a school
> librarian, a learning support assistant, or a curriculum specialist, and also
> if you teach at a technical or vocational school serving those ages. Now the
> other list, because Canva is explicit about it. Not eligible: qualified
> teachers who are not currently in a teaching post. Public librarians. Preschool
> and daycare. Colleges, universities, and their lecturers and staff. Adult and
> further education. Anyone running extracurricular activities outside school.
> Parents. And homeschool educators who are not licensed or employed by a K-12
> institution. If you are on that second list, do not close the video — I have
> got you at the end.

## S04 — GETTING SET UP (~155 words)

> Sign-up is quick if your email is right. Go to the Canva Education sign-up
> page, choose Teacher, and if you sign up with a verified educational email
> domain you get access straight away. If you do not, you upload one document
> showing your name, your school, and your position. If you are in the UK, the
> US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea or Europe, Canva says a
> copy of your teaching licence on its own is enough. Then your students, and
> there are two rules here that matter. Students can only get access if you
> invite them, from your settings — they cannot just sign themselves up into
> your class. And for any child under thirteen, you need parental consent first.
> That is Canva's requirement, not mine. Canva states the product is FERPA,
> COPPA and GDPR compliant, which is the sentence your safeguarding lead will
> ask you for.

## S05 — BUILDING THE ACTIVITY (~195 words)

> Now the actual thing you are going to hand out. I am building a one page
> activity, because a one page activity is what gets used and a twelve page
> booklet is what gets printed once and abandoned. Start from a blank A4, or
> from one of the education templates if you want the heavy lifting done. Top
> strip: title, and a line for the student's name. Do not skip the name line —
> you would be amazed. Then the body, and here is the bit that makes it work as
> a digital activity rather than a printout on a screen. Leave real empty boxes.
> A rectangle with a thin border and nothing in it is an answer space, and the
> student types straight into it. If it is drag-and-drop, put the pieces down the
> side in a column so they are obviously moveable. Last, lock everything you do
> not want touched. Select your title, your instructions, your borders, and use
> the lock. Because a student will drag your heading into the sea, not out of
> mischief, but because they clicked slightly wrong and did not notice.

→ **AD BREAK 1**

## S06 — THE ASSIGN BUTTON (~180 words)

> Right. The button. With your activity open, look at the top corner and you
> will see Assign. It is also inside the Share menu, second item, if you would
> rather find it there. Press it and Canva gives you two ways to hand this out,
> and they are not equivalent. The first is share via link. Canva copies the
> assignment link to your clipboard and you paste it wherever your class lives —
> Google Classroom, Teams, an email, your VLE. That link stays active for thirty
> days, and there is a Deactivate link button when you want it dead sooner. The
> second is an assignment code, which is the one you read out and they type in.
> And here is the number worth writing down: the code expires in four hours. So
> if you set up a code the night before, it is already dead when the bell goes.
> Generate the code in the lesson. For anything you plan in advance, use the
> link. Both of these do the important thing: every student gets their own copy.

## S07 — WHAT THE STUDENT ACTUALLY SEES (~160 words)

> From their side it is genuinely simple, which is the point. They will need a
> Canva account — if they do not have one they get prompted to make one, so if
> you are doing this cold with a class, budget five minutes for that and not
> thirty seconds. They open the assignment from the link, from the code, or from
> the Classroom tab on their Canva homepage. They do the work in their own copy.
> Then they press Send to teacher. Now, one behaviour to warn them about, because
> otherwise you will get a hand up every time: once they send it, their design
> becomes read only. That is deliberate — it is the equivalent of handing the
> book in. If they need to change something, you request changes and they
> resubmit. And if they just want to keep playing with their idea without
> touching what they handed in, there is Make a copy, which leaves the
> submission alone.

## S08 — MARKING WITHOUT THIRTY TABS (~145 words)

> Everything they send lands in one place: Classwork, on your Canva homepage.
> And the useful part is that it is a status list, not an inbox. Every
> assignment sits in one of four states. Not submitted — who still owes you it.
> To review — waiting for you. Done — approved. And resubmission requested,
> which is the ones you sent back. So chasing is just reading the first column
> instead of remembering. If you teach more than one class, set your classes up,
> because then you get a class dropdown on Classwork and on the student list
> inside each assignment, and you can filter down to just the group in front of
> you. And feedback is not only a comment box — you can drop sticker comments
> onto the design itself, which younger classes respond to far better than a
> paragraph they will not read.

## S09 — IF YOUR SCHOOL LIVES IN AN LMS (~175 words)

> If your school runs everything through a platform, you do not have to leave
> it. Canva integrates directly with Canvas, Schoology, D2L, Moodle, Blackboard,
> Google Classroom and Microsoft Teams. Google Classroom is the neatest: Assign,
> then assign in Google Classroom, connect the account once, fill in the title
> and the due date, and you can push the same activity to up to twenty classes
> in one go. One thing to know, and it is the kind of thing that wastes an
> afternoon: if you want the same design assigned on a different platform as
> well, Canva says make a new copy of the design first. In Canvas and Schoology
> you set it up as an assignment and pick Canva as the tool, and Schoology
> spells the important setting out in plain words — new design for each student.
> Same idea as the Assign button, just wearing your school's uniform. Students
> get their own copy, submit it back, and you mark it where you already mark
> everything else.

→ **AD BREAK 2**

## S10 — THE THING THAT SAVES THE MOST TIME (~145 words)

> Here is what actually gets a term back. Do not build activities one at a time
> when you need one. Build a small set of shells, once, and reuse them for
> everything. A do-now, four boxes and a title. An exit ticket, three questions
> and a confidence scale. A vocabulary sheet, word, definition, sentence,
> picture. A comparison grid. A blank labelled diagram frame. A reading response
> page. Six shells, no subject content in any of them. Then Tuesday's lesson is
> not designing, it is typing this week's words into a page that already exists
> and pressing Assign. Every one comes out looking consistent, because it is
> literally the same page. This is the single biggest difference between
> teachers who use Canva happily and teachers who quietly stop after half a
> term.

## S11 — IF YOU ARE NOT ELIGIBLE (~150 words)

> I promised I would come back to this. If you are a tutor, a homeschool parent,
> a lecturer, a public librarian, or a teacher who is not currently in post, you
> cannot have the education account or the Assign button. You can still do the
> important part. Open your design, go to Share, and choose template link.
> Canva's own description of it is the whole point: anyone with the link can use
> this design as a template. Which means when your learner opens it, they get
> their own copy, and your original is untouched. No shared file, no thirty
> people in one document. What you do not get is the marking side — no Classwork
> list, no statuses, no submit button — so they send work back the way they
> already do. But the actual teaching problem, everybody gets their own copy, is
> solved with one link, on a free account.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~115 words)

> Before you hand anything to a class. Eligibility checked, so you are not
> building on an account you cannot keep. Students invited by you, and parental
> consent sorted for anyone under thirteen. Answer boxes actually empty, so they
> can type in them. Titles, instructions and borders locked. Handing out with
> Assign, not with a share link. Link if you planned it, code only if you are
> generating it in the room, because the code dies after four hours. Classes set
> up so Classwork can filter. And your reusable shells saved somewhere you will
> find them again, because that is the bit that pays you back every week for the
> rest of the year.

## S14 — OUTRO (~80 words)

> Nearly everything that goes wrong with Canva in a classroom is one confusion:
> sharing a design instead of handing out copies of it. Assign for students,
> Share for colleagues, template link if you cannot have Assign. Get that right
> and the rest is just making nice worksheets, which is the fun part. If you
> want the worksheet side properly, there is a full lesson on that on the
> channel. See you in the next one.
