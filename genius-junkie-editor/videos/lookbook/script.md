# How to Make a Lookbook in Canva — the colour setting that is behind the paywall

## Facts verified 2026-08-26 — Canva's own Help Centre

**THE TRAP — the colour profile you need for print is Pro-only.**

From Canva's "Choose the right download file type" page, verbatim:

> "PDF Print: For printing with a resolution of 300 dpi, with bleed, crop marks,
> and color profile (RGB or CMYK) selection option"

And on the same page, the Pro-gated list:

> Quality, file size export limits, scale, compression, **CMYK color profile
> options**, and transparent PNGs/SVGs are restricted to Canva Pro, Teams,
> Education, and Nonprofit accounts.

So: **PDF Print is available, but the CMYK selection inside it is not** on a free
account. Say it exactly that way — do not claim PDF Print itself is paywalled,
because Canva's page does not say that.

**Why it matters for a lookbook specifically.** A lookbook is the one thing on
this slate people genuinely send to a printer. Screens are RGB and lit from
behind; printing is CMYK and lit from the front. If you hand a print shop an RGB
file, *they* convert it, with their profile and their judgement — and the
saturated colours go first. Bright orange, electric blue and anything neon land
noticeably duller than the screen. Nobody tells you this happened; you just get
a box of catalogues that look flat.

**The honest options on a free account (all three go in the video):**
1. Export **PDF Print in RGB** and tell the print shop, in writing, that it is
   RGB and ask them to convert. A good printer will. Ask for a proof.
2. **Design in colours that survive the conversion** — avoid the neons, and
   check a bright colour against a printed swatch before you commit a whole run.
3. Pay for one month of Pro for the export, then cancel. Legitimate, and worth
   saying out loud since the audience is cost-conscious.

**BLEED — the second trap, and this one is free to fix.** PDF Print offers bleed
and crop marks. Without bleed, any background that runs to the edge of the page
gets a white sliver when the guillotine drifts by a millimetre — and it always
drifts. Canva's bleed is a checkbox at download; the design has to actually
extend into it.

**RESOLUTION.** 300 dpi is what PDF Print renders at. A product photo pulled off
a web page at screen size will look fine in the Canva editor and soft on paper.
The check is to zoom to one hundred percent in the editor: if it is soft there,
it is worse in print.

## S01 — HOOK (~130 words)

> You design a lookbook in Canva. On screen it is gorgeous — the colours are
> exactly the ones you picked. You send the file to a print shop, you pay for
> fifty copies, and what turns up is flat. The bright colours have gone dull,
> and there is a thin white line down the edge of two pages. Neither of those is
> the printer being careless. Both of them are settings, and one of them is
> sitting behind Canva's paywall in a way almost nobody mentions. Today we build
> a proper lookbook, and I will show you exactly where the colour goes wrong,
> what you can do about it on a free account, and the one free checkbox that
> stops the white line. This is the video I wish existed before I paid for a
> print run.

## S02 — TWO LOOKBOOKS, NOT ONE (~145 words)

> First thing to get straight, because it changes every decision after it. You
> are not making one lookbook, you are making two exports of the same design. One
> is for screens — it gets emailed, it gets put on a website, people scroll it on
> a phone. The other is for paper, and it goes to a printer. They want different
> files. The screen one wants to be small enough to email and it lives in RGB,
> which is the colour model screens use. The paper one wants three hundred dots
> per inch, it wants bleed, and it really wants to be in CMYK, which is the
> colour model ink uses. Same design, two exports, and if you only ever make the
> first one you will be fine. The trouble starts the moment you print.

## S03 — SIZE, AND WHY BLEED EXISTS (~165 words)

> Set the page size before you design anything, because changing it later
> reflows the whole thing. For a lookbook, A four landscape is the safe choice —
> two hundred and ninety seven millimetres wide by two hundred and ten tall. It
> is a size every print shop on earth can handle, and landscape suits clothing
> and product photography better than portrait. Now, bleed. When a printer makes
> your lookbook, they print onto a slightly larger sheet and then cut it down
> with a guillotine. That cut is accurate to about a millimetre, which sounds
> fine until you realise a one millimetre drift on a page with a coloured
> background leaves a thin white stripe down the edge. Bleed is the fix: you
> extend the background past the edge of the page, so when the blade drifts, it
> is still cutting through your design. Canva handles this at download. You just
> have to have designed for it.

## S04 — SETTING UP (~145 words)

> Create a design, custom size, millimetres, two ninety seven by two ten. Now
> before anything else, turn on rulers and guides — that is View, then Show
> rulers, or command semicolon. Drag a guide in about five millimetres from each
> edge. That is your margin, and nothing important — no text, no faces, no
> prices — goes outside it. This is the same idea as the safe zone on a phone
> wallpaper: the edge of the page is not a place you can trust. Now add your
> pages. A lookbook is usually a cover, then spreads in pairs, then a contact
> page at the back. Eight to sixteen pages is normal. Add them now as blank
> pages so you can see the shape of the thing before you start filling it.

## S05 — BUILDING A SPREAD (~200 words)

> Here is the layout that does most of the work, and it is deliberately boring.
> One big image on the left page, bleeding off all three outer edges. On the
> right page, two or three smaller images stacked, and a block of type. That
> asymmetry is what makes it look designed rather than assembled. To make an
> image bleed properly, drag it past the edge of the canvas — actually past it,
> so part of the photo is outside the page. Canva will let you, and that overhang
> is what gets trimmed. Now the type. Product name, one line of description,
> price. Keep the description to a single line — a lookbook is not a catalogue,
> it is an invitation to ask. Use two font sizes and two only: one for the
> product name, one for everything else. The fastest way to make this look
> amateur is four sizes and three fonts. Once one spread works, duplicate the
> page and swap the photos. The grid staying identical across spreads is the
> entire trick — it reads as a house style, and it is less work.

→ **AD BREAK 1**

## S06 — THE COLOUR TRAP (~185 words)

> Now the important part. Screens make colour with light — red, green, blue,
> added together. Print makes colour with ink — cyan, magenta, yellow and black,
> stacked on paper. They can't do all the same colours. The bright ones,
> especially neon orange, electric blue, anything that glows on screen, simply do
> not exist in ink, so they come out as the closest ink can manage, which is
> duller. Canva's PDF Print export has a setting for this — Canva's own wording
> is "color profile, RGB or CMYK, selection option". But on the same help page,
> Canva lists CMYK colour profile options among the things restricted to Pro,
> Teams, Education and Nonprofit accounts. So PDF Print is there on a free
> account. The CMYK choice inside it is not. Which means on the free plan your
> print file leaves in RGB, and somebody else's software decides what your brand
> colours become. That is the whole trap, and it is invisible until the box
> arrives.

## S07 — WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT (~160 words)

> Three honest options, and I would not pretend one of them is free and perfect.
> One: export PDF Print in RGB and tell the printer in writing that it is RGB,
> and ask them to convert and send you a proof. Any decent print shop does this
> daily and would rather be told. Two: design in colours that survive. Deep
> colours convert well. Neons do not. If your brand colour is a bright one, get a
> printed swatch before you commit to a run of fifty. Three, and I will say it
> plainly because this audience watches the money: pay for one month of Pro,
> export your file, cancel. That is a few pounds against a print run that costs
> a lot more than that. What I would not do is design the whole thing in neon,
> export in RGB, order fifty, and hope.

## S08 — PHOTOS THAT SURVIVE PAPER (~150 words)

> Print renders at three hundred dots per inch, which is roughly four times the
> detail a screen needs. So a photo that looks perfect in the editor can print
> soft, and you will not find out until it is too late. Here is the check, and it
> takes five seconds per image: zoom the editor to one hundred percent and look
> at the photo at actual size. If it is soft there, it is worse on paper. If you
> are shooting the products yourself, shoot bigger than you think — you can
> always throw pixels away. If you are using supplied images, ask for the print
> versions, not the ones off the website. And do not scale a small photo up to
> fill a page. Enlarging does not add detail, it just makes the softness bigger.

## S09 — EXPORTING BOTH VERSIONS (~160 words)

> Two exports, in this order. Print first. Share, Download, file type PDF Print.
> Tick crop marks and bleed — that is the checkbox that saves you from the white
> stripe, and it is free. If you have Pro, set the colour profile to CMYK here.
> If you do not, leave it and note that it is RGB for the printer. Download it,
> then actually open it and look at page one at full size before you send it
> anywhere. Now the screen version. Share, Download, PDF Standard. No crop marks,
> no bleed — nobody wants crop marks on a file they are reading on a laptop.
> This one is smaller, it emails, and it is the one that goes on your website.
> Name them clearly, something like lookbook print and lookbook screen, because
> sending a printer the wrong one wastes a week.

→ **AD BREAK 2**

## S10 — THE DIGITAL VERSION IS THE ONE THAT SELLS (~150 words)

> Worth saying, because most people over-invest in the printed one. The screen
> version is what actually gets seen. It costs nothing to send, it goes in an
> email to a buyer, it sits as a link in your bio, and you can update it the
> moment a price changes. So make that one good. Keep the file under about ten
> megabytes so it does not bounce out of an inbox — if it is too big, that is
> almost always the photos, so re-export at a smaller scale. And put a way to
> get in touch on the last page. Not just a website. An actual email address, a
> phone number, an ordering link. A beautiful lookbook that does not say how to
> buy anything is a portfolio.

## S11 — WHAT TO SEND THE PRINT SHOP (~145 words)

> When you email a printer, send four things and you will get a better job for
> less hassle. The PDF, exported as we just did. The finished size in
> millimetres, written out. Whether the file is RGB or CMYK — and this is where
> you say it if you exported RGB. And how many copies, and what paper weight you
> want. If you have never picked paper, ask for their standard for catalogues and
> let them steer you. Then ask one question: can I see a proof. A proof is a
> single printed copy before the full run. It costs a little and it is the only
> way to see the colour before you have paid for fifty of them. Every print shop
> offers this. Almost nobody asks.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~115 words)

> All of it in one place. A four landscape, two ninety seven by two ten, set
> before you design. Guides five millimetres in, nothing important outside them.
> One grid, repeated across every spread. Backgrounds dragged past the edge so
> there is something to trim. Photos checked at one hundred percent zoom. PDF
> Print with bleed and crop marks for paper — CMYK if you have Pro, and tell the
> printer if you do not. PDF Standard, no bleed, under ten megabytes, for
> screens. Contact details on the last page. And ask for a proof before you print
> fifty.
