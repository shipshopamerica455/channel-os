# How to Make Etsy Listing Photos & Mockups in Canva (The 5:4 Crop Fix)

Target ~8.7 min · 14 sections · audience: Etsy sellers (US/UK), physical and
digital. Slate #8 — near-gap; pairs with #7 (wall art) which sets up the
"five listing images" idea. Two DESIGNED AD BREAKS after S05 and S09.
Product: Etsy listing image kit (£4.99/Club). Voice: Kokoro bm_fable.

**Facts verified 6 Aug 2026:**
- Upload at **2000 px on the shortest side** minimum; 2000×2000 (1:1) or
  2700×2025 (4:3) are the safe sizes. Below 2000 px Etsy's zoom won't engage.
- **Search results crop your FIRST photo to 5:4 — 570 × 456 px.** A square
  hero loses the top and bottom in the grid. This is the trap.
- **10 images + 1 video** per listing, max. Listings with fewer than five
  photos measurably underperform on both click-through and conversion.
- Formats JPG / PNG / GIF. Video 5–15 s.
- ⚠️ Reported file-size caps disagree across sources (1 / 10 / 20 / 50 MB) —
  the script gives the safe practice (JPEG ~90%, comfortably under a
  megabyte) and does NOT assert a specific cap.

## S02 — WHAT THE PHOTOS ARE FOR (~130 words)

> First, the job each photo is doing — because "more photos" isn't the point,
> coverage is. A buyer arrives with four questions and leaves if any go
> unanswered. What is it? What does it look like in real life? How big is it?
> And what exactly do I get? Your ten slots answer those in order, and the
> order matters, because Etsy shows the first few as thumbnails under the main
> image and almost nobody swipes past four. So: hero, context, scale,
> details, then the answers to the questions that would otherwise become
> messages. Photos are not decoration on Etsy — they're the entire sales
> conversation, held in advance.

[VISUAL: four question cards → mapped onto numbered photo slots; the
"nobody swipes past 4" fade.]

## S03 — THE CROP NOBODY MENTIONS (~150 words)

> Now the thing that changes how you design the first photo. You upload a
> square image — two thousand by two thousand, exactly as Etsy recommends.
> But in the search results, where every buying decision starts, Etsy doesn't
> show your square. It crops it to five by four — five hundred and seventy by
> four hundred and fifty-six pixels — taking the middle and throwing away the
> top and bottom. So if you centred your product to fill that square edge to
> edge, the search grid slices the top and bottom off it. Everything that
> matters has to live inside the middle five-by-four band of your square. Get
> that wrong and the best photo in your shop never gets seen, because the
> version buyers actually see is a bad crop of it.

[VISUAL: motion-graphics — square hero, 5:4 band drops over it, the outer
strips grey out and fall away; before/after in a search grid.]

## S04 — CANVAS SETUP + THE SAFE BAND (~140 words)

> So let's build it correctly. Create a design, Custom size, two thousand by
> two thousand pixels. First thing on the canvas, before any design: two
> guides marking the safe band. The five-by-four crop keeps the middle eighty
> percent of the height, so drag a guide at ten percent from the top and ten
> percent from the bottom — that's two hundred pixels each. Everything
> essential — the product, any text, your logo — lives between those lines.
> Outside them is decoration only: background, shadow, a bit of prop. Now your
> square looks generous on the listing page AND survives the crop in search.
> One design, both jobs. Delete the guides before you export.

[VISUAL: custom size 2000×2000 types; two guides drop at 10%/90%; the safe
band highlights; product snaps inside it.]

## S05 — THE HERO SHOT (~175 words)

> The hero — photo one, the only one most people ever see. Three rules. One:
> the product fills most of the frame. New sellers shoot from too far back,
> and at thumbnail size the product becomes a speck in a room. Fill it. Two:
> a plain, light background. Not white necessarily — a warm off-white or a
> soft neutral photographs better and looks less like a stock catalogue — but
> plain, because Etsy's search grid is a wall of competing images and clutter
> loses. Three: no text on the hero, or barely any. Etsy's own guidance
> discourages it, buyers read it as an advert, and remember it gets cropped
> anyway. If you're selling a digital product, the hero is your design shown
> as the object it becomes — a print in a frame, a planner on a desk — not
> the flat file. Build it once inside the safe band and everything else in
> the set can be looser.

[VISUAL: full say-it-show-it — product scales up to fill, background swatch
changes to warm neutral, text layer added then deleted with ✕, thumbnail
squint test.]

## S06 — MOCKUPS WITHOUT A CAMERA (~165 words)

> Photo two is the one that sells a digital product, and you can build it
> entirely in Canva. Elements, search "frame mockup" or "mug mockup" or
> whatever your product becomes, and pick one with an image placeholder —
> those show a little crop icon. Drag your design straight onto the
> placeholder and it drops in, sized and angled to match the scene. That's
> the whole trick. Two rules to keep it honest. Use a mockup that matches
> what the buyer will actually own — don't show a gallery wall of five prints
> if you're selling one file. And check the shadows: if your flat design sits
> on the mockup with no shadow while everything around it has one, it reads
> as fake, and Canva's Shadows effect fixes it in one click. A good mockup
> isn't a lie — it's showing the buyer the outcome instead of the file.

[VISUAL: Elements search → placeholder icons highlighted → drag-drop the
design in → shadow toggle before/after; honest vs overclaiming card.]

## S06b — IF YOU SELL PHYSICAL THINGS (~200 words)

> Everything so far assumes a digital product. If you make something real,
> you still don't need a studio — you need a window. Shoot in daylight,
> next to a window, with the light coming from the side rather than behind
> you, and turn the ceiling light off; mixing daylight with a yellow bulb is
> what makes home photos look muddy. Put a sheet of white card or a folded
> sheet on the opposite side to bounce light back into the shadows — that
> single piece of card is the difference between a snapshot and a product
> photo. Background: plain, and slightly out of frame, so nothing competes.
> Shoot from slightly above for anything flat, straight on for anything
> upright, and take three times more shots than you need. Then bring them
> into Canva and do two things: crop to two thousand by two thousand inside
> the safe band, and use Adjust to lift the brightness a little and drop the
> saturation a touch. Almost every amateur photo is slightly too dark and
> slightly too saturated. Fix those two sliders and your phone photos stop
> looking like phone photos.

[VISUAL: window-light diagram with the bounce card; ceiling-light ✕; the
Adjust panel with brightness/saturation sliders moving; before/after.]

## S07 — THE SCALE SHOT (~135 words)

> Photo three, and this one exists to stop returns and refund messages. Buyers
> are consistently wrong about size — they picture something bigger than it is,
> get it, and feel misled even though your listing said the dimensions. So
> show scale against something universally understood: a hand holding it, a
> coin next to it, a mug on the same desk. For anything that hangs on a wall,
> show it above a piece of furniture, because that's how people judge
> proportion. And add the actual dimensions as text on this photo — this is
> the one image where text belongs, because it's answering a question rather
> than shouting. One scale photo removes the most common reason for a
> disappointed review.

[VISUAL: product next to a hand/mug/sofa; dimension arrows animate on; the
"disappointed review" card struck through.]

## S08 — THE INFORMATION CARDS (~150 words)

> Photos four through seven aren't photos at all — they're cards you design,
> and they do more for conversion than another angle would. Card one: what's
> included, listed plainly. Card two: sizes or variations available. Card
> three: how it arrives — instant download, or shipped in two to three days.
> Card four: care, printing or usage instructions, whichever applies. Keep
> them consistent — same background, same font, same accent colour as your
> hero — so the set reads as one shop rather than four different sellers. And
> use plain sentences, not marketing. Every one of these cards is a message
> you don't have to answer at eleven at night. That's the real return on
> them: fewer questions, faster decisions, and a shop that looks like it has
> been doing this a while.

[VISUAL: four cards build in sequence with the shared style; an inbox with
messages ✕-ing out one by one.]

## S09 — THE LAST SLOTS + THE VIDEO (~145 words)

> Photos eight to ten, and the video. Eight and nine: detail shots — the
> texture, the stitching, the actual pixels of a print — because these are
> what convince the buyer who has already decided but wants permission. Ten:
> your best lifestyle shot, the one where the product is in a real setting
> being used. And the video slot, which is genuinely underused: five to
> fifteen seconds, no sound needed, and it can be as simple as a slow pan
> across the product in Canva. Etsy shows the video in the gallery, and
> listings that use it hold attention longer. Export everything as JPEG at
> around ninety percent quality — high enough to look sharp, small enough to
> load fast. Big files make your listing feel sluggish on a phone, which is
> where most of your buyers are.

## S10 — THE TEMPLATE SYSTEM (~135 words)

> Here's where it stops being work. Every card and mockup you just built is a
> page in one Canva design — so for the next product, you duplicate the whole
> design, swap the artwork and the words, and you have ten listing images in
> about ten minutes. Keep one design per product type and the layout, the
> fonts, the safe band and the shadows are all already solved. And there's a
> second benefit that compounds: because every listing in your shop uses the
> same card design, your shop starts to look like a brand instead of a
> collection. Buyers who land on one listing recognise the next one. That
> consistency is something almost no small shop bothers with, and it's free.

[VISUAL: design duplicates; artwork swaps across all 10 pages at once; shop
grid showing visual consistency.]

## S11 — THE MISTAKES THAT COST CLICKS (~125 words)

> Four quick ones I see constantly. Fewer than five photos — listings with
> under five measurably underperform, and you have ten slots that cost
> nothing. Text sprayed across the hero, which gets cropped and reads as an
> advert. Mockups that overclaim, showing a framed set when the buyer gets one
> file, which is where bad reviews come from. And inconsistent styling across
> a shop, so every listing looks like it came from a different seller. All
> four are free to fix and none of them require a better product — just an
> hour with a template.
