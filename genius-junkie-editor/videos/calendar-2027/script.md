# How to Make a 2027 Calendar in Canva (And Why Last Year's Won't Work)

## Facts verified 2026-08-18

**The arithmetic (computed, not looked up — this is the trap):**

- **1 January 2026 = Thursday. 1 January 2027 = Friday.** A common year is 365
  days = 52 weeks + 1 day, so every date lands one weekday later the following
  year. 2026 is not a leap year, so 2026 → 2027 is a shift of exactly **one**.
- **2027 is not a leap year.** February has 28 days. **2028 is**, so next year's
  shift will be two days, not one.
- **The twelve starting weekdays for 2027:** Jan Fri · Feb Mon · Mar Mon ·
  Apr Thu · May Sat · Jun Tue · Jul Thu · Aug Sun · Sep Wed · Oct Fri ·
  Nov Mon · Dec Wed.
- **THE SELF-CHECK:** February 2027 starts on a Monday and has 28 days —
  **exactly four full weeks**. On a Monday-start grid it fills a perfect four
  by seven block with no blank cells at either end. If your February has a gap,
  your grid is wrong. (On a Sunday-start grid it begins in the second column,
  so state which you are building.)

**From Canva's Help Centre:**

- **The date feature will not help you.** "Adding dates is only available in
  Canva Sheets and Docs." Source: "Add and customize dates in your design". So
  in an actual calendar design the grid is typed by hand, or built from a table,
  or generated by an app.
- **PDF Print** is "for printing with a resolution of 300 dpi, with bleed, crop
  marks, and colour profile (RGB or CMYK) selection option". PDF Standard is
  96 dpi. Source: "Choose the right download file type for your Canva design".
- Canva has a **Calendar Maker** app in the Apps panel. Mentioned as a shortcut
  to look at — its exact behaviour is not documented in the Help Centre, so no
  claim is made here about what it generates.

Target: ~1,950 words @ 3.70 w/s ≈ 8 min 47 s. Ad breaks after S05 and S09.

## S01 — HOOK (~130 words)

> Here is what almost everybody does in August. You open last year's calendar
> file, you change twenty twenty six to twenty twenty seven, you export it, and
> you list it. And every single date on all twelve pages is wrong. Not one
> month. Every month. Because the first of January moves from a Thursday to a
> Friday, and everything behind it shifts with it. If you sell that, somebody
> prints it, sticks it on a wall, and books a dentist appointment on the wrong
> day. There is a one second check that tells you instantly whether your grid
> is right, and it only works this year, which is why I want to show you now.
> But first, let us build the thing properly.

## S02 — WHY EVERY YEAR SHIFTS (~145 words)

> This is the whole thing in one sentence, and once you have got it you will
> never make the mistake again. A normal year is three hundred and sixty five
> days. Fifty two weeks is three hundred and sixty four. So a year is fifty two
> weeks and one day left over, which means every date lands one weekday later
> the next year. New Year's Day twenty twenty six is a Thursday. New Year's Day
> twenty twenty seven is a Friday. One day later, and so is everything else.
> A leap year has three hundred and sixty six days, so it pushes you two days
> instead of one. Twenty twenty seven is not a leap year, so February has
> twenty eight days. But twenty twenty eight is, so next year the shift is two,
> and February gets a twenty ninth. Write that down somewhere.

## S03 — THE TWELVE STARTING DAYS, AND THE ONE SECOND CHECK (~160 words)

> Here are the twelve, and I would screenshot this. January starts Friday.
> February, Monday. March, Monday. April, Thursday. May, Saturday. June,
> Tuesday. July, Thursday. August, Sunday. September, Wednesday. October,
> Friday. November, Monday. December, Wednesday. Now the check, and it is
> lovely, and it only works in twenty twenty seven. February starts on a
> Monday, and it has twenty eight days. Twenty eight days is exactly four
> weeks. So on a calendar where the week starts on Monday, February twenty
> twenty seven fills a perfect four by seven block. Four rows, seven columns,
> completely full, no blank squares at the beginning and none at the end. It is
> the only month of the year that does that. So build February, look at it, and
> if there is a single gap anywhere in that block, your grid is wrong and so are
> the other eleven. If you build Sunday start calendars it begins one column in
> instead, so just know which one you are making.

## S04 — SETTING IT UP (~150 words)

> New design, and the size depends on what it is for. A wall calendar wants A4
> or A3 portrait, with the picture on the top half and the grid on the bottom.
> A desk calendar is usually landscape A5, sitting in a little easel. If you are
> going to sell it as a printable, A4 portrait is the safest thing in the world,
> because it prints on ordinary paper in every country that uses A sizes, and
> for the US you add a US Letter version, which is a slightly different shape,
> not just a resize. Now, one thing to know before you go hunting for it. Canva
> does have a date feature, but their own documentation says it only works in
> Canva Sheets and Canva Docs. It is not going to fill your calendar grid in for
> you. So in an actual calendar design you are building the grid yourself, and
> the fastest way to do that is a table.

## S05 — BUILDING THE GRID (~200 words)

> Elements, search table, and drop one in. You want seven columns, because
> seven days, and six rows. Six, not five. Most months only need five rows, but
> some need six, and if you build a five row table you will be fighting it in
> August. Top row is the day names. Monday through Sunday if you are building a
> Monday start calendar, which is what most of the world outside America uses.
> Now the numbers. January twenty twenty seven starts on a Friday, so in your
> top row you leave Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday empty, and you put
> the one under Friday. Then two under Saturday, three under Sunday, and four
> starts the next row under Monday. Carry on to thirty one. Style the table once
> and properly, because you are about to copy it eleven times. Numbers aligned
> the same way in every cell, top left or centred, pick one. Give the cells real
> height, because a calendar people write on needs somewhere to write. And keep
> the day names visually quieter than the numbers, because the numbers are what
> people are looking for.

**→ AD BREAK 1** (1.4 s scripted silence + hard cut card, after S05)

## S06 — WHY LAST YEAR'S FILE IS A TRAP (~205 words)

> Right. The thing I promised. Here is last year's January next to this year's
> January. Twenty twenty six starts on a Thursday. Twenty twenty seven starts on
> a Friday. So every number in the grid moves one square to the right, and the
> ones that fall off the end of a row wrap round to the start of the next one.
> Which means if you take last year's file and edit the year in the title, you
> have not made a twenty twenty seven calendar. You have made a twenty twenty
> six calendar with the wrong year printed on it, and it looks completely
> normal. That is what makes this dangerous. There is no error message, nothing
> turns red. It just quietly tells you Christmas Day is a Friday, when in
> twenty twenty seven Christmas Day is a Saturday. And people trust a printed calendar in a way they do not
> trust a screen. They will write on it. So either rebuild the grid, or start
> from a template that was actually built for twenty twenty seven and check
> February against the four week rule before you trust it. And if you are
> reusing your own design, reuse the layout, the fonts and the pictures. Never
> reuse the numbers.

## S07 — THE OTHER ELEVEN MONTHS, FAST (~180 words)

> You are not building twelve grids from scratch. Duplicate the page, and then
> for each month you are doing two small edits. Change the month name, and shift
> the numbers to the new starting day. Go in the order I gave you, and use the
> list, because this is exactly the point where people start guessing and get
> October wrong. Two shortcuts that genuinely save time. First, several months
> share a starting day, so once you have built one you can copy its grid
> wholesale. In twenty twenty seven, February, March and November all start on a
> Monday. April and July both start on a Thursday. September and December both
> start on a Wednesday. But sharing a start day only gets you most of the way,
> because the lengths differ, so the last row will be wrong. There is exactly
> one pair that is completely identical, and it is January and October. Same
> starting day, same thirty one days. Build January, and October is a copy and
> paste with the month name changed. That is the only one you get truly free. Second, do all twelve grids
> first, in one pass, before you touch a single photograph. Once you start
> laying out pictures you will not want to go back into the numbers, and the
> numbers are the part that has to be right.

## S08 — MAKING IT LOOK LIKE SOMETHING (~165 words)

> Now the design layer, and a calendar is a strange object because it is going
> to be on a wall for a whole month at a time. So the picture has to be
> something you can look at for thirty days without getting sick of it, which
> in practice means calmer than you think. Big, simple, quiet images beat busy
> ones. Give the month name room. It wants to be the second biggest thing on the
> page after the picture, and it wants space around it, not tucked into a
> corner. Keep the same layout on all twelve pages. The picture in the same
> place, the month in the same place, the grid in the same place, so that when
> somebody flips the page it feels like the same object. And leave the bottom
> right cell alone. Do not put a logo or a flourish in the last week of the
> month, because that is exactly where people write things.

## S09 — DATES YOU MARK, AND ONES YOU SHOULD NOT (~160 words)

> If you are going to mark holidays, be careful, because this is where a
> calendar stops being universal. Bank holidays are different in England,
> Scotland and Northern Ireland. American holidays are different again, and half
> of them move. If you print UK bank holidays on a calendar and sell it to
> somebody in Texas, you have made their calendar slightly wrong and slightly
> confusing. So you have got two honest options. Either mark nothing, and let
> people write their own, which is what most of the best selling printable
> calendars do. Or make a specific version and say so in the title, so a UK
> twenty twenty seven wall calendar with the bank holidays on it. Naming it is
> the difference between a feature and a complaint. And if you are marking
> anything, double check the dates against an official source, because moving
> holidays are precisely the thing that is wrong in last year's file.

**→ AD BREAK 2** (1.4 s scripted silence + hard cut card, after S09)

## S10 — PRINTING IT PROPERLY (~155 words)

> This is a print object, so the export matters more than usual. Share,
> download, and pick P D F Print. Canva's own documentation says P D F Print is
> three hundred D P I with bleed, crop marks and a colour profile option, and
> P D F Standard is ninety six D P I. Ninety six is for screens. If you send
> ninety six to a printer, the numbers come out soft and slightly fuzzy and it
> looks cheap. Tick bleed if anything on your page runs to the edge, which for
> a calendar picture it almost always does, and tick crop marks if a print shop
> is cutting it. If you are selling it as a printable that people print at home,
> do not use bleed, because a home printer cannot print to the edge and they
> will end up with a white border and a cropped picture. Two different files for
> two different buyers.

## S11 — SELLING IT (~150 words)

> If this is going on Etsy, three things move the needle. One, sell the set, not
> the month. Twelve pages, one download, one price. Two, put the year in the
> title and in the first line of the description, because that is what people
> search in August and September and it is what stops somebody buying it in
> March expecting a different year. Three, include both A4 and US Letter, and
> say so, because half the complaints on printable calendars are somebody
> printing an A4 file on Letter paper and losing an edge. And the timing matters
> more than anything else here. Calendars sell from August to December, and the
> big weeks are late November and early December when people are buying gifts.
> If it is not listed by the end of October, you have missed most of it.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~110 words)

> Before you export. January starts on a Friday. February is a perfect four by
> seven block with no gaps, and if it is not, stop and fix the grid. Six rows in
> the table, not five. Same layout on every one of the twelve pages. Nothing in
> the bottom right cell where people write. Holidays either absent or named in
> the title. P D F Print, not Standard, at three hundred D P I. Bleed on for a
> print shop, off for a home printable. Year in the file name. And the one that
> actually matters, if you started from last year's file, you rebuilt the
> numbers rather than editing them.
