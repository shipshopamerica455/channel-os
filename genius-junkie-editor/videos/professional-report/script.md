## S02 — THE SKIM RULE (~125 words)

> Before we design anything, the uncomfortable truth: nobody reads your
> report top to bottom. Readers skim in a pattern — the cover, then the
> executive summary, then headlines and charts, and only then, maybe, the
> paragraphs. So we design for the skim. Three principles carry the whole
> document. Hierarchy: one clear reading order on every page — big
> heading, subheading, body. Restraint: one font family, one accent
> colour; a report with five colours reads as a flyer, and flyers don't
> win contracts. And white space: margins are not wasted paper — they're
> what makes the important things findable. Design for the ninety-second
> skim, and the people who matter will stay for the detail.

[VISUAL: skim heat-path animates over a report spread; three principle
chips; five-colour ✕ vs one-accent ✓ flash.]

## S03 — CANVAS + THE FIVE-PAGE SKELETON (~140 words)

> Into Canva: Create a design, search "Report" — A4, portrait — or start
> from our template, link below. One design, five page types, and that's
> the whole skeleton: a cover; a table of contents; an executive summary
> — one page, always; your content pages, as many as you need; and a
> back page with contact details and next steps. Open the Pages panel on
> the left so you can see the whole document as thumbnails — reports are
> built page-level, not element-level. Add the five pages now, empty, and
> label each with a small note you'll delete later. Structure first,
> decoration second — that order is what separates documents that look
> professional from documents that look decorated.

## S04 — THE COVER + YOUR BRAND (~145 words)

> The cover is one decision: what does the client see in the first two
> seconds? Keep it to four elements. The report title, big — forty
> points or more. A subtitle with the period or scope — "Quarterly
> Review, January to March". Your logo. And one strong visual: a single
> image, or just a bold block of your brand colour — honestly, the
> colour block looks more expensive. Pull your palette from your own
> brand: upload your logo, and use Canva's colour picker to lift your
> exact brand colour from it, then use that one colour for every accent
> in the document. If you're on Canva Pro, save it as a Brand Kit so
> every future report starts on-brand. Date, author, client name — small,
> bottom corner. Done. Confident covers are quiet.

[VISUAL: cover assembles: title stack, logo drop, colour-block; eyedropper
lifts brand colour from logo; Brand Kit chip; date/author line.]

## S05 — BODY PAGES, BUILT LIVE (~165 words)

> Now a content page, built properly once — then duplicated forever.
> Start with the running header: a text box with the report title, small,
> grey, top of the page — and the page number bottom corner; Canva won't
> auto-number design pages, so we make the footer once and it copies with
> every duplicate. Body text: eleven or twelve points, dark grey rather
> than pure black, lines no wider than about seventy characters — full-
> width text is why documents feel exhausting. Headings in your accent
> colour, twenty-plus points, with real space above them. And give every
> page one focal moment: a pull-quote, a stat in large type, or an image
> — one, not three. Now the system: group nothing, just duplicate the
> whole page for each new section and replace the content. The layout
> stays identical, and identical is what professional looks like.

## S06 — CHARTS THAT LOOK LIKE EVIDENCE (~145 words)

> Numbers persuade when they're visible. Elements, search "chart" — bar,
> line, pie, donut — pick one and Canva opens a small table: paste your
> data straight from your spreadsheet, and the chart redraws. Now the
> rules that make charts look professional. One chart, one point — if a
> chart needs a paragraph to explain it, split it. Label the bars
> directly instead of sending readers to a legend. Use your accent
> colour for the number that matters and grey for everything else —
> that's how you point without pointing. Never use three-D charts;
> nothing says amateur faster. And headline the chart with the finding,
> not the topic: "Revenue up eighteen percent", not "Revenue Overview".
> The chart is the evidence; the headline is the verdict.

[VISUAL: chart search → data table paste → bars redraw; direct labels vs
legend flash; accent-vs-grey recolour beat; 3D ✕ card; headline morph.]

## S07 — THE TABLE OF CONTENTS (~130 words)

> Back to page two — the table of contents. Build it as a simple
> two-column list: section names on the left, page numbers tucked at the
> end of each line. Match the cover's fonts, add your accent rule under
> the heading, and resist decorating it — a clean TOC signals a
> organised mind. Two power moves. First, in a digital report, select
> each line, click the link icon, and link it to its page — Canva keeps
> those links live in the PDF, so clients click straight to the section.
> Second, write TOC entries as findings where you can — "Where the
> budget went", "What we recommend" — because the TOC is the second most
> read page in the document.

[VISUAL: TOC builds line by line; link icon → page link; exported PDF
click-through beat.]

## S07b — TABLES, ICONS & IMAGE DISCIPLINE (~250 words)

> Two more evidence tools, and the discipline that keeps them
> professional. First, tables — for the detail that backs up your
> charts. Elements, search "table", and keep the styling almost
> invisible: no zebra stripes in five colours, no heavy grids. Light
> lines, generous row height, numbers right-aligned so the digits line
> up, and bold only the totals row. If a table runs past ten rows, it
> belongs in an appendix at the back, not in the middle of your
> argument. Second, icons — Canva has millions, and that's exactly the
> danger. Used well, one small icon anchors each item of a list and
> helps the skimmer navigate. The rules: same style family for every
> icon — all outline, or all filled, never mixed; one colour — your
> accent or your grey; and the same size, every time. Search "outline"
> with your keyword and Canva filters the style for you. And images: in
> a business report, every image needs a job — evidence, or orientation.
> A photo of your actual project, your actual team, your actual product
> — that's evidence. A map, a diagram, a screenshot — orientation.
> Generic stock photos of handshakes and lightbulbs are neither; they're
> padding, and clients can smell padding a page away. If you don't have
> a real photo, use a colour block, a big stat, or nothing — confident
> white space beats a fake handshake every time. Tables for precision,
> icons for navigation, images for proof — each one earning its place.

[VISUAL: table restyle beat (heavy → light); icon row unifies style/

## S08 — THE CONSISTENCY PASS (~120 words)

> Before export, the pass that separates designed from decorated. Zoom
> out to see all pages as a grid. Check the margins: same on every page
> — content starting at the same height, headers in the same place.
> Check the fonts: one family, two weights; if a stray third font crept
> in from a template element, fix it now. Check the accent colour: is it
> doing one job — headings and highlights — or has it leaked everywhere?
> And check alignment: Canva's pink guide lines appear when things line
> up; if you're seeing them constantly, you're doing it right. Ten
> minutes of boring checking is what clients experience as quality.

[VISUAL: grid view; margin guides overlay across pages; font audit chips;
pink snap-guides moment.]

## S09 — EXPORT: THE RIGHT PDF (~135 words)

> Export, and reports have a fork. Share, Download, and here's the
> decision: PDF Standard for anything sent by email or read on screen —
> smaller file, and it keeps your TOC links clickable. PDF Print only if
> it's going to an actual printer — bigger file, print colour profile,
> and add crop marks only if a print shop asks. For a client report,
> Standard is almost always right — a report that arrives as a
> forty-megabyte attachment starts the meeting badly. One more habit:
> name the file like a professional — client, report name, date — because
> "final-final-v3.pdf" undoes every design decision we just made. Attach,
> send, and your work arrives looking like it came from a bigger firm
> than it did.

## S10 — THE TEN-MINUTE MONTHLY SYSTEM (~115 words)

> Here's the trick from the intro. Next month, don't start again — open
> this design, click the three dots, Make a copy. Swap the cover date,
> paste the new numbers into your charts — they redraw themselves — and
> update the exec summary. Same skeleton, same brand, ten minutes. Do
> this every month and something compounds: your reports become
> recognisable. Clients open them knowing where everything is, and that
> familiarity reads as reliability. Agencies charge retainers for
> exactly this consistency. Yours now costs a duplicate.

[VISUAL: Make a copy; date swap; chart data re-paste redraw; months fan

## S11 — THE CHECKLIST (~85 words)

> Before it goes to the client, six checks. One: skim test — cover,
> summary, headlines carry the story alone. Two: one font family, one
> accent colour. Three: every chart headlines its finding. Four: TOC
> page numbers correct after your final edits. Five: margins and headers
> identical across pages. Six: PDF Standard, professionally named. Six
> ticks, and hit send with your shoulders back.
