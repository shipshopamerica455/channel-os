## S02 — MENU PSYCHOLOGY, 30 SECONDS (~120 words)

> Before we design anything, three things menu engineers know. One: eyes
> don't read menus like books — they scan, and they land first in the
> middle, then the top corners. Your most profitable dish belongs in those
> hot zones, not buried in a list. Two: long menus overwhelm — around seven
> items per section is the sweet spot; more than that and people default to
> the cheapest safe option. Three: the way you write prices changes what
> people spend — and that's the trick I'll show you at the end. Design
> with those three in mind and the same kitchen makes more money.

lock on trick #3.]

## S03 — CANVAS SETUP (~110 words)

> Now the canvas. Menus are usually A4 or US letter — use what your local
> print shop prints. In Canva: Create a design, Custom size, and here's the
> part people skip: if a professional printer is producing these, add bleed
> — go to File, Settings, and turn on "Show print bleed". That thin border
> means your background colour runs right to the cut edge instead of
> leaving white slivers. Set your margins too: nothing important within
> half an inch of the edge. Laminated table menus survive about a year of
> service — design once, print often.

[VISUAL: custom size dialog types; bleed border snaps on; margin guides;
white-sliver vs full-bleed comparison.]

## S04 — THE MENU SKELETON (~150 words)

> Every good menu is the same skeleton. At the top: your restaurant name
> and one line of identity — "Wood-fired pizza since 2011" — that's it, no
> paragraphs. Then sections in the order people eat: starters, mains,
> desserts, drinks. Each dish is a three-part line: the name in bold, the
> price, and one line of description underneath — and write appetite, not
> inventory: "slow-braised beef, red wine, rosemary" sells; "beef with
> sauce" doesn't. Keep one font family for everything except your
> restaurant name, and keep body text at eleven points or more —
> candlelight and reading glasses are real. That skeleton, repeated
> cleanly, is ninety percent of a professional menu.

[VISUAL: skeleton assembles with zone labels; good vs bad description
cards; font-size chip.]

## S04b — COLOURS & FONTS BY CUISINE (~200 words)

> Before we fill the menu, give it a personality — and steal it from your
> own food. The fastest way: upload a photo of your best dish or your
> dining room, then use Canva's colour picker to pull your accent colour
> straight out of it — the paprika red, the espresso brown, the basil
> green. One background colour, one text colour, one accent. That's the
> whole palette; menus with five colours read as takeaway flyers. The
> pairing rules by cuisine are almost a cheat sheet: dark background with
> gold or cream type reads upscale steakhouse or cocktail bar. Warm kraft
> tones with simple black type reads artisan café and brunch. Clean white
> with one bright accent reads modern, family-friendly, fast-casual. And
> for fonts: a serif for your restaurant name says tradition; a clean
> sans-serif says contemporary — pick whichever matches the room, then
> never mix more than the two. If choosing from thousands of fonts
> paralyses you, type your restaurant name, browse Canva's font
> combinations in the Styles tab, and click one — they're pre-paired by
> designers. Personality settled in two minutes — now let's fill it.

[VISUAL: colour-picker pulls swatches from a dish photo (Flow cutaway);

## S05 — BUILD A SECTION LIVE (~170 words)

> Let's build the mains section for real. Add a text box for the section
> header — MAINS, letter-spaced, small and confident, not shouting. Below
> it, your first dish: name on the left in bold, price on the same line —
> and skip the row of dots between them; dotted leaders are nineteen-fifty
> diner style, and they drag the eye to the price instead of the food.
> Description on the next line, smaller, grey — two contrast levels doing
> the work. Now the speed trick: select all three elements, group them,
> and duplicate the group for every dish — spacing stays identical, which
> is exactly what makes a menu look professionally set. Six dishes,
> forty-five seconds each once the first one's right. Nudge your most
> profitable dish to the top of the section — first and last items get
> read most.

## S06 — PHOTOS: THE HONEST RULE (~110 words)

> Photos. The honest rule: great photos sell food, average photos kill it.
> If you have professional shots of your actual dishes, use two or three
> per page in clean frames — Canva's Elements, search "frame", drop your
> photo in. If all you have is phone snaps under kitchen lighting, use
> none — text-only menus read as more upscale anyway; it's why fine-dining
> menus are pure typography. And never use stock photos of food you don't
> serve — customers notice, and review sites remember. Two or three real
> photos, or zero. Nothing in between.

[VISUAL: frames demo with a real-photo cutaway; upscale text-only menu

## S07 — THE PRICING PSYCHOLOGY PASS (~140 words)

> Now the trick I promised. Go through your prices and delete every
> currency symbol. Twelve, not twelve dollars, not a pound sign in sight.
> Cornell University researchers found diners spend significantly more
> when menus drop the symbols — the sign triggers the pain of paying; the
> bare number reads as just information. While you're there: no trailing
> zeros — twelve, not twelve point zero zero — and stop right-aligning
> prices into a neat column, because a column invites comparison shopping.
> Tuck each price at the end of its dish line instead. Three small
> deletions, and the same menu quietly sells more. That's the cheapest
> consultant advice you'll ever get.

[VISUAL: currency symbols vanish one by one with pops; £12.00 → 12 morph;
column vs tucked comparison.]

## S08 — THE QR CODE (~130 words)

> The QR code — for allergen info, your full drinks list, or online
> ordering. Canva has it built in: on the left rail, click Apps, search
> "QR code", and paste the link you want it to open. It drops onto your
> page as an editable element — size it about an inch, bottom corner,
> with a three-word label: "Scan for allergens", "Order online". Two
> rules: test it with your own phone camera before you print — a broken
> QR code on two hundred laminated menus is an expensive lesson — and
> keep it black on a light background; decorative coloured codes fail in
> dim restaurant lighting.

[VISUAL: Apps rail → QR app → link pastes → code drops in; phone-scan
cutaway; black vs decorative code comparison.]

## S09 — EXPORT FOR THE PRINTERS (~130 words)

> Export time, and menus have their own rules. Share, Download, PDF Print
> — and if a print shop is producing these, tick "Crop marks and bleed";
> that's the professional handoff format, and it's the difference between
> "we can work with this" and an email asking for a proper file. Printing
> at home or laminating yourself? Untick the crop marks and just use
> flatten. Ask your printer for three-hundred-GSM stock or lamination for
> table menus — standard paper dies in a week of service. One file, two
> export recipes: crop marks for the pros, flatten for the office printer.

> Before you send it to print, six checks. One: bleed on, if a printer's
> involved. Two: nothing inside the half-inch edge. Three: seven-ish items
