# How to Make Wedding Invitations in Canva (Print-Ready A7 + On-the-Day Set)

## Facts verified 2026-09-28

- **THE TRAP — "A7" is two completely different sizes.** In the United States an
  **A7 envelope is 5.25 × 7.25 inches**, made to hold a 5 × 7 inch card — that is
  the standard wedding invitation envelope, and it is what every US stationery
  supplier means by A7. Under **ISO 216, A7 paper is 74 × 105 mm** — about
  2.9 × 4.1 inches, smaller than a postcard. Same two characters, two sizes,
  roughly three times apart in area.
  Sources: US announcement-envelope sizing (JAM Paper, LCI Paper, Envelopes.com
  all list A7 as 5¼ × 7¼ in); ISO 216, where each size is the previous one
  halved across the long side: A4 210×297 → A5 148×210 → A6 105×148 →
  **A7 74×105 mm**.
- **The ISO series is definitional, not a lookup.** Every A size is the one above
  halved, so the arithmetic in this video cannot drift: A4 is 210 × 297 mm.
- **Square and rigid envelopes are non-machinable.** Square envelopes, wax seals,
  ribbon and anything rigid cannot go through USPS automated sorting and carry a
  surcharge on top of the letter rate. **No figures are quoted in this script** —
  rates change and I have not verified a current number against USPS's own rate
  page. The video tells the viewer to take one complete, sealed invitation to the
  counter and have it weighed and measured before buying a hundred stamps.
  The mechanism is the fact; the price is not.
- **Canva's own preset is "5 × 7 in"**, not "A7" — which is exactly why the
  collision bites: you design 5 × 7, you say "A7" because that is what the
  envelope is called, and a UK supplier sends you 74 × 105 mm envelopes.

NOT CLAIMED: any postage price, any Etsy or Canva fee, anything about print
costs. Any figure in those categories would need checking at source first.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> A seven is two different sizes, and wedding stationery is the one place that
> will cost you real money. In America, an A seven envelope is five and a
> quarter by seven and a quarter inches, and it is made to hold a five by seven
> card. That is the standard wedding invitation envelope. But under the
> international paper standard, A seven is seventy four by one hundred and five
> millimetres — smaller than a postcard. Same two characters. Three times the
> difference in area. Order a hundred envelopes using the wrong one and you have
> a hundred envelopes you cannot use. There is a free size card in the
> description with every measurement laid out. Now let me show you how to build
> the whole suite so this never happens.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~110 words)

> The free wedding stationery size card is linked below — every card, every
> envelope, in both inches and millimetres, on one page.
>
> Three principles first. One: design to the CARD size and buy the envelope to
> match, never the other way round. Two: the envelope is always bigger than the
> card — if the numbers are the same, something is wrong. Three: nothing gets
> ordered in quantity until one real, finished piece has been printed, put in
> the envelope, and weighed. Every expensive mistake in stationery is a quantity
> bought before a sample was checked.

## S03 — SETTING UP THE INVITATION (~145 words)

> Open Canva, Custom size, and switch the unit to inches before you type
> anything, because Canva opens in pixels and five is a speck.
>
> Five by seven inches. That is the card. Notice Canva's own preset is called
> five by seven — it does not say A seven anywhere, and that is the first clue
> that A seven is an envelope word, not a card word.
>
> If your artwork runs off the edge, add bleed: an eighth of an inch on every
> side, so five point two five by seven point two five, and then you trim back
> to five by seven after printing. Yes, that number looks like the envelope
> size. It is a coincidence, and it is a cruel one. The bled artwork is not the
> envelope. Write both numbers down.
>
> Now guides. An eighth of an inch is bleed, and keep all your type at least a
> quarter inch inside the trim.

## S04 — WHAT THE INVITATION HAS TO SAY (~140 words)

> Before any decoration, the invitation has a job, and there are five things it
> has to answer. Who is getting married. Who is doing the inviting — which is
> not always the same people, and that line matters to families. The date, the
> time, and the place. And what happens next.
>
> Set those five in a clear hierarchy. The two names are the largest thing on
> the card. The date and the venue come second. Everything else is small.
>
> The most common mistake I see is the date typed as digits — ten oh nine
> twenty twenty seven. To an American reader that is the ninth of October. To a
> British reader it is the tenth of September. Write the month as a word. It
> costs you nothing and it removes the ambiguity completely.

## S05 — THE DETAILS CARD AND THE RSVP (~150 words)

> A modern invitation suite is usually three pieces. The invitation. A details
> card — travel, parking, accommodation, dress code, the website. And the
> R S V P.
>
> Put the details card at five by seven as well, so it stacks. The R S V P is
> traditionally smaller — three and a half by five inches, which takes an A one
> envelope, or a postcard at four by six if you want to skip the envelope
> entirely.
>
> Here is the one people forget. If the R S V P is a card that comes back to
> you, it needs your address on it, and it needs a stamp on it. A reply card
> with no stamp is a reply card that does not come back. Design the address
> block now, while you are here, and leave the top right corner clear for the
> stamp.

## S06 — THE TRAP: A SEVEN IS TWO SIZES (~180 words)

> Now the thing that costs money.
>
> You have designed a five by seven card. You go to buy envelopes. Every
> American stationery supplier sells an A seven envelope, five and a quarter by
> seven and a quarter inches, and it fits your card with a comfortable
> sixteenth of an inch all round. Perfect.
>
> But the international paper standard also has an A seven, and it is a
> different thing entirely. That standard is built by halving: A four is two
> hundred and ten by two hundred and ninety seven millimetres. Halve it, A five.
> Halve it, A six — one hundred and five by one hundred and forty eight. Halve
> it again and you get A seven: seventy four by one hundred and five
> millimetres. That is two point nine by four point one inches. It is smaller
> than the reply card.
>
> So if you are in Britain and you order A seven envelopes for a five by seven
> invitation, a hundred envelopes arrive that your invitation cannot physically
> enter. Nobody has made a mistake. The word just means two things.

## S07 — THE FIX: MEASURE, DO NOT TRUST THE NAME (~145 words)

> The fix is one habit. Never order an envelope by its letter and number. Order
> it by its measurement.
>
> When you buy, the listing will give you both — A seven, five and a quarter by
> seven and a quarter inches. Read the inches. If the listing does not give a
> measurement in inches or millimetres, do not buy from that listing.
>
> And the sanity check that catches everything: the envelope must be bigger than
> the card in both directions, by about an eighth to a quarter of an inch. If
> the envelope number and the card number look the same, stop. If the envelope
> is smaller than the card, obviously stop.
>
> This is also why the size card is free and why it lists both systems side by
> side. Print it and put it on the wall next to wherever you order from.

## S08 — POSTAGE, AND WHY SQUARE COSTS MORE (~135 words)

> Postage. Square invitations look wonderful and they cost more to send, because
> automated sorting machines cannot handle a square envelope. The same applies
> to anything rigid — a wax seal, a ribbon, a bellyband, an acrylic card. All of
> those make the envelope non machinable, and non machinable carries a
> surcharge.
>
> I am deliberately not giving you a price, because rates change and the only
> number that matters is the one at your counter on the day.
>
> So do this instead. Make one complete invitation. Put everything in it — the
> invitation, the details card, the reply card, the envelope. Seal it. Take that
> one sealed envelope to the post office and have it weighed and measured. Then
> buy stamps. Never the other way round.

## S09 — THE ON-THE-DAY SET (~150 words)

> The suite that goes in the post is half the job. The on the day set is the
> other half, and it is where most of the design value sits, because it is what
> people photograph.
>
> Four pieces cover almost every wedding. Order of service, usually A five —
> one hundred and forty eight by two hundred and ten millimetres — folded from
> an A four sheet. Place cards, which are small and get folded in half, so think
> of them as a tent. Table numbers, big enough to read across a room, so A five
> standing in a frame. And a seating plan, which is a single large sheet, A two
> or A one, depending on the guest count.
>
> Build all four from the invitation. Same typeface, same spacing, same two or
> three colours. That is what makes it look like a suite instead of four
> separate things.

## S10 — ONE DESIGN, MANY NAMES (~140 words)

> Place cards are the one piece where you are making the same design sixty or a
> hundred times with only the name changing. Do not do that by hand.
>
> Canva has Bulk Create. Make one place card, connect a name field, paste your
> guest list in, and it generates one card per guest.
>
> The setting that catches people is under Advanced options, and Advanced
> options is a link sitting underneath the Continue button, so it is easy to
> miss entirely. In there, set Output to individual designs and name each page
> using your name column. Miss it and Canva hands you a single sixty page file
> with every page called page one, and you spend an evening renaming.
>
> I have a full video on Bulk Create if you want the detail — it is linked in
> the description.

## S11 — PRINTING THE SUITE (~145 words)

> Printing. Two routes, and the choice is about volume.
>
> At home, on a good card stock, for anything under about forty invitations.
> Remember two things from my printing video: your printer cannot print to the
> edge, and every print dialog defaults to some version of Fit, which shrinks
> your design by a few percent — which is exactly enough that a five by seven
> card stops fitting a five and a quarter by seven and a quarter envelope. Set
> it to Actual size, or one hundred percent, every single time.
>
> Or send it to a print shop, in which case export P D F Print, include the
> bleed, and ask them for crop marks. They will trim it properly and it will
> look better than anything a home printer does.
>
> Either way: print one first. Just one.

## S12 — SELLING THE SUITE AS A TEMPLATE (~130 words)

> If you are making these to sell rather than for your own wedding, one change
> matters more than everything else.
>
> What buyers want is an editable Canva template — a link that gives them their
> own copy to edit, not a flat P D F and not access to your original. When you
> share, choose the template link, not the edit link. An edit link lets a buyer
> change the file you are still selling.
>
> And give them every size in the suite in one purchase. The invitation, the
> details card, the reply card, and the on the day pieces. A buyer who has to
> buy the place cards separately from the invitation usually does not come back
> for the second one.

## S13 — THE NUMBERS (~110 words)

> So, the numbers that matter.
>
> Five by seven inches is the invitation. Five and a quarter by seven and a
> quarter inches is the American A seven envelope that holds it. Seventy four by
> one hundred and five millimetres is the international A seven, which holds
> nothing you have just made. Three and a half by five is the reply card. A
> five, one forty eight by two ten, is the order of service and the table
> number.
>
> Order the envelope by its measurement, never by its name. Print one before you
> print a hundred. And weigh a finished one before you buy a single stamp.
