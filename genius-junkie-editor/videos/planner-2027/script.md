# How to Make a 2027 Printable Planner in Canva to Sell on Etsy

## Facts verified 2026-10-02 — all from Canva's own Help Centre

- **THE TRAP — Template link vs edit link.** Canva, word for word:
  *"By sharing your design as a **Template link**, anyone can view and use it as
  a template."* The edit link is a different thing entirely — Canva says it
  *"lets people make changes without signing up or logging in to Canva"*, and
  those people appear as guests **in your design**. Paste that into an Etsy
  order and every buyer is editing the one master file.
  Source: Help Centre, "Share your design as a template link" and "Sharing
  designs via email or links".
- **Template links are PRO-ONLY**, verbatim: *"Creating and sharing a Template
  link is only available to Canva Pro, Canva Teams, Canva Enterprise, Canva for
  Education, and Canva for Nonprofits users."* A free-plan seller cannot deliver
  an editable template properly. Same source.
- **Making one:** *"Open the design… Select Share… Select the Template link. If
  you don't see it, select See all first. Select Create template link."*
- **Deleting one kills the URL:** *"If you delete a Template link it'll also
  make the URL inactive. If you make a new Template link it'll create a new
  URL."* So every buyer who ever bought is holding a link you can break.
- **PDF Standard is 96 DPI; PDF Print is 300 DPI** — Canva Help Centre,
  "Download designs as flattened PDF". Verified for the pitch-deck video and
  re-checked here.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> There are two ways to send a Canva design to a buyer, they look almost
> identical, and one of them hands over your master file. Canva's words: a
> template link lets anyone view and use your design as a template — they get
> their own copy. The edit link is the other one. Canva says it lets people make
> changes without even signing up, and they turn up as guests inside your
> design. Not a copy. Yours. So buyer number three opens the planner and finds
> buyer number two's dentist appointments already in it. There is a free
> delivery checklist in the description. And now I will build the planner, and
> set it up so that cannot happen.

## S02 — THE FREE CHECKLIST, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~110 words)

> The free printable planner delivery checklist is linked below — one page,
> every setting, checked before you list.
>
> Three principles. One: build in two page sizes from the start, because your
> buyers are in two countries and they do not print on the same paper. Two: the
> dates have to be right, and nobody checks a planner's dates until a customer
> does. Three: how you deliver it is part of the product — a beautiful planner
> delivered on the wrong link is a refund and a bad review.

## S03 — US LETTER AND A4, BOTH (~145 words)

> Page size first, and you need two.
>
> American buyers print on US Letter: eight and a half by eleven inches.
> Everywhere else prints on A4: two hundred and ten by two hundred and ninety
> seven millimetres. A4 is narrower and taller. They are not interchangeable,
> and a planner built only on Letter prints with a white stripe down one side
> and the bottom cut off for half your customers.
>
> So. Custom size, eight and a half by eleven inches, and set the unit to inches
> before you type, because Canva defaults to pixels.
>
> Build the whole thing at Letter. We will make the A4 version at the end with
> Resize, and I will show you the bit of that you have to check by hand — because
> Resize stretches the page, and it does not ask whether your grid still lines up.

## S04 — THE MARGIN THAT IS NOT A STYLE CHOICE (~140 words)

> Now a margin, and this one is physical.
>
> Home printers cannot print to the edge of the paper. Every one of them has a
> strip at the edge it physically cannot reach — usually somewhere between three
> and six millimetres. Your buyer is printing this at home. So no background
> colour running off the page, no box touching the edge, nothing important in
> that border.
>
> I am setting guides at half an inch all round, and everything lives inside
> them.
>
> And there is a second reason. People put planners in binders. Half an inch on
> the left is the difference between a planner you can punch holes in and a
> planner where the hole punch goes through Tuesday.
>
> A standard hole punch bites about a centimetre in from the edge. Half an inch
> is a little over a centimetre, so half an inch clears it. A quarter of an inch
> does not. That is the whole reason for the number.

## S05 — BUILDING THE WEEK SPREAD (~165 words)

> Here is the week spread, and I am building it with a table, not with
> rectangles.
>
> Elements, search table, and I want eight rows. Seven days and a header. Canva's
> tables resize as one object, which is the whole point — when you change your
> mind about the height of a row, you change it once, and not fourteen times.
>
> Header row gets the day names. I am typing Monday, and then tabbing through.
>
> Now the column on the right, which is where most planners go wrong. A week
> spread that is only seven boxes is a diary. What makes it a planner is the
> stuff beside it — priorities, notes, water, meals, whatever your niche
> actually needs. I am adding one narrow column, and splitting it into three
> labelled blocks.
>
> Select the whole table, and the border colour. One click, and I will come back
> to this.

## S06 — THE TRAP: TWO LINKS THAT LOOK THE SAME (~180 words)

> Now the thing that costs people their listing.
>
> Share, top right. And look at what is in here. There is a link under Share,
> and there is a Template link, and they are not the same thing at all.
>
> Canva's own words for the template link: by sharing your design as a template
> link, anyone can view and use it as a template. Their own copy. They cannot
> touch yours.
>
> The other one — the edit link — Canva describes like this: it lets people make
> changes without signing up or logging in to Canva. They appear as guests. In
> your design. The one you are selling. There is only one of it.
>
> So picture it. Twelve people buy your planner. You sent the edit link. They are
> all in the same file. One of them deletes a page because they did not want it.
> Everyone else's planner just lost a page. Including the master you would need
> to fix it.
>
> Nothing warns you. Both links copy to your clipboard exactly the same way.

## S07 — THE FIX, AND THE PART THAT IS PRO-ONLY (~150 words)

> The fix is three clicks, and one of them is hidden.
>
> Share. Then — and this is the one people miss — if you do not see Template
> link, click See all first. It is behind that. Then Create template link, then
> Copy.
>
> That is the link that goes to your buyer. It ends in slash template, and if
> the link you are about to send does not say template in it, do not send it.
>
> Now the part nobody mentions. Canva says creating and sharing a template link
> is only available to Pro, Teams, Enterprise, Education and Nonprofits. On the
> free plan the option is not there. So if you intend to sell editable
> templates, that is the floor — not a nice-to-have, the actual requirement.
>
> And one more thing: Canva says if you delete a template link, the URL goes
> inactive. Every buyer you have ever had is holding that link. Do not tidy it up.

## S08 — THE DATES, AND WHY THEY ARE DANGEROUS (~150 words)

> Dates. This is a 2027 planner, so every date on it has to be a 2027 date, and
> this is the single most common reason a planner gets returned.
>
> Do not type them from memory and do not copy last year's file and change the
> year, because the weekdays move. The first of January 2027 is a Friday. In
> 2026 it was a Thursday. Every single date shifts.
>
> Open a calendar, actually look at it, and build one month correctly. Then check
> three things in the finished file: that the first of the month lands on the
> right weekday, that the last day is right — because thirty, thirty one and
> twenty eight are not interchangeable — and that February has twenty eight days,
> because 2027 is not a leap year.
>
> Those three checks take a minute and they prevent the one mistake buyers
> always spot.

## S09 — THE MONTH SPREAD, FAST (~145 words)

> The month spread is the same table trick with different numbers. Seven columns,
> six rows, plus a header.
>
> Six rows, not five — a thirty-one day month that starts on a Saturday needs six
> rows, and if you build five you will rebuild it in March.
>
> Day numbers go in the top left of each cell, small, and in a lighter grey than
> the things the buyer writes. The numbers are furniture. Whatever they add is
> the content.
>
> And leave the cells empty and generous. The instinct is to fill the page
> because it looks better in a listing photo. Resist it. A planner is sold on how
> it looks and used on how much room it has, and the second one is what gets you
> the five star review.
>
> One more thing on the month grid. Put the weekday names in, and put them in
> every month, not just January. People flip to a single month and print that
> one page, and a page of numbers with no weekday header is unreadable on its
> own.

## S10 — EXPORTING, AND THE RESOLUTION TRAP (~145 words)

> Download. File type. And this is where the file quietly gets worse.
>
> PDF Standard is the default and it is ninety six D P I. For something a buyer
> prints at home, that is visibly soft — grey type, fuzzy rules.
>
> Choose PDF Print. Three hundred D P I.
>
> Underneath, tick Flatten PDF. That locks your layout and embeds the fonts, so
> it looks the same on their machine as it does on yours.
>
> But — and this matters if you are also selling a digital version — flattening
> kills clickable links. If your planner has tabs that jump between months, you
> need a second export with flatten switched off. One flattened file for
> printing, one unflattened for tablets. Two files, two jobs, and the listing
> should say which is which.

## S11 — WHAT THE BUYER ACTUALLY RECEIVES (~145 words)

> So here is what goes in the delivery, and this is the bit that earns the
> reviews.
>
> One: the printable PDF at Letter. Two: the printable PDF at A4. Three: the
> template link — the real one, with template in the URL. Four: one page of
> instructions, because most of your support messages will be people who do not
> know they are supposed to click the link to get their own copy.
>
> That instruction page is the cheapest thing you will ever make and it removes
> most of your messages.
>
> And then do the thing almost nobody does. Buy your own listing. Go through the
> whole purchase as a stranger, on a different browser, and open what arrives.
> That is the only way you find out the link was wrong.

## S12 — THE A4 VERSION, AND WHAT RESIZE BREAKS (~120 words)

> Last build step: the A4 version.
>
> Resize, choose A4, and make a copy rather than converting — you want both.
>
> Then check it, because Resize scales the page and it does not re-think your
> layout. A4 is proportionally narrower and taller than Letter, so anything that
> was comfortably inside your margin can end up tight, and a table that fitted
> can end up a few millimetres over.
>
> Open every page. Look at the right edge and the bottom edge specifically.
> That is where it goes wrong, and it takes two minutes.
>
> And check your text, not just your boxes. Canva scales type when it resizes,
> so a heading that was comfortably one line at Letter can wrap to two at A4,
> and a wrapped heading pushes everything under it down. The page still looks
> fine in the thumbnail. It is wrong on paper.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~95 words)

> So, before you list it.
>
> The link says template, not edit. Both page sizes exist, Letter and A4, and
> you have opened both. The dates are 2027 dates and February has twenty eight
> days. The print file is P D F Print, flattened. If there is a digital version,
> it is a separate unflattened file. And you have bought your own listing to see
> what actually lands.
>
> Six checks. Every one of them is something a buyer would otherwise find first.
