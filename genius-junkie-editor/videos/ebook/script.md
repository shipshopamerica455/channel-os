# How to Make and Sell an Ebook in Canva

## Facts verified 2026-10-02 — all from Canva's own Help Centre

- **THE TRAP — PDF Print is the WRONG export for an ebook**, and this channel
  has told you to use it four times. Canva's own words: **PDF Standard** is
  *"for multi-page and text-heavy documents"* and you use it *"if you intend to
  share your file through email"*; **PDF Print** is *"for printing"* and is
  *"the only file type that saves professional print quality at 300 DPI"*.
  An ebook is read on a screen. 300 DPI buys nothing a screen can show and
  costs you a file size that stops people downloading it.
  Source: Help Centre, "Choose the right download file type for your Canva
  design".
- **Flattening makes it worse, twice.** Canva: flattening *"may increase file
  size, especially with high-resolution images or complex designs"* — and a
  flattened PDF loses its clickable links, which is the contents page of an
  ebook. Source: Help Centre, "Download designs as flattened PDF".
- **Compress PDF exists and is PAID.** Canva lists it as a third option for
  reduced file size, *"available only to paid Canva accounts"*. Same source.
- **Resolution figures:** PDF Standard 96 DPI, PDF Print 300 DPI. Same source,
  re-verified for this video.

NOT CLAIMED: any Gumroad, Etsy, Payhip or Kindle fee, any file-size limit on
any platform, and anything about which formats sell best. None was checked
against the platform's own page in this session. Where a limit matters the
script says "check your platform's limit", never a number.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> I have told you four times on this channel to export as P D F Print. For an
> ebook, that is the wrong answer, and it is the reason nobody finishes
> downloading yours. Canva's own help pages say it plainly: P D F Print is for
> printing. P D F Standard is, their words, for multi-page and text-heavy
> documents, and for sharing by email. An ebook is read on a screen. Three
> hundred D P I buys you nothing a screen can show, and costs you a file so
> large that people give up on it. There is a free export decision card in the
> description. Now let me build the ebook, and pick the right export at the end.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free export decision card is linked below — one page, which export for
> which job, so you never have to guess again.
>
> Three principles. One: decide now whether this will ever be printed, because
> everything else follows from that one answer. Two: an ebook is a reading
> experience, not a poster — the enemy is friction, not pixels. Three: the
> contents page has to be clickable, and one of Canva's settings quietly
> destroys that. We will come to it.
>
> And one framing worth having before we start. Nobody buys an ebook. They buy
> the thing the ebook does for them — an afternoon saved, a mistake avoided, a
> decision made. Everything from the page size to the price follows from being
> clear about which of those yours is.

## S03 — THE PAGE SIZE FOR A SCREEN (~150 words)

> Page size first, and the instinct here is wrong too.
>
> Most people reach for A4 or US Letter, because that is what a document is. But
> nobody prints your ebook. They read it on a laptop, held in landscape, or on a
> phone.
>
> A portrait A4 page on a laptop screen shows you the top third and nothing
> else. The reader is scrolling before they have read a sentence.
>
> So I am using a custom size: twelve hundred by sixteen hundred pixels. That is
> a three by four portrait — tall enough to feel like a page, short enough that a
> whole page fits on a laptop screen at once.
>
> If you genuinely might print it later, use A4 and accept the scrolling. But
> decide now. Changing page size after you have laid out forty pages is not a
> resize, it is a rebuild.

## S04 — TYPE SIZE, WHICH IS NOT A DESIGN CHOICE (~150 words)

> Now type, and this is where most Canva ebooks fail as ebooks.
>
> Body text at the size that looks elegant in the Canva editor is almost always
> too small to read on the thing it will be read on. You are looking at it
> zoomed to fit on a big monitor. Your reader is looking at it on a laptop, or
> half a phone screen.
>
> So zoom your Canva view to one hundred percent and read a paragraph. Not
> glance at it. Read it. If you find yourself leaning in, it is too small.
>
> Line length matters as much as size. Somewhere around sixty to seventy five
> characters a line is comfortable; much longer and the eye loses its place
> coming back to the left margin. On a wide page that means a generous margin,
> or two columns, and for an ebook I would always choose the margin.
>
> Two columns look professional in a magazine and are miserable in a PDF,
> because the reader has to scroll back up to the top of the page to start the
> second column. Magazines are paper. Yours is a scroll.

## S05 — THE CONTENTS PAGE, BUILT PROPERLY (~160 words)

> The contents page, and this is the thing that makes a PDF feel like a product
> rather than a file.
>
> Type your chapter list. Then select the first line, and use the link button —
> the chain icon, or command K. And here is the part people miss: you are not
> linking to a website. In the link box, choose the page. Canva will offer you
> the pages in this design.
>
> Link chapter one to page three. Chapter two to page seven. All the way down.
>
> Now the reader can jump. That is the difference between a document somebody
> opens once and a reference somebody keeps.
>
> And while we are here, do the reverse: put a small "back to contents" link in
> the footer of every chapter opener. It takes two minutes with copy and paste
> and it is the single most useful thing in most ebooks.
>
> One warning while we are building these. Links point at page numbers, so if
> you insert a page later, everything after it shifts and every link below that
> point is now one page out. Build the contents page LAST, once the page order
> is settled.

## S06 — THE TRAP: THE EXPORT THAT KILLS IT (~180 words)

> Now the export, and here is where I have to correct my own advice.
>
> Share, download, file type. And every print video I have made says choose P D F
> Print. For a printable, that is right. For this, it is wrong.
>
> Canva's own help pages describe them differently. P D F Standard: for
> multi-page and text-heavy documents, and share your file through email. P D F
> Print: for printing, and the only file type that saves professional print
> quality at three hundred D P I.
>
> An ebook is multi-page and text-heavy, and it gets shared through email. It is
> the Standard case, exactly as Canva describes it.
>
> And the cost of getting it wrong is not subtle. Three hundred D P I on a forty
> page ebook full of images gives you a file that is tens of megabytes. Your
> buyer is on a phone, on mobile data, and the download bar is not moving.
>
> Ninety six D P I is not low quality on a screen. A screen cannot show you more
> than that.

## S07 — AND DO NOT FLATTEN IT (~155 words)

> There is a second setting underneath, and I have recommended it before, and
> here it is also wrong.
>
> Flatten P D F. On a pitch deck it is exactly right — it stops people editing
> your file and it locks your fonts.
>
> On an ebook it costs you two things.
>
> First, Canva says flattening may increase file size, especially with
> high-resolution images. So you have just made the problem in the last section
> worse.
>
> Second, and this is the one that hurts: flattening turns your pages into
> images. Every link you just built — the whole contents page, every back to
> contents, every buy link at the end — stops working. The page still looks
> identical. Nothing in it is clickable any more.
>
> So for an ebook: P D F Standard, flatten off. Two settings, both the opposite
> of what a print tutorial would tell you.

## S08 — IF THE FILE IS STILL TOO BIG (~145 words)

> If your ebook is still heavy after all that, it is the images, and there are
> three things to do before you start deleting them.
>
> One: Canva has a Compress P D F option in the same download menu. It reduces
> file size and it is, by Canva's own note, only available on paid accounts. If
> you have it, try it first.
>
> Two: look at what the images are actually doing. A full-bleed photograph
> behind a chapter title is lovely and costs you megabytes. A flat colour block
> with the same type on it costs almost nothing and often looks better.
>
> Three: check your platform's own file size limit before you blame the file.
> They differ, they change, and I am not going to quote you a number that is
> wrong by the time you watch this.

## S09 — WHAT THE BUYER ACTUALLY GETS (~150 words)

> Delivery, and this is where a good ebook loses its reviews.
>
> Whatever you sell through delivers a file. Your buyer downloads it, and then
> what? On a phone it lands in a downloads folder they will never find again.
>
> So two things. Put a title page that says what the thing is, because the
> filename is often all they will see later — and name the file properly too,
> not ebook final version two.
>
> And put a single page at the front that says how to use it. Which app opens
> it, that the contents page is clickable, and where to email you if it does not
> work. Four lines.
>
> Most of your support messages are people who could not open the file. That
> page prevents nearly all of them.

## S10 — A COVER THAT WORKS AT THUMBNAIL SIZE (~150 words)

> The cover, and the only rule that matters: it is going to be seen small.
>
> On a storefront your cover is a thumbnail a couple of centimetres across. The
> elegant thin serif and the three-line subtitle that look beautiful at full
> size turn into grey mush at that size.
>
> So build the cover, then shrink it on screen until it is about the size of a
> postage stamp and look at it. If you cannot read the title, it is not finished.
>
> That usually means: fewer words, heavier type, and more contrast than feels
> comfortable. A cover is a sign, not a page.
>
> And make it the first page of the ebook as well as the listing image. People
> forward PDFs. The cover is how the next person knows what they have got.

## S11 — PRICING THE THING, HONESTLY (~145 words)

> I am not going to tell you what to charge, because I do not know your market
> and anyone who gives you a number without knowing it is guessing.
>
> But here is the useful frame. An ebook is not priced on how long it took you.
> It is priced on what it saves the person reading it — time, money, or a
> mistake.
>
> So the question is not how many pages is this. It is what does the reader not
> have to do now.
>
> Which is also an editing instruction. If a chapter does not save the reader
> something, it is padding, and padding is what people are actually complaining
> about when they say an ebook was not worth it.
>
> Shorter and sharper beats longer and thorough, almost every time.

## S12 — UPDATING IT LATER (~130 words)

> One thing almost nobody sets up, and it costs them repeat buyers.
>
> Put a version and a date on the last page. Version one point zero, October
> twenty twenty six. That is it.
>
> Why: when you update the ebook — and you will — you can tell people. An email
> that says here is version one point one, with a new chapter, free because you
> bought it, is one of the cheapest goodwill exercises there is.
>
> Without a version number you cannot do that, because nobody including you
> knows which one they have got.
>
> And keep the Canva design. Do not tidy it away into a folder you will not
> find, and do not start version two by deleting version one. The design IS the
> product. The PDF is just the copy you handed out.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~105 words)

> Before you upload it anywhere.
>
> You exported P D F Standard, not Print. Flatten is off. You opened the exported
> file — not the Canva design — and clicked a contents link to prove it still
> works. You read a paragraph at one hundred percent zoom without leaning in.
> Your cover is legible at thumbnail size. There is a how-to-open page at the
> front and a version number at the back.
>
> Six things. The contents-link one is the one people skip, and it is the one
> that generates the refund requests.

>
> Go and write something worth downloading.
