# How to Make Sublimation Designs in Canva

## Facts verified 2026-10-05

- **THE TRAP — there is no white ink in sublimation.** Printify's own guide:
  *"Dye sublimation requires light-colored materials because there's no white
  ink in the standard process. White areas are inherent to the product
  itself."* Sawgrass — who make the ink — say the same of their VersiFlex
  system: it does not currently print with white ink, so *"you will need to be
  smart about the colors of material you choose."*
  In Canva white is a colour you pick from a palette. In sublimation it is the
  absence of ink, which means **every white area is whatever your blank is**.
- **It only works on polyester or a polymer coating.** Epson, who make the
  printers: *"Dye-sublimation printing requires polyester fabrics or
  polymer-coated surfaces, making it a go-to for custom apparel, promotional
  products, and signage."* Cotton is not an option in the standard process.
- **Canva works in RGB.** Canva Help Centre, "Design with print colours":
  *"When your design is sent for printing, the colors are automatically
  converted from RGB values to the closest possible CMYK match."* You do not
  get to manage that conversion, and for your own sublimation printer Canva
  hands over an RGB file and nothing else.
- **WHITE AND TRANSPARENT ARE THE SAME THING AT THE PRESS — and nearly every
  tutorial gets this backwards.** There is no white channel, so a white area
  and a transparent area both come out as no ink. The "export transparent or
  you'll get a white box" rule is a PRINT ON DEMAND rule (where a provider
  lays down white ink) and does not apply to sublimation. The real reason to
  export transparent is that it is the only way to SEE which parts of your
  design are windows onto the blank.
- **Transparent PNG is Pro-only.** Canva Help Centre: downloading with a
  transparent background is *"only available to Canva Pro, Canva Teams, Canva
  Business, Canva Education, and Canva for Nonprofits plans."*
- **The licence NAMES MUGS.** Canva Content Licence Agreement, prohibited uses
  of Pro Content: *"…'on demand' products, including postcards, **mugs**,
  t-shirts, posters and other items."* Free content is permitted for *"prints,
  posters (i.e. hardcopies) and other reproductions for… resale"*. Mugs are the
  single biggest sublimation category, so this is not a footnote here.

NOT CLAIMED: the price of any printer, ink, paper or blank; which brand is
best; or a universal blank size. Blank templates differ by supplier and the
script says so out loud — it teaches the arithmetic (inches × three hundred)
and sends you to your own supplier's template for the numbers.

## S01 — HOOK (~120 words)

> There is no white ink in sublimation. None. Printify put it plainly: dye
> sublimation requires light coloured materials because there is no white ink
> in the standard process, and white areas are inherent to the product itself.
>
> So here is what that means for Canva. In Canva, white is a colour. You click
> it, you get it. In sublimation, white is the absence of ink — which means
> every white part of your design is not printed at all. It is simply whatever
> your blank happens to be.
>
> Design the normal Canva way, on a white background with white text, and you
> have made something that only works on a white blank and falls apart on
> anything else. There is a free sizing and colour card in the description. Now
> let me build a sublimation file properly.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free sublimation card is linked below — what the blank has to be made of,
> the sizing arithmetic, and the export settings on one page.
>
> Three principles, and they are all consequences of that one missing ink.
>
> One: white is not a colour you can print. It is a hole in your design that
> the blank fills.
>
> Two: the blank is the lightest value you will ever get. Nothing in your
> artwork can be brighter than the thing you press it onto.
>
> Three: the file goes out as a transparent P N G — not because the printer
> needs it, but because it is the only way you can see what you have actually
> made.
>
> Get those three and the rest is ordinary Canva.

## S03 — THE CANVAS SIZE (~150 words)

> Size first, and sublimation is stricter than print on demand because you are
> pressing onto a physical object that is already a fixed shape.
>
> Your blank supplier publishes a template. That is the number that counts —
> not a number from a video, including this one. Suppliers differ, and a mug
> wrap from one is not a mug wrap from another.
>
> What I can give you is the arithmetic, because it never changes. Inches times
> three hundred equals pixels. Your template says the print area is nine inches
> by three and a half? That is two thousand seven hundred by one thousand and
> fifty. A ten by twelve inch shirt area is three thousand by three thousand six
> hundred.
>
> In Canva: Custom size, switch the units to pixels, type those numbers in.
>
> Do not design at the size of the object and scale it up afterwards. You will
> be stretching a small file onto a big blank and it shows.

## S04 — DESIGNING WHEN WHITE IS NOT AVAILABLE (~155 words)

> Now the part that is genuinely different from every other Canva tutorial you
> have watched.
>
> Delete the background. Not make it white — delete it. Your canvas should be
> the chequerboard, because that chequerboard is the blank.
>
> Then go through your design and find everything that is white and ask what it
> is actually for. White text on a dark shape is fine, because the dark shape is
> ink and the letters are the blank showing through it. That works. White text
> on nothing is invisible — it is blank on blank.
>
> A white drop shadow does nothing. A white outline around dark text does
> nothing. A soft white glow does nothing at all.
>
> And watch for near white. Off white, cream, the very palest grey — those are
> ink, they do print, and on a cream blank they will print as a faint dirty
> patch you did not ask for.

## S05 — THE ELEMENTS, AND THE LICENCE (~150 words)

> Elements, and sublimation has the same licence problem as every other thing
> you make to sell.
>
> Canva's Content Licence Agreement lists what you may not do with Pro content,
> and it names the products: on demand products, including postcards, mugs,
> t-shirts and posters. Mugs. The single biggest sublimation category, named in
> the prohibited list.
>
> Free content is explicitly allowed for prints and other reproductions for
> resale. So the rule is the same one: Elements, the filter icon, price set to
> Free, every single search.
>
> One extra thing that matters more here than anywhere else. When you filter to
> free and start placing elements, prefer solid shapes over photographic ones.
> A flat vector shape sublimates beautifully. A photograph with a soft white
> vignette fading out at the edges does not, because that vignette is the part
> with no ink in it.

## S06 — THE TRAP: WATCH IT HAPPEN (~180 words)

> Let me show you the thing, because it is much more obvious seen than
> described.
>
> Here is a design. A navy banner with white lettering inside it, and behind
> that a pale starburst with a navy outline. In Canva, on the white page, that
> is exactly what I want.
>
> Press it on a white mug and it is what I drew — because every white area in
> the file is simply left alone, and what shows through is the mug, and the mug
> is white.
>
> Now the same file, same press, on a pale blue mug. The lettering inside the
> banner is blue. The starburst has gone see through — the navy outline is
> still there, but the middle of it is mug.
>
> Nothing failed. The printer did not misfire, the press was not too cold. The
> file was always like this and the white mug was hiding it.
>
> So: in sublimation, white is not a colour. It is a window, and every white
> area is a window onto the blank.
>
> The fix takes ten seconds. Drop a mid tone rectangle behind your whole design
> in Canva and look at what survives. Anything that changes colour was never
> ink.


## S07 — THE BLANK ITSELF (~150 words)

> Which brings us to the blank, because half the failed sublimation I see is not
> a design problem at all.
>
> Epson, who make the printers, put the rule in one line: dye sublimation
> printing requires polyester fabrics or polymer coated surfaces.
>
> That is not a preference. The dye turns to gas and bonds with polyester. On
> cotton there is nothing for it to bond to, so it sits on the surface looking
> acceptable for about one wash and then it is gone.
>
> Hard goods — mugs, tumblers, slate, metal signs — are the same rule in a
> different form. They are not polyester, they are coated with a polymer, and
> the coating is what takes the dye. An uncoated mug from a supermarket will
> take nothing at all.
>
> And colour. Light blanks only. On a dark blank the ink is translucent and
> simply disappears into it.

## S08 — COLOUR, AND WHY IT SHIFTS (~145 words)

> Second thing nobody warns you about: the colours will not match your screen,
> and Canva cannot help you with that.
>
> Canva works in R G B. Their own help pages say that when a design is sent for
> printing, the colours are automatically converted from R G B values to the
> closest possible C M Y K match. Automatically. You are not invited to the
> conversion.
>
> And that is Canva's own print service. For your sublimation printer, Canva
> hands you an R G B P N G and your printer software does whatever it does.
>
> Practically: bright saturated colours tend to come out close. Deep navy
> drifts purple. Bright red often presses orange. Neons do not exist.
>
> So the habit is simple — press a colour swatch sheet once, onto your actual
> blanks, and keep it. Your own swatch beats any screen.

## S09 — THE EXPORT (~175 words)

> The export, and there is a myth to clear up first.
>
> Everyone tells you to export a transparent P N G or you will get a white box.
> That is true for print on demand, where a provider lays down white ink onto a
> dark shirt. It is not true here. Your sublimation printer has no white ink at
> all, so a white background and a transparent background do exactly the same
> thing. No ink. Blank shows through.
>
> Which sounds like good news and is the opposite. It means a white rectangle
> you forgot about costs you nothing, and a white starburst you were relying on
> costs you everything — and the file looks identical either way.
>
> So export transparent anyway. Not for the printer. For you. On a transparent
> P N G you can see the windows. Open it on a dark background and every window
> lights up.
>
> Share, Download, P N G, transparent background on. Canva's help pages are
> explicit that this one is paid: only available to Pro, Teams, Business,
> Education and Nonprofits.
>
> And tick the size up to two times if it is offered. More pixels is free, and
> sublimation is unforgiving about resolution.

## S10 — MIRRORING: ONCE, NEVER TWICE (~135 words)

> Mirroring. Sublimation prints onto paper and then you press the paper face
> down onto the blank, so the image flips. Something in the chain has to mirror
> it.
>
> Canva has a flip. Select everything, Flip, Horizontal, and your design is
> mirrored.
>
> But most sublimation printer software mirrors for you automatically, and some
> of it does so without telling you. Mirror in Canva as well and you have
> mirrored twice, which is the same as not mirroring at all — and you find out
> when the text comes out backwards on a blank you have just paid for.
>
> So: check your printer software first. If it mirrors, do nothing in Canva. If
> it does not, flip in Canva. One or the other, never both, and write down which
> one you chose.

## S11 — PRINT PAST THE EDGE (~140 words)

> If your design is meant to run off the edge of the blank — a full wrap mug, a
> full front shirt panel — you have to give it somewhere to go.
>
> Nothing in this process is perfectly aligned. You tape the paper on by hand.
> The press has a margin. The blank is round.
>
> So extend your artwork past the canvas edge. In Canva that means dragging the
> background shape bigger than the page, so it is cropped by the page boundary
> rather than stopping short of it. If it stops short, you get a thin unprinted
> line of blank along one edge, and on a mug that line goes all the way round.
>
> And keep anything that must be read — names, dates, a logo — well inside the
> edges. On a mug, further in than feels necessary, because the handle eats more
> than you think.

## S12 — THE PRESS LOG (~125 words)

> One habit, and it costs you nothing.
>
> Every time you press a new type of blank, write down three things: the blank,
> the temperature and the time. That is it. Three lines in a notebook.
>
> Sublimation is not one process, it is a different process per material. A
> ceramic mug and a polyester shirt and a slate coaster all want different
> settings, and the only reliable source for your particular press is your
> particular press.
>
> You will get a result you love and then be unable to reproduce it four months
> later. That is the thing the notebook prevents.
>
> Keep the Canva file, the exported P N G, and the three lines. Same folder,
> same name.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~110 words)

> Before you press.
>
> The background is deleted, not white. Everything that needs to be visible is
> ink, not white. You have looked at the design on a mid-tone background and it
> still reads. The P N G is transparent and in the thousands of pixels. Your
> blank is polyester or polymer coated, and it is light. You have mirrored
> exactly once, in the printer software or in Canva but not both. And every
> element came from a search filtered to Free.
>
> Seven things. The first three are all the same thing, which tells you how
> often it is the thing that goes wrong.
