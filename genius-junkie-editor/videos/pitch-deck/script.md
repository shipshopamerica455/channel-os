# How to Make a Pitch Deck in Canva 2026 (And What Your PDF Is Still Carrying)

## Facts verified 2026-08-16 — all from Canva's own Help Centre

- **THE TRAP — cropped images survive the export.** Canva's download docs, word
  for word: *"If you download a design containing cropped images to a PDF file,
  the PDF format will include the original image. This means someone could use
  a third-party tool (for example, a PDF editor) to get the full, uncropped
  image from your PDF export."* Their stated advice: delete the content you do
  not want seen, or recreate the image, **before** downloading.
  Source: Help Centre, "Choose the right download file type for your Canva design".
- **THE FIX — Flatten PDF.** Share → Download → PDF Standard or PDF Print →
  tick **Flatten PDF**. Canva: *"converts the file into a static image,
  preventing editing in software like Adobe Acrobat"* and *"ensures fonts
  display accurately and secures your design."* Cost: a bigger file.
  Source: Help Centre, "Download designs as flattened PDF". Not marked Pro-only.
- **PDF Standard = 96 dpi. PDF Print = 300 dpi**, with bleed, crop marks and a
  colour profile option. Same source as the trap.
- **PPTX is presentations only**, and Canva warns: *"Designs downloaded as PPTX
  or DOCX files may look different when opened in Microsoft PowerPoint... You
  may need to make edits, or download and install the fonts you used in Canva."*
  And: *"Animations and embedded videos are not yet supported in Microsoft
  PowerPoint."* Same source.
- **Notes** live at the bottom of the editor and are *"only visible in Presenter
  View"* — but the PDF download has an **Include notes** checkbox, which puts
  your private presenter cues into the file you send.
  Source: Help Centre, "Download designs with notes".
- **Download pages as separate files is Pro-only.** Free users see the toggle
  but cannot use it. Same source.
- The 10/20/30 rule (ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point type) is Guy
  Kawasaki's, and it is a guideline, not a Canva feature. Attributed as such.

Target: ~1,900 words @ 3.70 w/s ≈ 8 min 34 s. Ad breaks after S05 and S09.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> You crop a screenshot so the investor cannot see your real revenue number.
> You export the deck as a P D F. You send it. And the number is still in
> there. Not visible, but still in the file, and anybody can pull it back out
> with a free P D F editor in about ten seconds. That is not a bug and it is
> not a rumour. It is written in Canva's own help pages, and there is one
> checkbox that fixes it. I will show you exactly where. But first we are going
> to build the deck itself, ten slides, from an empty page, and then I will
> show you the three things your finished file is carrying that you never meant
> to send.

## S02 — A DECK IS A FILE, NOT A PRESENTATION (~140 words)

> Here is the frame that makes everything else in this video make sense. You
> are not building a presentation. You are building a file that gets forwarded.
> Think about what actually happens. You send the deck to one person. They skim
> it on a phone. If they like it, they forward it to two partners you have
> never met, who open it without you in the room, with no voiceover and no
> context. So every slide has to survive being read alone and in silence. That
> kills the two habits people bring from school presentations. No slide that
> only makes sense while you are talking over it. And no slide so dense that
> nobody reads it on a phone. One idea per slide, and the idea is the headline,
> not the body text.

## S03 — THE TEN SLIDES (~155 words)

> Open Canva and search Presentation. That gives you sixteen by nine at nineteen
> twenty by ten eighty, which is the size every investor expects. Ten slides.
> This is Guy Kawasaki's old rule, ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty point
> type, and it has survived because it is right. Here they are. One, the title
> and one line on what you do. Two, the problem. Three, the solution. Four, how
> it works. Five, the market. Six, the business model, which is just how you
> make money. Seven, traction, and that is the slide they actually read. Eight,
> competition. Nine, the team. Ten, the ask, how much you want and what it
> buys. That is the whole thing. If you cannot fill one of those slides, that
> is not a design problem, that is something to go and find out. And keep them
> in that order. It is not arbitrary. Problem before solution, because a
> solution to a problem nobody has stated sounds like a hobby. Traction before
> competition, because once they believe somebody wants this, they read the
> competition slide as reassurance instead of as a warning.

## S04 — CANVAS SETUP AND THE FONT DECISION (~145 words)

> Before you place anything, two decisions that are painful to undo. First,
> colours. Open a shape, set it to your brand colour, and it lands in the
> document colours row so every element after it can pull from the same set.
> Second, and this one matters more than it looks, the font. If you think there
> is any chance an investor will ask for an editable copy, pick a font that
> exists on other people's computers. Arial, Verdana, Georgia. Because a
> PowerPoint export does not carry your font with it, and I will show you what
> that looks like later. Set the type sizes now as well. Headline forty to
> fifty four, body twenty four minimum, and nothing under eighteen anywhere in
> the deck. If it does not fit at twenty four, it is too many words.

## S05 — BUILDING THE FIRST THREE SLIDES (~190 words)

> Slide one. Text, click a heading, type your company name. Underneath, one
> line that says what you do in plain words, no adjectives. Add your logo small
> in a corner, not enormous in the middle. Slide two, the problem. One
> sentence at the top as the headline, and this is the key move, write it as a
> statement, not a topic. Not "the problem". Instead, the actual sentence.
> Underneath it, three short lines of evidence, and if you have a number, the
> number goes in bigger type than the words around it. Slide three, the
> solution. Same shape. Headline sentence, then show it rather than describe
> it, so a screenshot, a photo, a diagram, anything that is not a paragraph.
> Now duplicate slide two to build the rest, because duplicating is what keeps
> the type sizes and the margins identical across ten slides. Every time you
> start a slide from scratch you are inviting the deck to drift. Right click the
> page, duplicate, then swap the words. And leave the margins alone. Pick a
> distance from the edge on slide one and never change it, because the eye
> notices a headline that moved four pixels between slides even when it cannot
> say why. That consistency is most of what people mean when they call a deck
> professional. It is not the colours and it is not the icons.

**→ AD BREAK 1** (1.4 s scripted silence + hard cut card, after S05)

## S06 — WHAT YOUR PDF IS STILL CARRYING (~200 words)

> Right. The thing I promised. Say you have a screenshot of your dashboard, and
> the real revenue figure is in the corner, and you are not ready to share it.
> So you crop it out. It is gone from the screen, you export the deck as a
> P D F, and you send it. Here is Canva's own documentation. If you download a
> design containing cropped images to a P D F, the P D F will include the
> original image. Which means somebody can open your deck in a P D F editor and
> pull the full uncropped picture straight back out. The crop is a mask. It
> hides the pixels. It does not delete them. Canva's own advice is to delete
> the content or rebuild the image before you download. But there is a faster
> fix, and it is one checkbox. When you download, choose P D F, and tick
> Flatten P D F. Canva says flattening converts the file into a static image,
> which prevents editing in software like Acrobat, and it makes fonts display
> accurately. Your cropped pixels are gone, your layout is locked, and nobody
> is editing your deck. It costs you a bigger file. Tick it every single time.

## S07 — THE TRACTION SLIDE (~175 words)

> Slide seven is the one they actually read, so it gets its own rules. Numbers
> beat adjectives, always. Not "strong growth". The number, and the window it
> covers. One chart, not three. Click Elements, search Charts, and pick a line
> chart if you are showing something over time, a bar chart if you are
> comparing things. Then delete the chart's own title, because your slide
> headline already said it, and two titles on one slide is the fastest way to
> look amateur. Now the part people skip. Label the axis and put the date range
> on it. A line going up with no dates on the bottom is not evidence, it is
> decoration, and an investor who reads decks all day will read it as something
> you are hiding. And if the number is small, say it anyway, with the window
> attached. Honest and small beats vague and impressive, because vague is what
> they see forty times a week. And one last thing about this slide in
> particular. If that chart is a screenshot of your own dashboard, it is
> exactly the kind of image the last section was about, so go back and check
> you flattened it.

## S08 — SPEAKER NOTES, AND THE CHECKBOX THAT LEAKS THEM (~160 words)

> At the bottom of the editor there is Notes. Anything you type there is only
> visible in Presenter View, so it is the right place for the things you want
> to say but not show. The number you will quote if they ask. The thing you are
> not going to mention unless they bring it up first. Write them there rather
> than cramming them onto the slide, and the slide stays readable. But here is
> the catch, and it is the second thing your file carries. When you download as
> a P D F, there is a checkbox called Include notes. It exists so you can print
> your own cue cards, and it is genuinely useful for that. Tick it by accident
> before you send the deck to an investor, and you have just emailed them every
> private note you wrote to yourself. Look at that checkbox before every send.

## S09 — POWERPOINT, HONESTLY (~150 words)

> Sooner or later somebody asks for it in PowerPoint. Here is what actually
> happens, again in Canva's own words. Designs downloaded as P P T X may look
> different when opened in PowerPoint. You may need to make edits, or download
> and install the fonts you used in Canva onto your device. And separately,
> animations and embedded videos are not supported in PowerPoint, so anything
> that moves in your deck will not move in theirs. So the rule is simple. Send
> a P D F by default. It looks identical on every machine, and flattened, it
> cannot be edited. Only send P P T X when they have specifically said they
> want to edit it, and when you do, tell them which font you used so the layout
> does not collapse on them. That one sentence saves an embarrassing email.

**→ AD BREAK 2** (1.4 s scripted silence + hard cut card, after S09)

## S10 — SENDING IT (~150 words)

> Three things before it leaves your outbox. File size. Investors read decks on
> phones and some corporate mail servers bounce anything over about ten
> megabytes, and flattening makes the file bigger, so check it. If it is heavy,
> download at P D F Standard rather than P D F Print, because Standard is
> ninety six D P I and Print is three hundred, and three hundred is for paper,
> not for email. Second, the filename. Your company name and the month.
> Not "deck final version three", which tells them exactly how many versions
> there were. Third, attach it, do not just link it. A link can be revoked, it
> can ask them to sign in, and it tells you nothing good about how much you
> trust them. The forward is the whole point, so make forwarding effortless.

## S11 — PRESENTING IT LIVE (~140 words)

> If you do get in the room, use Presenter View rather than plain full screen.
> It gives you your notes on your screen and the slides on theirs, plus a
> timer, and the timer matters more than you would think. Twenty minutes of
> content for a thirty minute meeting, because the good meetings get
> interrupted constantly and the bad ones are the ones where you got to talk
> uninterrupted for the full thirty. Practise the first two slides until you do
> not need the notes at all, because that is where you either have the room or
> you do not. And have the P D F open in another window. Screen sharing fails,
> the internet drops, and the person who can carry on from a file on their
> desktop looks unbothered.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~105 words)

> Before you send it. Ten slides, one idea each, every headline a sentence
> rather than a topic. Nothing under eighteen point anywhere. The traction
> chart has a labelled axis and a date range. A system font if there is any
> chance they want to edit it. Include notes UNticked. Filename is your company
> and the month. Under ten megabytes. And the one that actually matters, tick
> Flatten P D F, so the pixels you cropped out are really gone and nobody can
> open your deck in Acrobat and start editing it.
