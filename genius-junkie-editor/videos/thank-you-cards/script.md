# How to Make Thank You Cards in Canva (Folded, Print at Home)

## Facts verified 2026-10-02

- **THE TRAP — the back panel of a half-fold card is upside down.** This is
  GEOMETRY, not a platform quirk, which is why it is demonstrated on screen
  rather than quoted. Fold a landscape sheet in half across the short way and
  the top half rotates one hundred and eighty degrees relative to the bottom
  half. So on a single-sheet half-fold card, the FRONT lives on the right half
  and the BACK lives on the left half **rotated one eighty**. Lay it out
  "normally" and the back of every card prints upside down.
- **Duplex flip edge decides where side two lands.** Printer drivers call it
  "flip on long edge" / "flip on short edge" (or "binding: long/short"), and
  the naming is not consistent between makes. The script therefore does NOT
  assert one correct setting — it says print ONE test card, look at it, and if
  the inside is upside down, change the flip edge and print one more.
- **PDF Standard is 96 DPI; PDF Print is 300 DPI** — Canva Help Centre,
  "Download designs as flattened PDF". Re-verified for this video.
- **Home printers have a non-printable border** of roughly three to six
  millimetres — established in the baby-shower video and unchanged.
- **A6 is 105 × 148 mm**, computed from ISO 216 (A4 210 × 297 halved twice).
  An A5 sheet folded once gives an A6 card.

NOT CLAIMED: any card-stock weight a specific printer will accept, any postage
price, any Canva subscription price. None was checked against a manufacturer's
or carrier's own page in this session.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> If you lay out a folded card the way it looks right on screen, the back of it
> prints upside down. Every time. Not a Canva bug — it is just what folding is.
> Fold a sheet in half and the top half turns over, so the panel that becomes
> the back of your card is rotated a hundred and eighty degrees relative to the
> front. Your design does not know that. It prints the back the right way up on
> the sheet, which is the wrong way up on the card. There is a free fold and
> print card in the description with the layout drawn out. And now I will build
> the cards so that never happens.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~110 words)

> The free fold and print card is linked below — one page, the layout and the
> settings, stuck to the wall next to the printer.
>
> Three principles. One: a folded card is ONE sheet with two panels on each
> side, not four separate pages, and thinking of it as one sheet is what makes
> the geometry obvious. Two: print one before you print thirty. Three: the fold
> is the most visible thing about a handmade card — people forgive a lot, but a
> fold that misses by three millimetres looks careless from across a room.
>
> And a note on that first one, because it is the whole video. Most people
> build a folded card as four separate designs — front, back, inside left,
> inside right — and then cannot work out why it comes out wrong. It is not
> four things. It is one sheet, printed on both sides, with a crease down the
> middle. Hold that in your head and the rest is obvious.

## S03 — THE SIZE, AND WHAT IT FOLDS FROM (~140 words)

> Start from the finished card, then work backwards to the sheet.
>
> I am making an A6 card — a hundred and five by a hundred and forty eight
> millimetres. That is a lovely size for a thank you: big enough to write a
> paragraph, small enough to feel personal.
>
> An A6 card folded comes from an A5 sheet, because A5 folded in half IS A6.
> That is how the whole A series works — each size is the one above halved
> across the long side.
>
> So in Canva: custom size, A5, landscape. Two hundred and ten wide by a
> hundred and forty eight high. One page is the OUTSIDE of the card. A second
> page is the INSIDE.
>
> Two pages. One sheet. That is the whole document.
>
> If your buyers are American, the equivalent is a card of five and a half by
> four and a quarter inches, folded from a sheet of eight and a half by five
> and a half. Same idea, different paper. If you are selling these, you want
> both — but build one first and convert at the end.

## S04 — THE MARGIN, AND THE FOLD LINE (~145 words)

> Two guides before anything else.
>
> First, a margin. Home printers cannot print to the edge — there is a strip of
> three to six millimetres they physically cannot reach. So nothing important
> within ten millimetres of any edge. I am putting guides there now.
>
> Second, and this is the one people skip: a guide down the exact centre. A
> hundred and five millimetres across a two hundred and ten millimetre sheet.
> That is the fold.
>
> Now look at what that centre line does. It splits the outside page into two
> panels. The right-hand panel is the FRONT of the card. The left-hand panel is
> the BACK.
>
> Not the left is the front. The right. Because when you fold it, the right
> panel comes over the top and lands face up.

## S05 — DESIGNING THE FRONT (~150 words)

> The front panel, right-hand side.
>
> Thank you, large, centred in the panel — and centred in the PANEL, not on the
> sheet. This is the single most common slip: you hit align-centre, Canva
> centres it on the two hundred and ten millimetre sheet, and your lettering
> sits on the fold.
>
> So select the text, and set the position by hand. Panel centre is fifty two
> and a half millimetres from the right edge of the sheet, because the panel is
> a hundred and five wide.
>
> Underneath, something small — a line, a sprig, a dot. Thank you cards do not
> need much. The front is a door, not a wall.
>
> And keep it inside that ten millimetre margin. Anything that touches the edge
> is going to come out with a white hairline, because the printer could not
> reach it.

## S06 — THE TRAP: THE BACK IS UPSIDE DOWN (~180 words)

> Now the back panel, and this is where folded cards go wrong.
>
> Watch. I put a small line of text on the left panel — just my name, the way
> you would on the back of a card.
>
> Looks right. Print it. Fold it.
>
> Upside down.
>
> And here is why, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. Fold a sheet in
> half and the top half flips over. The left panel does not stay where it is —
> it rotates a hundred and eighty degrees and becomes the back of the card.
>
> So anything on the left panel has to be placed upside down ON THE SHEET to
> come out the right way up ON THE CARD.
>
> Select it. Rotate one eighty. It now looks wrong on your screen, and that is
> the point. Your screen is a flat sheet. The card is a folded object. They
> disagree, and the card wins.
>
> Nothing in Canva warns you. It cannot — it does not know you are going to
> fold it.

## S07 — THE INSIDE, WHICH IS THE EASY ONE (~145 words)

> Page two. The inside.
>
> Good news: the inside does NOT need rotating, and that catches people the
> other way. Once the sheet is folded, the inside spread reads as one
> landscape area, left to right, exactly as it looks on screen.
>
> So the message goes on the right half, the way it would in a book. The left
> half is usually left empty, or takes something very small.
>
> Why no rotation? Because the inside is the same side of the sheet behaving
> normally — you are reading it as a spread, not as two panels that flipped.
> Only the panel that folds BEHIND changes its orientation.
>
> One rotated panel, on the outside, on the left. That is the entire rule.
>
> Here is the test that settles it without thinking. Take a sheet of scrap
> paper. Write FRONT on the bottom right, BACK on the bottom left, and fold it.
> Look at where BACK ended up. Thirty seconds, and you will never have to
> reason about it again — which is better than remembering a rule, because the
> rule only covers the card you are making today.

## S08 — PRINTING ONE (~150 words)

> Download. P D F Print, not P D F Standard — Standard is ninety six D P I and
> this will have type on it.
>
> Now the printer dialog, and the setting that matters is double sided.

> Your printer calls it flip on long edge or flip on short edge, or sometimes
> binding, long or short. The names are not consistent between makes, and I am
> not going to tell you which one yours needs — because I do not know what you
> are printing on.
>
> Here is what you do instead. Print ONE. Fold it. Look at it.
>
> If the inside is upside down relative to the front, go back and change the
> flip edge to the other one. Print one more. That is the whole diagnostic, it
> costs you two sheets of paper, and it is faster than reasoning about it.

## S09 — THE FOLD ITSELF (~145 words)

> The fold is the bit that makes it look bought or homemade, and it takes
> thirty seconds to do properly.
>
> Do not fold freehand. Score it first. Run the back of a butter knife, or a
> bone folder if you have one, down the centre line against a ruler. You are
> crushing the paper fibres in a straight line, not cutting them.
>
> Then fold against the score, and run your thumbnail along it.
>
> On anything heavier than ordinary paper, scoring is not optional. Card stock
> that is folded without scoring cracks along the spine — the surface splits
> and you get a white line through your fold. Once it cracks, that card is
> finished.
>
> Score, fold, press. Every time.
>
> What scoring actually does is worth knowing, because it tells you when you
> can skip it. Paper is layers of fibre. Bending it stretches the fibres on the
> outside of the curve, and if they cannot stretch they tear — which is the
> white line. Scoring crushes them in a controlled line first, so the fold has
> somewhere to happen.
>
> On ordinary printer paper you can get away without it. On anything that feels
> like card in your hand, you cannot.

## S10 — FOUR UP ON A SHEET (~140 words)

> If you need twenty cards, do not print twenty A5 sheets.
>
> Make a new design at A4, portrait, and place four A6 card fronts on it, two
> by two. Each one still needs its own ten millimetre margin, and the artwork
> on adjacent cards should touch where they meet, so that when you cut down the
> shared line both sides come out with colour right to the edge.
>
> That is how you get a full bleed card out of a printer that cannot do full
> bleed. You are not printing to the edge. You are cutting to it.
>
> And cut with a steel rule and a sharp blade, three light passes rather than
> one hard one. Scissors will wander, and a wandering edge is the other thing
> that makes a card look homemade.

## S11 — WHAT TO WRITE (~140 words)

> One short detour, because the thing people actually get stuck on is not the
> design.
>
> A thank you card has three jobs, and it is four sentences long. Name the
> specific thing. Say what it meant or what you did with it. Say something
> forward-looking. Sign it.
>
> That is it. The specific thing is the whole trick — thank you for the
> generous gift is a card anyone could have sent to anyone. Thank you for the
> blue casserole dish, which has already survived two Sunday roasts, is a card
> only you could have sent to them.
>
> If you are making these to sell, leave the inside blank and say so in the
> listing. Buyers want the words to be theirs.

## S12 — MAKING IT A PRODUCT (~130 words)

> If this is going in a shop rather than an envelope, three changes.
>
> One: supply both page sizes. A5 folding to A6 for most of the world, and
> five and a half by four and a quarter inches folding from eight and a half by
> five and a half for the United States. Your buyers do not share a paper size.
>
> Two: supply a printed-one-up version AND a four-up sheet, because some people
> want one card and some want twenty.
>
> Three: put the fold and cut instructions in the file itself, not just the
> listing. People lose the listing. They do not lose the PDF.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~95 words)

> Before you print thirty, or list it.
>
> The left panel on the outside is rotated one eighty. The inside is NOT
> rotated. Nothing is within ten millimetres of an edge. Your title is centred
> on its panel and not on the sheet. You exported P D F Print. And you printed
> one, folded it, and looked at it.
>
> Six things. The last one catches everything the first five miss.
>
> And keep the test card. Write the printer and the flip setting on the back of
> it in pencil and put it in the drawer with the paper. Next time you make
> cards you will not have to work it out again, because the answer is sitting
> there in your handwriting.
