# How to Make a Photo Calendar in Canva

## Facts verified 2026-10-16

- **THE TRAP — Canva adds the bleed OUTSIDE your page, and it is not
  adjustable.** Canva Help Centre, "Use margins, bleed, rulers, and crop
  marks": *"Canva adds 0.125 inches (3.175 mm) on all sides for products that
  trim or get cut"*, and *"Changing margin, bleed, and crop mark sizes are also
  not available. These are fixed and are measured based on our print
  specifications."* Turn it on with **File → Settings → Show print bleed**; a
  dashed border appears near the edges. Canva's own instruction when it is
  showing: *"resize or stretch your background to cover them."*
  A photo calendar is the one design where **every page is a full-bleed
  photograph**, so a photo dropped to "fit" the page is three millimetres short
  on all four sides — and that is a white sliver on all twelve months.
- **THE MARGIN IS THE OTHER HALF.** Same page: margins are *"a layout guide for
  balanced design"* that keep *"important content, such as text, logos, or key
  visuals, safely away from the edge of your design."* On a calendar the dates
  are the important content, and the trim is what eats them.
- **THE UPLOAD CEILING.** Canva Help Centre, "Check file upload formats and
  requirements": images must be *"under 50MB in size"* and *"not more than 250
  million total pixels (width x height)"*, in JPEG, PNG, HEIC/HEIF or WebP.
- **THE EXPORT.** Canva Help Centre, "Choose the right download file type":
  **PDF Print** is *"for printing with a resolution of 300 dpi, with bleed,
  crop marks, and color profile (RGB or CMYK) selection option"*. An A4 page at
  300 DPI is 2,480 × 3,508 pixels; with Canva's bleed it is about 2,565 × 3,583.

NOT CLAIMED: what Canva Print or any print shop charges, turnaround times,
paper weights or finishes. None was checked against a price page in this
session, so none is stated.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> Your photo calendar is going to print with a white line down the edge of
> every single month, and you will not see it until all twelve are in the box.
>
> Canva adds the bleed outside your page. Their words: nought point one two
> five inches, three point one seven five millimetres, on all sides for
> products that trim or get cut. And you cannot change it — fixed, their print
> specifications.
>
> So a photo you dropped in to fit the page is three millimetres short on all
> four sides. On screen it looks perfect. The trimmer cuts through where your
> photo already ended.
>
> There is a free photo calendar print card in the description. Let me build
> one properly.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free card is linked below — the bleed figure, the pixel table for every
> page size, and the upload ceiling, on one page.
>
> Three principles.
>
> One: this is a gift, not a planner. Nobody hangs it for the date grid. The
> photographs do the work and the dates just have to be correct and legible.
>
> Two: everything that touches an edge gets stretched past it. Not nudged —
> past it, beyond the dashed line, into the part that gets thrown away.
>
> Three: the photo has to be big enough before you crop it, because cropping is
> where the pixels go.
>
> A gift, stretched past the edge, with pixels to spare.

## S03 — THE SIZE, AND THE PAGES (~155 words)

> Start with the page, and with the fact that a calendar is two designs
> pretending to be one.
>
> Pick A four or letter unless you already know your printer wants something
> else. Canva has both under Documents. A four is two ten by two ninety seven
> millimetres.
>
> Then: thirteen pages, not twelve. A cover, then January to December. People
> forget the cover and end up with December facing the wall for a month.
>
> And decide now whether it hangs or stands, because that changes everything.
> A wall calendar is usually portrait with the photo on top and the grid
> underneath. A desk calendar is landscape and the photo is often the whole
> page with the grid beside it.
>
> Set it up as one design with thirteen pages. One design downloads as one
> multi-page P D F, which is what a printer wants. Thirteen separate designs is
> thirteen files and a conversation you do not want to have.

## S04 — WHICH PHOTOS ACTUALLY WORK (~155 words)

> Now the photographs, and this is where most photo calendars are decided
> before anybody opens Canva.
>
> You need the photo to be about two and a half thousand pixels on its long
> edge for an A four page. That is the number to check. Not megapixels, not how
> it looks on your phone — the pixel dimensions, in your file browser.
>
> A photo straight off a modern phone is comfortably over that. The ones that
> fail are the copies: anything that came through a messaging app, anything
> downloaded off social media, anything that was emailed. Those are
> compressed on the way, often down to around sixteen hundred pixels, and you
> cannot tell by looking at them on a screen.
>
> So go back to the original wherever you can. The version in your camera roll,
> not the one somebody sent you back.
>
> And Canva will not take anything over fifty megabytes, or over two hundred
> and fifty million pixels — which only bites on scans and panoramas.

## S05 — THE PHOTO WELL AND THE GRID (~150 words)

> Then the layout, and the easiest version of it that still looks deliberate.
>
> Turn on the guides. File, Settings, show margins and show print bleed. You
> will get two dashed borders: the outer one is the bleed, the inner one is the
> margin.
>
> Put the photo in the top portion as a frame, or as a plain image you crop to
> the shape. A frame is easier because it crops to fill automatically — but
> watch where it crops, because Canva crops from the centre and that is how
> everybody loses the tops of heads.
>
> The grid goes underneath. Seven columns, six rows, a header row for the
> weekday initials. Keep all of it inside the margin guide.
>
> And set the month name once, in one text style, and then copy that page
> twelve times. Consistency across thirteen pages is the whole look.

## S06 — THE TRAP, DEMONSTRATED (~190 words)

> Here is the bleed, and I am going to get it wrong first so you can see it.
>
> File, Settings, show print bleed. There is the dashed line, just outside the
> page edge.
>
> Now I drop a photo in and size it to the page. Looks right. Edge to edge,
> nothing hanging over, very tidy. And that is the mistake — because the page
> edge is not where the paper gets cut. The cut happens somewhere between the
> page edge and that dashed line, and no two copies get cut in exactly the same
> place.
>
> So on this copy you get a white line down the right. On the next one you get
> it along the bottom. Twelve months, thirteen pages, every one of them a
> gamble.
>
> The fix is one drag. Canva's own instruction when bleed is showing: resize or
> stretch your background to cover them. So I pull the photo out past the
> dashed line on all four sides.
>
> Now the photo overshoots the cut everywhere, and wherever the blade lands
> there is photograph under it. That is what bleed is for.
>
> It costs you three millimetres of picture and it saves the whole calendar.

## S07 — THE PIXELS YOU HAVE LEFT (~155 words)

> And that drag has a cost, which is the second half of this.
>
> An A four page at three hundred D P I is two thousand four hundred and eighty
> by three thousand five hundred and eight pixels. Add Canva's bleed and you
> are at roughly two thousand five hundred and sixty five by three thousand
> five hundred and eighty three.
>
> So the photo has to cover more than the page, after you have already cropped
> it to the shape of the photo well.
>
> This is why the number to check is the long edge before you start. Two and a
> half thousand gives you room. Sixteen hundred does not, and no amount of
> dragging in Canva adds detail that was never in the file.
>
> If a photo is too small there are exactly two honest options: use it smaller,
> inset with a border around it, or do not use that photo. Stretching it to
> fill the page is the third option and it is the one that looks cheap in
> print.

## S08 — THE DATES (~145 words)

> The dates, which are the boring half and the half that gets noticed.
>
> Check the first weekday of every month against a real calendar. Not from
> memory, and not from last year's file — the first of the month moves every
> year, and a calendar with one month wrong is firewood.
>
> Decide whether your week starts Monday or Sunday and keep it the same on all
> twelve pages. Mixed is worse than either.
>
> Mark the public holidays for wherever this is going, and if you are gifting
> it, put the birthdays in. That is the single thing that turns a nice object
> into a thing somebody keeps.
>
> And keep the numbers inside the margin guide. They are the important content
> that Canva's margin exists to protect, and a date clipped by the trimmer is
> the one defect everybody spots immediately.

## S09 — WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE AT ARM'S LENGTH (~150 words)

> Before you export, the check that costs ten seconds.
>
> A calendar is not read at arm's length. It is read from across a kitchen.
>
> So zoom out until the page is about the size of a playing card, and then
> stand back from the screen.
>
> What should survive: the photograph, obviously, and the shape of the grid.
> And crucially, this month's number block should still read as a block of
> numbers rather than grey fuzz.
>
> If the dates have dissolved, they are too small or too light. Make them
> bigger before you make them prettier — a thin elegant numeral at eight point
> is useless on a kitchen wall.
>
> And look at the photo at that size too. A busy photograph with no clear
> subject turns into mush when it is small, and it was probably already mush
> when it was big.

## S10 — THE EXPORT (~145 words)

> The export, and for this one there is only one correct answer.
>
> Download, P D F Print. Canva's own description: three hundred D P I, with
> bleed, crop marks and a colour profile option.
>
> Tick crop marks and bleed. The crop marks are the little corner lines that
> tell whoever is trimming it where the cut goes, and the bleed is the three
> millimetres of overshoot you just dragged out.
>
> Not P D F Standard, which is ninety six D P I and will print soft. Not J P G
> or P N G, which give you thirteen separate files with no crop marks on any of
> them.
>
> And open the P D F before you send it. Look at the corners — you should see
> the crop marks sitting outside the page, and photograph running all the way
> out to them. If the photo stops at the page edge, you did not stretch it.

## S11 — GETTING IT PRINTED (~140 words)

> Then printing it, and I am going to be careful here because I have not
> checked any printer's prices or specifications today, so I am not going to
> quote you any.
>
> What I will tell you is what to ask them. Three questions.
>
> Does your bleed requirement match three millimetres? Most do, but ask, and if
> theirs is different you set the page size up differently rather than arguing
> with Canva's fixed bleed.
>
> Do you want the P D F with crop marks, or without? Some want them, some
> strip them.
>
> And how do you bind it? Wire, staple and spiral all eat a different amount of
> the inside edge, and that affects where you put the grid.
>
> Ask before you design the thirteenth page, not after.

## S12 — NEXT YEAR (~125 words)

> One habit, because this is the one thing you will make again.
>
> Once it is right, strip it. Delete the photographs, keep the page size, the
> grid, the text styles and the margin setup, and save that as its own design.
> Call it the template.
>
> Next year you duplicate the template and change the dates. You do not
> duplicate this year's calendar, because this year's calendar has this year's
> dates in it and that is exactly the mistake that ships.
>
> And keep a plain text note: page size, bleed on, which photos you used and
> where the originals live. When somebody asks for a second copy in March you
> will want that note.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~110 words)

> Before you export.
>
> Show print bleed is on, and every photograph is stretched past the dashed
> line on all four sides. Every date sits inside the margin guide. The first
> weekday of all twelve months has been checked against a real calendar. Every
> photo was at least two and a half thousand pixels on its long edge before you
> cropped it. There are thirteen pages, including a cover. The week starts on
> the same day throughout. And you exported P D F Print with crop marks, not
> P D F Standard.
>
> Seven things. The first one is the one that ruins all twelve months at once.
