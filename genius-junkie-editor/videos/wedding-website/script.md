# How to Make a Wedding Website in Canva

## Facts verified 2026-10-19

- **THE TRAP — the RSVP form belongs to whoever made it.** Google Docs Editors
  Help, "View & manage form responses": responses are collected in the form,
  and *"form owners can download and delete existing responses"*; they can be
  linked to a spreadsheet with *Link to Sheets*. So when the RSVP button on a
  wedding website points at **your** Google Form, every guest's reply lands in
  **your** Responses tab — not the couple's. If you build sites for other
  people, or sell this as a template, every couple's guest list ends up in your
  account.
- **THE FIX — a copy, not a share.** Google's help: *More → Make a copy*. For
  handing a form to someone else, a Google Docs Editors **Community guide**
  ("How to share a Google Form so that others can make a copy of it") describes
  changing the end of the form's URL to `/copy`, which prompts whoever opens it
  to make their own copy — and *"viewers that make a copy of your form can edit
  their own copy"*. That method is a community guide, not an official policy
  page, and is said so on screen.
- **CANVA'S PUBLISHING RULES.** Canva Help Centre, "Publish websites to a free
  domain": a free `.canva.site` address requires a design made from a **Website**
  template; *"Canva Free users can have up to five live websites on the free
  domain"*; **Mobile website and Link in Bio** templates publish *"to a canva.link
  address only"* and do not support custom domains. Publishing settings include
  *"Turn on Resize on mobile to optimise your site for mobile devices."* The
  *Designed with Canva* footer stays on Free plans. Steps: *Publish website →
  Site address → Settings → Continue → Publish.*
- **LINKS.** Canva Help Centre, "Add hyperlinks": links can be put on text,
  elements, images and videos, and a link can point to another page inside the
  same design. Canva's **Embeds** app (Apps panel → Embeds → paste the link →
  Add to design) adds online media.
- **THE COUNTDOWN IS CANVA CODE, NOT A STOCK ELEMENT.** Canva Newsroom, "Designed
  to say 'I do'": *"Build your own wedding website to share all the key details,
  including interactive elements like venue maps, a countdown timer, or a gift
  registry list, using Canva Code."* There is no countdown in Elements; the
  editor's built-in Timer is for presenting.
- **CLOSING THE RSVP.** Google Workspace Learning Center, "Publish your form and
  get responses": switch **Accepting responses** off, or choose **Set close date
  or response limit** to close it automatically at a date and time.

NOT CLAIMED: the price of any Canva plan or domain; whether Canva Code is
available on a Free account (not checked); whether any particular
Google Form renders inside an embed (viewers on this lane report embedded RSVP
forms showing "unavailable" — that is a comment, not a source, so the script
tells you to test the published site instead); what happens to Pro elements on
a free account when a site is published — not checked, so not stated.

## S01 — HOOK (~120 words)

> The RSVP form on your Canva wedding website probably belongs to you, not the
> couple — and every guest who replies is filling in your form.
>
> Google's own help page is plain about it: the responses live in the form, and
> the form's owner is the one who downloads them. So if you built the site for a
> friend, or you sell it as a template, every guest list ends up in your inbox.
>
> And the fix is not sharing the form. Sharing gives them yours. They need a
> copy — their own form, collecting their own replies.
>
> There is a free wedding website checklist in the description. Let me build
> one properly — pages, countdown, RSVP that goes to the right person, and a
> site that does not fall apart on a phone.

## S02 — THE FREE CHECKLIST, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free checklist is linked below — the publishing rules, the RSVP copy
> method, and the phone checks, on one page.
>
> Three principles.
>
> One: start from the right template. Canva has several kinds of website, and
> the one you pick at the start decides what address you can publish to later.
>
> Two: the RSVP is the only part that has to work. Photos can be imperfect. A
> reply that goes to the wrong inbox is a guest who never gets counted.
>
> Three: most guests open it on a phone, from a message, once. Build it for that
> phone first.
>
> Right template, working RSVP, built for the phone.

## S03 — THE TEMPLATE DECIDES THE ADDRESS (~160 words)

> Start with the template, because this is the decision people make by accident.
>
> Canva's help page is specific. To publish to the free canva dot site address,
> the design has to be made from a Website template. Not a presentation, not an
> invitation resized to look like a page — a Website template.
>
> There is a second family, Mobile website and Link in Bio. Those publish to a
> canva dot link address only, and they do not support a custom domain at all.
> Fine for a single page. Wrong for a wedding site you might want on your own
> name later.
>
> And one limit worth knowing now rather than after: a free Canva account can
> have up to five live websites on the free domain. That is plenty for one
> wedding. It is not plenty if you are building them for other people.
>
> So: search Website, pick one with pages and a navigation bar, and you have
> made the only decision you cannot easily undo.

## S04 — THE PAGES (~155 words)

> Now the structure. A wedding website needs four pages, and almost nobody needs
> more.
>
> Home: the names, the date, one photograph, and the countdown. Our story: a few
> lines and two or three pictures. The details: the venue, the times, the dress
> code, where to park, where to stay. And RSVP: one button and one sentence
> saying when replies close.
>
> Each page in Canva is a section of the website. Add them in that order, so the
> page order is the reading order.
>
> Then the navigation. Select the menu text, add a link, and point each item at
> its page — Canva lets a link go to another page inside the same design, which
> is exactly what a menu is.
>
> Keep the details page boring on purpose. Guests open it the night before,
> looking for one fact — the time, or the postcode. If they have to scroll past
> a love story to find it, they will message you instead.

## S05 — THE COUNTDOWN, THE PHOTOS, THE MUSIC (~150 words)

> Then the parts people actually enjoy building.
>
> The countdown — and this is not where people look for it. There is no
> countdown in Elements. Canva's own wedding page says the interactive pieces,
> the countdown included, are made with Canva Code: describe what you want, a
> countdown to the ceremony date and time, and add the result to the home page
> under the names. Check the time zone if anyone is travelling — a countdown that
> hits zero three hours early is a joke at the wrong moment.
>
> Photos. Use fewer than you want. One hero on the home page, two or three on
> the story page. Every photo is something a guest's phone has to download on a
> slow connection at a train station.
>
> Music. If you add a song, put it behind a play button and never set it to
> start on its own. Guests open this at work, on the bus, in bed beside someone
> asleep. Sound that starts by itself gets the tab closed.
>
> Everything else is decoration. The RSVP is the part that matters.

## S06 — THE TRAP, DEMONSTRATED (~190 words)

> So here is the RSVP, and I am going to make the mistake on purpose.
>
> Google Forms. A new form: name, attending yes or no, number of guests, dietary
> requirements. Copy the link. Back in Canva, select the RSVP button, add a link,
> paste it. Publish. It works. A guest taps the button, fills it in, submits.
>
> Now look where that reply went. My Google Forms, my Responses tab. Google's
> help page says it directly — form owners are the ones who download the
> responses. I made the form, so I own every reply.
>
> If this is my own wedding, that is fine. If I built it for my sister, every one
> of her guests is now in my account and she cannot see a single reply.
>
> And if I sell this as a template, it is worse. Every couple who buys it is
> sending their guests to my form. Fifty weddings, one inbox.
>
> Sharing the form does not fix it — that gives them access to mine. The fix is
> a copy. Their own form, owned by them, collecting their own replies.

## S07 — MAKING THE RSVP GO TO THE RIGHT PERSON (~160 words)

> So, the copy, two ways.
>
> If it is your own wedding, there is nothing to do — you made the form, you are
> the owner, the replies are yours.
>
> If you are building it for someone else, have them make the form in their own
> Google account, or open yours and use More, then Make a copy — that is on
> Google's own help pages. Then put the link to their copy on the button.
>
> If you are selling the template, a Google community guide describes a neater
> trick. Take the form's address and change the end of it to slash copy. Anyone
> who opens that link is asked to make their own copy, and that copy is theirs
> to edit. That is a community guide rather than an official Google page, so test
> it yourself before you rely on it.
>
> And whichever way you go, put the link on a button rather than relying on an
> embedded form. Then test it on the published site, on a phone, before a single
> guest sees it.

## S08 — THE PHONE (~150 words)

> Before you publish, the check that catches most of the problems.
>
> In the publishing settings there is a switch called Resize on mobile, and
> Canva's help says it optimises the site for phones and tablets. Turn it on.
>
> Then do not trust it. Preview the site in mobile view and go through every
> page, top to bottom.
>
> What you are looking for: text that has moved, text that has shrunk to
> nothing, and buttons too small to tap. Desktop layouts are wide and phones are
> narrow, so anything placed side by side has to stack, and the stacking is
> where things go wrong.
>
> The RSVP button gets special attention. It must be big enough to hit with a
> thumb and close enough to the top of the RSVP page that nobody has to hunt for
> it.
>
> If something looks wrong on the phone, fix it on the phone view. The phone is
> the website most of your guests will ever see.

## S09 — PUBLISHING (~150 words)

> Then publish, which is five steps.
>
> Publish website, top right. The site address — choose the free domain and,
> if you want, change the last part to the couple's names, so it reads like a
> real address instead of a string of letters. Settings, where Resize on mobile
> lives. Continue. Publish.
>
> Two things to know before you press it.
>
> On a free Canva account, a small Designed with Canva footer stays on the site.
> Canva's help says removing it needs Pro. For most weddings nobody minds; if you
> are selling the template, tell the buyer, so it is not a surprise.
>
> And the address is a promise. Once it is printed on an invitation it cannot
> change. Decide the name now, publish once, and only then put it anywhere
> permanent.
>
> Edits after that are free. Change the design, publish again, and the same
> address shows the new version.

## S10 — GETTING IT TO THE GUESTS (~145 words)

> Then getting the address to the people who need it.
>
> The simplest version is the link in a message. Copy the published address and
> send it. Test it from a phone that is not yours first — the address you see
> while you are logged in to Canva is not always the address a guest sees.
>
> The better version is a QR code on the printed invitation. Canva can make one:
> search for the QR code app, paste the published address, and place it on the
> invitation. A guest points their camera at the card and lands on the site.
>
> Make the QR code large enough to scan from arm's length, put it on a plain
> background, and scan it yourself from the printed proof, not from the screen.
> A QR code that works on a laptop and fails on paper is the most common way this
> goes wrong.

## S11 — IF YOU ARE BUILDING THESE FOR OTHER PEOPLE (~140 words)

> If you are making these for friends, clients or buyers, three things change.
>
> The RSVP form, which we have done: their copy, never yours.
>
> The five-site limit. A free account can have five live sites on the free
> domain. If you build them all in your own account, you hit the ceiling at the
> fifth wedding, and you are hosting other people's guest lists. Build it, then
> hand over the design so it lives in their account.
>
> And the photographs. Every picture on the site should be one the couple owns
> or one you have the right to use. A wedding website is public, and it outlives
> the wedding.
>
> Hand over three things with every site: the Canva design, the copy of the
> form, and a one-line note of the address. That is the whole handover.

## S12 — KEEPING THE FILE (~120 words)

> One habit, same as always.
>
> Keep the Canva design, a note of the published address, and a note of which
> Google account owns the RSVP form.
>
> That last one matters more than it sounds. Six months from now, somebody will
> ask for the final guest list, and the replies will be in whichever account
> made the form. If nobody wrote down which one, you will be searching three
> inboxes for a spreadsheet.
>
> And decide the date the RSVP closes, then let Google close it for you. In the
> form, under Accepting responses, there is Set close date — pick the deadline and
> the form stops itself. Late replies after the caterer has the numbers are worse
> than no replies.
>
> Three notes per site.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~110 words)

> Before you send the address to anyone.
>
> The design was made from a Website template. The RSVP button points at a form
> the couple owns, not you. You have filled in the form yourself from the
> published site and the reply arrived in the right account. Resize on mobile is
> on, and every page has been checked in mobile view. The RSVP button is big
> enough to tap. The form has a close date. And the QR code scans from the
> printed proof.
>
> Seven things. The second is the one that goes wrong silently — and nobody
> finds out until the guest list is missing.
