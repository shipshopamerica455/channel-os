# How to Make a Newsletter in Canva

## Facts verified 2026-10-10

- **THE TRAP — hyperlinks survive ONE download format, and Canva's own
  font fix destroys them.** Canva Help Centre, "Add hyperlinks": *"Hyperlinks
  will only work if you download your design as a PDF (except flattened
  PDFs)"*, and *"Image formats like JPG and PNG do not support hyperlinks
  regardless of the image source"* — the page also says links will not work
  *"on PNGs, JPGs, MP4s, etc., or when you post your design on social media"*.
  Links can be put on **text, elements, images and videos**, and a link can
  point at another page inside the same design.
- **THE SECOND HALF OF THE TRAP — flattening.** Canva Help Centre, "Download
  designs as flattened PDF": flattening *"converts your design with multiple
  layers into one"* and *"ensures fonts display accurately and secures your
  design"*, *"preventing editing in software like Adobe Acrobat"*. It *"can
  increase file size"*. So the fix Canva offers for the most common newsletter
  complaint — fonts rendering wrong on somebody else's machine — is also the
  one download setting that silently kills every link in the file. Neither the
  export dialog nor the file tells you.
- **THE EXPORT — ninety six versus three hundred.** Canva Help Centre, "Choose
  the right download file type": **PDF Standard** is *"for illustrations, text,
  and graphics with a resolution of 96 dpi"*; **PDF Print** is *"for printing
  with a resolution of 300 dpi, with bleed, crop marks, and color profile (RGB
  or CMYK) selection option"*. A newsletter that gets printed and handed out —
  which is most school and community newsletters — needs the second one.

NOT CLAIMED: how any email client renders or clips a message, at what size
Gmail truncates, what any email platform charges, open rates, click rates or
deliverability. Gmail's own sending-guidelines page was checked in this session
and does not carry a clipping figure, so no number is stated — the script sends
you to send yourself a test instead.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> Every link in your Canva newsletter is probably dead, and nothing told you.
>
> Canva's own help page says it plainly. Hyperlinks only work if you download
> as a P D F — and not a flattened one. Image formats do not support hyperlinks
> at all, regardless of the image source. So if you exported a J P G and pasted
> it into an email, which is what most people do, every link in it is a
> picture of a link.
>
> And here is the part that catches careful people. If your fonts came out
> wrong and you ticked flatten to fix them — Canva's own suggestion — you just
> killed every link in the file a second time.
>
> There is a free newsletter export card in the description. Let me build one
> properly.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free card is linked below — which download format keeps your links, the
> flatten warning, and the ninety six versus three hundred D P I split, on one
> page.
>
> Three principles.
>
> One: decide how it will be read before you design a single box. Printed,
> attached, or opened on a phone. They are three different documents.
>
> Two: a newsletter is not a poster. It is a set of blocks in a grid, and the
> grid is what makes it readable when there are nine items on it.
>
> Three: the links are the point. Everything else is decoration around the one
> thing you want somebody to click.
>
> Decide the destination, build on a grid, protect the links.

## S03 — THE SIZE, AND HOW MANY PAGES (~155 words)

> Start with the page, and start with the question most people skip.
>
> If it is going to be printed or attached as a P D F, use the paper size your
> readers actually use. A four in most of the world, letter in the United
> States. Canva has both under Documents.
>
> If it is only ever going to be read on a screen, you can go longer and
> narrower, but stay sensible — a two thousand pixel tall page is a scroll, not
> a newsletter.
>
> Pages. Two is a newsletter. Four is a newsletter. Eight is a magazine that
> nobody will finish, and if you have eight pages of news you have a website.
>
> And set it up as multiple pages inside one design, not one design per page.
> That matters at export: one design downloads as one multi-page P D F, and
> four separate designs download as four files that somebody has to open in
> order.

## S04 — THE GRID AND THE MASTHEAD (~155 words)

> Now the structure, and this is the bit that separates a newsletter from a
> flyer with a lot of writing on it.
>
> Turn on rulers and guides — File, Settings, show rulers and guides — and put
> in columns. Two or three. Three is tighter and looks more like a newspaper;
> two is easier to read on a phone. Every block you place from now on starts
> and ends on a column edge. That one rule does most of the work.
>
> The masthead is the band across the top: the name of the newsletter, the
> issue, and the date. Keep it identical every single issue. It is the thing
> that makes issue nine recognisable as the same publication as issue one.
>
> Underneath it, leave a strip for one headline and one image. That is the
> only part of your newsletter most people will ever look at, so give it the
> room it deserves rather than squeezing it.

## S05 — THE BLOCKS (~150 words)

> Then the body, built as blocks, not as a wall.
>
> One item per block. A heading, two or three sentences, and a link. If an item
> needs five paragraphs, it is not a newsletter item — it is a page on your
> site that the newsletter links to.
>
> Use Canva's text styles rather than resizing by hand. Set a heading size, a
> body size and a caption size once, and reuse them. Three sizes, the same way
> round every time.
>
> Put a rule or a change of background colour between blocks. Not a border on
> every box — that makes it look like a form. A line, or a tinted panel behind
> every other item.
>
> And leave white space you think is too much. A newsletter with six items and
> air beats one with eleven items crammed edge to edge, every time, because the
> second one does not get read at all.

## S06 — THE TRAP, DEMONSTRATED (~190 words)

> Now the links, and I am going to break this on purpose so you can see it.
>
> Here is a headline with a link on it. Select the text, the link button,
> paste the U R L. Canva will do this on text, on elements, on images and on
> videos, and you can also link to another page inside the same design. In the
> editor it works. You can click it right now.
>
> Download. J P G. Open it. Click the headline — nothing. There is no link in
> it, there never was, and nothing in that export dialog warned me. Canva's
> help page says it outright: image formats do not support hyperlinks
> regardless of the image source.
>
> So: P D F instead. Download, P D F Standard. Open it, click the headline — it
> works.
>
> Now watch this. Same P D F, and I tick flatten, because my fonts looked wrong
> on somebody else's computer and flatten is what Canva recommends for that.
> Download. Click the headline. Dead.
>
> Flattening merges every layer into one image. Your links were layers. The
> fix for the fonts is the thing that kills the links, and nothing says so.

## S07 — NINETY SIX OR THREE HUNDRED (~155 words)

> The second thing the export dialog does quietly.
>
> There are two P D F options and they are not the same file. Canva's help
> page: P D F Standard is ninety six D P I, for illustrations, text and
> graphics. P D F Print is three hundred D P I, with bleed, crop marks and a
> colour profile option.
>
> Ninety six is a screen resolution. If your newsletter is emailed as an
> attachment or read on a laptop, Standard is correct and the file is smaller,
> which matters when you are attaching it.
>
> But if anybody is going to print it — and school newsletters, church
> newsletters and community newsletters all get printed — Standard will come
> out soft and slightly muddy, and there is no fixing it afterwards.
>
> So the honest answer is that you often need both. Export Standard for the
> email, export Print for whoever is running it off. Same design, two
> downloads, thirty seconds.

## S08 — WRITING IT SO IT GETS READ (~145 words)

> A word about the words, because the design is not what makes a newsletter
> fail.
>
> Front-load every item. The first six words of a block have to say what it is
> about — not set the scene, not greet anybody. People scan a newsletter the
> way they scan a notice board.
>
> Headings are not titles. "Sports Day" is a title. "Sports Day moved to the
> twelfth" is a heading, and it is the one that gets read.
>
> Dates, times and places go in the block, not just in the link. Half your
> readers will never click.
>
> And cut your own editorial. The welcome paragraph from the head, the
> chairman, the founder — nobody reads it, and it occupies the single most
> valuable rectangle on the page. If it has to exist, put it at the bottom.

## S09 — WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE ON A PHONE (~150 words)

> Before you export, the check almost nobody does.
>
> Most newsletters are opened on a phone. A P D F on a phone is the whole page,
> zoomed out to fit — so your A four page becomes about the size of a playing
> card before anybody pinches to zoom.
>
> So zoom your design out until it is roughly that size and look at it.
>
> What should survive: the masthead, the lead headline, and the shape of the
> blocks. If your body text has turned into grey stripes, that is fine — that
> is what body text does. If your headlines have also turned into grey stripes,
> they are too small, and nobody will zoom in to find out what they say.
>
> And check the first screen separately. On a phone that top third is the whole
> newsletter until somebody decides to scroll.

## S10 — THE EXPORT DECISION (~145 words)

> So, the export, as a decision tree rather than a habit.
>
> Does it contain links you need people to click? Then it is a P D F, and
> flatten stays off. That is not a preference, it is the only combination that
> works.
>
> Is it being printed? P D F Print, three hundred D P I.
>
> Is it being attached to an email and only read on screens? P D F Standard,
> smaller file.
>
> Does it genuinely need to be an image — because you are posting it as a
> social post or embedding it somewhere that will not take a P D F? Then accept
> that the links are gone, and put the one U R L that matters in the post text
> as plain typed characters.
>
> And if your fonts render wrong somewhere, do not reach for flatten. Embed the
> message instead: export it twice and check it on the machine that was wrong.

## S11 — SENDING IT (~140 words)

> Then getting it to people, and I am going to be careful here because I have
> not checked any email platform's behaviour today, so I am not going to tell
> you how one renders.
>
> What I will tell you is the test. Send it to yourself. Open it on your phone,
> not your laptop. Click two links — one near the top, one near the bottom.
>
> That five-minute test catches the dead links, the font substitution, and the
> attachment that is too big, which are the three things that actually go
> wrong.
>
> And do it from the account you will really send from, to an address on a
> different provider from your own. Sending yourself a message inside the same
> provider proves less than you think.
>
> Test, then send. Never the other way round.

## S12 — MAKE IT A TEMPLATE (~125 words)

> One habit, and for a newsletter it pays more than for anything else, because
> a newsletter is the one thing you make again next month.
>
> Once issue one is right, strip it. Delete the content, keep the masthead,
> the grid, the text styles and the block structure. Save that as its own
> design and call it the template.
>
> Next month you duplicate the template, not last month's issue. Duplicating
> last month's issue is how a newsletter slowly fills with things nobody
> removed — a dead link, a sponsor who left, a date from March.
>
> And keep a plain text note of the sizes, the fonts and the export settings
> you landed on, so issue twelve still looks like issue one.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~115 words)

> Before you send it.
>
> Every link has been clicked in the exported file, not in the editor. Flatten
> is off. You know whether this copy is the ninety six or the three hundred
> one, and which one each person is getting. The masthead is identical to last
> issue. Every block has a date, a time and a place in it, not only in the
> link. It still reads at phone size, and the headlines are not grey stripes.
> And it came out of the template, not out of last month's issue.
>
> Seven things. The first is the one that actually goes wrong, and it goes
> wrong silently.
