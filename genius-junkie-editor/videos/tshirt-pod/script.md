# How to Design T-Shirts in Canva for Print on Demand

## Facts verified 2026-10-05

- **THE TRAP — Canva's licence forbids PRO content on t-shirts.** Content
  Licence Agreement, in the list of things you may NOT do with Pro Content:
  *"Use or display the Content on a standalone basis on websites or other
  venues designed to induce or involving the sale, license or other
  distribution of 'on demand' products, including postcards, mugs, **t-shirts**,
  posters and other items."*
  Source: Canva Content Licence Agreement.
- **FREE content is explicitly allowed**, same agreement: free content may be
  used for *"prints, posters (i.e. hardcopies) and other reproductions for
  personal or promotional purposes, **resale**, license or other distribution"*.
  So the whole licence turns on one small crown icon — and Canva mixes free and
  Pro results in the same element search.
- **THE IRONY — transparent PNG is Pro-only.** Canva Help Centre: downloading
  with a transparent background is *"only available to Canva Pro, Canva Teams,
  Canva Business, Canva Education, and Canva for Nonprofits plans"*. So you need
  Pro for the export, and you must not use Pro ELEMENTS in the design. Both are
  true at once and nobody says so.
- **Print file size, from Printful's own guidance:** maximum t-shirt print area
  **12 × 16 inches**; files at **150–300 DPI**, which is roughly
  **3,600–4,800 px** across for a 12–16 inch print; PNG or JPEG.
  Source: Printful, "How to prepare the perfect print file".

NOT CLAIMED: what any POD company charges, what any shirt costs, what margin to
expect, or which provider is best. None was checked against a provider's own
pricing page in this session. Where a number matters the script says "check
your provider's own spec page", never a figure.

Other providers differ — the script names Printful's figures as an example and
says so out loud, rather than presenting them as universal.

## S01 — HOOK (~120 words)

> Canva's licence says you cannot put Pro elements on a t-shirt you sell. Their
> words, in the list of things you may not do: use the content on venues
> involving the sale of on demand products, including mugs, t-shirts and
> posters. Free elements are fine — the same agreement allows resale of prints
> and reproductions. So the entire licence turns on one small crown icon, and
> Canva mixes free and Pro results in the same search. And here is the part
> that makes it properly annoying: the transparent P N G that print on demand
> requires is itself a Pro feature. You need Pro for the export and you must not
> use Pro in the design. There is a free licence and size card in the
> description. Now let me build a shirt that is actually safe to sell.

## S02 — THE FREE CARD, AND THREE PRINCIPLES (~115 words)

> The free print on demand card is linked below — the licence line, the sizes,
> and the export settings on one page.
>
> Three principles. One: every element on that shirt has to be free content, or
> yours, and you check that per element, not per design. Two: the file is
> enormous compared to what you are used to — this is not a social post. Three:
> the background has to actually be transparent, and "looks white" is not
> transparent.
>
> And a framing before we start, because it changes what you make. Print on
> demand is not really a design business. It is a research business with a
> design step on the end. The people doing well at it are not better
> illustrators than you — they are far more specific about who the shirt is for.

## S03 — THE CANVAS SIZE NOBODY USES (~155 words)

> Start with the size, because Canva's own t-shirt template will let you down.
>
> Search t-shirt in Canva and you get a template sized for a picture of a
> t-shirt. That is a mockup. It is not a print file.
>
> A print file is just the artwork, on nothing, at the size it will be printed.
>
> Printful — and I am naming them as an example, other providers differ — give a
> maximum print area of twelve by sixteen inches, and ask for a hundred and
> fifty to three hundred D P I. That works out at roughly three thousand six
> hundred to four thousand eight hundred pixels across.
>
> So: custom size, forty five hundred by fifty four hundred pixels. That is a
> comfortable middle, it fits inside a twelve by sixteen area at three hundred
> D P I, and it is what most people building for print on demand use.
>
> Check your own provider's spec page before you commit to a number.

## S04 — DESIGNING FOR A SHIRT, NOT A SCREEN (~150 words)

> Now the design itself, and shirts have rules that screens do not.
>
> One: no thin lines. A screen can show you a hairline. Fabric cannot. Anything
> under about two or three millimetres at final print size will break up or
> disappear, especially on a dark shirt.
>
> Two: no subtle gradients into the background. Printing lays ink on cloth; a
> gradient that fades to nothing often prints as a visible edge where the ink
> stops.
>
> Three: contrast is everything, and you do not know the shirt colour. Design it
> to work on both black and white, or commit to one and say so in the listing.
>
> And keep it simple. The designs that sell are usually a phrase and one shape.
> Not because simple is better art — because it is legible from across a room,
> and that is what a t-shirt does.

## S05 — THE ELEMENTS PANEL, CAREFULLY (~165 words)

> Elements. And this is where the licence problem actually happens, so slow
> down here.
>
> Search for anything — let us say a mountain. The results come back mixed. Some
> are free. Some are Pro, and the only difference you can see is a small crown
> in the corner of the thumbnail.
>
> There is a filter. Click the filter icon, and set price to Free. Now
> everything on screen is free content, and free content is the stuff the
> licence allows you to sell on a shirt.
>
> Use that filter every single time. Not once at the start of the session —
> every search, because it does not always persist.
>
> And a word on what free means here. Free content can be used by anyone, which
> means other sellers have the same mountain. If you want something nobody else
> has, you have to draw it, or buy it somewhere with a commercial licence that
> says merchandise in writing.

## S06 — THE TRAP: THE CROWN ICON (~185 words)

> Now the thing that can close your shop.
>
> Canva's Content Licence Agreement has a list of things you may not do with Pro
> content. One of them, word for word: use or display the content on a
> standalone basis on websites or other venues designed to induce or involving
> the sale, license or other distribution of on demand products, including
> postcards, mugs, t-shirts, posters and other items.
>
> T-shirts. Named. In the prohibited list.
>
> And the same agreement is just as clear the other way about free content — it
> allows prints and other reproductions for resale.
>
> So one element with a crown on it turns a legal product into a licence
> violation. Not a style problem. A takedown, or worse, depending on who
> notices.
>
> Watch what that looks like. Here is my design. Here is the element I dragged
> in twenty minutes ago. Small crown, bottom corner of the thumbnail in the
> panel — which I cannot see at all now that it is on the canvas.
>
> That is the whole trap. It is invisible after you place it.

## S07 — HOW TO ACTUALLY CHECK (~150 words)

> So how do you check a finished design?
>
> There is no button that says "is this safe to sell". What there is, is the
> download dialog.
>
> Hit Share, Download. If your design contains Pro content and you are on a free
> plan, Canva tells you — it will show you what needs a licence before it lets
> you export.
>
> But if you are a Pro subscriber it will not tell you anything, because for
> every other purpose you are fully entitled to use it. Your subscription is not
> the issue. The licence for this specific use is.
>
> So the only reliable method is discipline at the point of placing: filter to
> free, every search, every time. And if you are unsure about an element that is
> already in a design, delete it and place it again from a filtered search.
>
> Rebuilding is cheaper than a takedown.

## S08 — THE TRANSPARENT EXPORT, AND ITS IRONY (~160 words)

> The export. Download, P N G. And then the setting that matters: transparent
> background.
>
> Your artwork has to sit on nothing. If it exports with a white rectangle
> behind it, that rectangle prints — a white box on a black shirt.
>
> Canva's help pages are explicit that this one is paid: downloading with a
> transparent background is only available to Pro, Teams, Business, Education
> and Nonprofits.
>
> Which produces a genuinely silly situation. You need Pro to export a file that
> print on demand will accept, and you must not use Pro elements in the design
> you are exporting. Both true at the same time.
>
> So a free plan can design a legal shirt and cannot export it properly. A Pro
> plan can export it properly and can very easily make it illegal. Knowing that
> is most of the job.
>
> Also tick the size up to two times if the option is there — more pixels is the
> cheap win.

## S09 — CHECKING THE FILE BEFORE YOU UPLOAD (~145 words)

> Before it goes anywhere near a product page, three checks on the actual file.
>
> One: open the P N G and put it on a dark background. Your computer's preview
> will usually show transparency as a chequerboard. If you see white where you
> expected nothing, the toggle did not take.
>
> Two: check the pixel dimensions. Right click, get info, or properties. It
> should be the thousands, not the hundreds. If it says eight hundred by eight
> hundred you exported the mockup template by mistake.
>
> Three: zoom to one hundred percent and look at your thinnest line. That is the
> part that disappears on fabric.
>
> Then upload it to your provider's design tool and look at their preview with
> the print area showing. That preview is the first honest look you get.

## S10 — THE MOCKUP IS A SEPARATE JOB (~140 words)

> Your listing photo is not your print file, and this trips people constantly.
>
> The print file is artwork on transparency at four and a half thousand pixels.
> The listing photo is a picture of a person wearing the shirt.
>
> Most print on demand providers generate mockups for you, free, from the design
> you uploaded. Use those first — they are accurate, because they are generated
> from your actual placement.
>
> Canva is good for the second kind of listing image: the lifestyle one, the
> comparison grid, the one with text on it. Build those at listing dimensions,
> not print dimensions.
>
> Two different files, two different sizes, two different jobs. Keep them in
> separate folders or you will upload the wrong one.

## S11 — WHAT ACTUALLY SELLS (~145 words)

> A word on the designs themselves, because the technical side is the easy part.
>
> The shirts that sell are almost never beautiful. They are specific. A phrase
> that one group of people recognises and everyone else does not.
>
> Which means the research is not "what looks good". It is "which group am I
> making this for, and what do they say to each other".
>
> Niche down further than feels sensible. Not "nurses" — night shift nurses.
> Not "dog owners" — people whose greyhound has taken the sofa.
>
> And be careful with phrases you have seen elsewhere. Popular slogans are often
> trademarked, including ones that feel like common speech. If a phrase is doing
> well on a thousand listings, that is a reason to check it, not a reason to
> copy it.

## S12 — THE FOLDER THAT SAVES YOU LATER (~130 words)

> One habit, and it costs you nothing today.
>
> For every design, keep the Canva file, the exported P N G, and a plain text
> note of where each element came from. Free from Canva elements. Drawn by me.
> Bought from this place, this licence.
>
> You will not need it ninety nine times out of a hundred. The hundredth time is
> somebody asking you to prove you had the right to sell it, and the answer
> "I think it was free" is not an answer.
>
> It is a two line note per design. Write it when you place the element, not
> when someone asks.

## S13 — THE CHECKS (~110 words)

> Before you upload.
>
> Every element came from a search filtered to Free, or you made it. The file is
> P N G with transparency actually on, checked against a dark background. The
> dimensions are in the thousands and match your provider's print area. Your
> thinnest line survives at one hundred percent zoom. The print file and the
> listing images are in different folders. And you have a note of where every
> element came from.
>
> Six things. The first one is the one that matters legally, and it is the one
> that cannot be fixed after the fact.

## S14 — CLOSE (~60 words)

> The licence and size card is free and linked below, and if you want
> ready-made illustration assets you can legally put on a shirt, the sticker and
> illustration kit is in the description.
>
> Go and make something a very specific group of people will recognise.
