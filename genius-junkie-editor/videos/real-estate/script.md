# Marketing a property in Canva — the photo you are not licensed to use

## Facts verified 2026-09-01 — Canva's own Content License Agreement

**THE TRAP — Canva does not give you the right to put a stock person in your
property marketing.**

From Canva's Content License Agreement, verbatim:

> "Releases are generally not obtained for Free Content, and some jurisdictions
> provide legal protection against a person's image, likeness or property being
> used for commercial purposes when they have not provided a release."

Read the middle of that sentence again: **image, likeness or property.** Canva
names property explicitly, and property marketing is unambiguously commercial
use. The agreement then puts the duty on the user to work out whether a release
is needed and to obtain it themselves — Canva is not doing it for you.

The same agreement also prohibits using content "in a way that places any person
depicted in the content in a bad light", and prohibits using Branded Content
"in any way that implies endorsement, affiliation, or sponsorship".

**Why this matters for this audience specifically.** Estate agents reach for the
"happy couple holding keys" stock photo constantly — on flyers, window cards,
Instagram, and worst of all in fake testimonial cards. A stock headshot beside
the words "great service, sold in two weeks" is presenting a model as a genuine
customer endorsing the business. That is the exact thing the agreement warns
about, and it is also the thing a regulator would look at hardest.

**The honest, practical position — all of it goes in the video:**
1. **Use your own photographs of the actual property.** Always. Non-negotiable.
2. **Do not put stock people in property marketing.** Not on the flyer, not in
   a testimonial card, not as "your agent". If you need a person, it is you —
   photograph yourself.
3. If you genuinely need stock people for something non-property (a recruitment
   post, say), understand that the free tier is where the release problem is
   sharpest, and the duty to check sits with you.
4. **This is not legal advice** and the video says so once, plainly. Agents are
   regulated differently in every market. The instruction is: read the clause,
   then ask your broker or compliance lead. That single line protects the
   channel and is genuinely the right advice.

**THE SECOND TRAP — the interior shot that is not the interior.** Every portal
expects the photographs to be of the property being sold. Dropping a Canva stock
kitchen into a listing because the real one photographs badly is a
misrepresentation problem, not a design shortcut. Say it plainly, once, and move
on — it is the shortest section in the video.

**What this video is NOT.** It is not the lookbook (bleed and colour profile —
one sentence only, then move on) and it is not the media kit. The subject is a
listing kit: window card, flyer, brochure, and the social set cut from the same
design.

## S02 — TWO THINGS THAT DECIDE EVERYTHING ELSE (~130 words)

> Two ideas, and every decision after this follows from them. The first: your
> property marketing is commercial use. Not personal, not editorial — you are
> using an image to help sell a thing for money. That is the category with the

> in. The second: the photographs of the property must be photographs of the

## S03 — WHAT A LISTING KIT ACTUALLY CONTAINS (~135 words)

> Before we design anything, let us agree what we are making, because "some
> marketing for a house" is how you end up with fourteen files and no system.
> A listing kit is four things. A window card — the one in the office window,
> usually A four portrait, read from about a metre away. A property flyer —
> the handout at the viewing, same size, but read in the hand. A brochure for
> the bigger instructions, which is the flyer with more pages. And a social
> set — three or four posts cut from the same design so the listing looks like
> one thing across every channel. Build them in that order, because the window
> card forces you to decide what actually matters, and everything else inherits
> that decision.

[VISUAL: the four artefacts appear as labelled cards in a row, then arrange into
a numbered order with arrows.]

## S04 — SET IT UP ONCE (~135 words)

> Start with the window card and start it at the right size, because changing
> the page size later reflows every element you have placed. A four portrait —
> two hundred and ten by two hundred and ninety-seven millimetres — is the
> right default for the UK and most of Europe; US Letter portrait is the
> equivalent everywhere else. Turn on rulers and drag a guide about fifteen
> millimetres in from every edge, because a window card sits behind glass in a
> frame and frames eat the outside edge. Then set your two brand colours and
> one font pair in the Brand section if you have it, or just decide on them now
> and write them down. Every piece in the kit uses those and nothing else.

[VISUAL: Canva chrome, custom size A4 portrait; rulers dragging out to a 15 mm
margin; a brand colour pair and font pair being locked in.]

## S05 — THE HIERARCHY THAT SELLS (~190 words)

> Now the part that makes a flyer work. Somebody walking past your window gives
> it about three seconds, so you get three pieces of information, in this
> order. The price, the location, and one photograph that makes them stop.
> That is it. Put the price in the largest type on the page — bigger than you
> are comfortable with, big enough to read from the pavement. Put the location
> underneath it, smaller but still large. And give the hero photograph the top
> two thirds of the page, because a small photograph of a beautiful house looks
> like a big photograph of nothing.
>
> Everything else — the bedroom count, the tenure, the square metres, your
> phone number — is a second tier, and it belongs in a band across the bottom
> in type you would call too small. It is not too small. Nobody reads the
> details until the price and the picture have already done their job. If you
> take one layout idea from this video, take that: three things large, the
> rest deliberately quiet.
>
> One more placement decision people get wrong: the floor plan. It does not
> belong on the window card at all — it is unreadable at that size and it steals
> room from the photograph. Put it on the back of the flyer, or on its own page
> in the brochure, where somebody is holding it and can actually study it. A
> floor plan is the piece buyers spend the longest looking at once they are
> interested, and almost no time on before they are.

[VISUAL: flyer builds live — hero photo fills two thirds; price lands huge;
location under it; then the detail band populates in small type. Then a
side-by-side: everything-the-same-size version vs the hierarchy version, seen
from a simulated distance, blurred. The hierarchy one still reads.]

## S06 — THE PHOTOGRAPH YOU ARE NOT LICENSED TO USE (~215 words)

> So — the couple with the keys. Here is what Canva's own Content License
> Agreement says, and I would encourage you to go and read the whole thing
> rather than take my word for it. Quote: "Releases are generally not obtained
> for Free Content, and some jurisdictions provide legal protection against a
> person's image, likeness or property being used for commercial purposes when
> they have not provided a release."
>
> Read the middle of that again. Image, likeness, or property. Canva names
> property explicitly. And a release, in this context, is simply the piece of
> paper in which the person in the photograph agrees to their face being used
> to sell things. Canva is telling you that for free content, that paper
> generally does not exist — and the same agreement puts the job of working out
> whether you need one, and getting it, on you.
>
> So here is the rule, and it is simpler than the paragraph that produced it.
> No stock people anywhere in property marketing. Not on the flyer. Not on the
> window card. And absolutely never on a testimonial card, because a stock
> headshot next to the words "sold in two weeks, wonderful service" is
> presenting a model as a real client endorsing your business.

[VISUAL: the licence clause on screen, source visible, "image, likeness or
property" highlighted word by word as spoken. Then three refusals stamp on in
sequence over real examples: flyer ✕, window card ✕, testimonial card ✕✕. Then
one green tick: a photograph of the agent, taken by the agent.]

## S07 — WHAT TO USE INSTEAD (~150 words)

> The replacement is not complicated and it is usually better marketing anyway.
> If you need a person in the picture, the person is you. Photograph yourself,
> once, properly, and use that everywhere — buyers respond to a named human
> being far more than to a model they have half-seen on three other adverts.
> If you need the property to look its best, that is lighting and tidying and
> a wide lens, not a substitution.
>
> And if the photographs you have are genuinely poor, the answer is to go back,
> not to compensate in the design. Shoot on a bright but overcast day so the
> windows are not blown out, turn every light on anyway, and take the room from
> its corner rather than its doorway — corners make rooms look their real size.
> Twenty minutes of reshooting beats an hour trying to rescue a dark photograph
> with filters, and the listing looks honest, which is the thing you are
> actually selling.
>
> And I want to say this once, clearly, because I am not a lawyer and you are
> regulated in ways that differ by market: read that clause yourself, then ask
> your broker or your compliance lead what applies where you work. What I am
> giving you is the sentence to go and ask about. That is worth more than a
> confident answer from someone who does not know your jurisdiction.

[VISUAL: a stock model photo dissolves into a real agent headshot in the same
frame position. Then a short "ask your broker" card, quiet and plain.]

## S08 — YOUR NAME ON EVERY PIECE (~135 words)

> Every item in the kit carries the same three things in the same place: your
> name, your agency, and whatever your market requires you to display — a
> licence number, a registration line, the regulator's wording. Put them in the
> bottom band, set them once, and then never think about them again. This is
> exactly the kind of detail that gets forgotten on the fourth flyer of a busy
> week, and it is the kind that a compliance review picks up immediately.
>
> Group those elements together in Canva and lock the group. A locked group
> cannot be dragged out of place by accident when you are working quickly,
> which is the actual failure mode — nobody deletes their licence number on
> purpose.

[VISUAL: the bottom compliance band being built, then grouped, then the padlock
clicking shut. A quick demo: dragging wildly at the page, and the locked band
not moving.]

## S09 — THREE EXPORTS, ONE DESIGN (~155 words)

> The same design leaves in three different ways and getting this wrong is the
> most common reason a flyer looks soft. For the printed window card and the
> handouts, download as PDF Print, and if it is going to a print shop rather
> than your office printer, tick crop marks and bleed and run any background
> colour past the edge of the page — we covered why in the lookbook video. For
> the property portals, they usually want a JPEG or a PDF at a specific size,
> so check the portal's own spec sheet before you export; every one of them
> publishes it. And for social, do not export the A four and hope — make the
> social set properly, which is the next thing we are doing.
>
> Three exports, one design, in about ninety seconds once you have done it
> twice.

[VISUAL: the download panel three times, each with different settings, as three
labelled branches. Files landing in a folder.]

## S10 — THE SOCIAL SET, CUT FROM THE SAME DESIGN (~135 words)

> Duplicate the design, resize the copy to a square, and rebuild the same three
> pieces of information for a phone: price, location, hero photo. Not a shrunk
> flyer — a rebuilt one, because the detail band that works at arm's length is
> invisible at thumbnail size and just makes the post look busy.
>
> Then make two more from the same file: one photograph with the price as the
> only text on it, and one that is just the address and the viewing time. Three
> posts, one listing, all visibly the same family. That consistency is the
> entire point — a buyer scrolling should recognise your listings before they
> read the agency name.

[VISUAL: A4 design duplicated, resized to square; the detail band deleted;
elements re-laid for phone. Then the three posts side by side in a feed mock.]

## S11 — WHY THIS ONE PAYS FOR ITSELF (~145 words)

> Here is what makes a listing kit worth the afternoon. Every property you take
> on from now on is the same four files with different photographs and a
> different price. The layout decisions — the hierarchy, the margins, the
> compliance band — are made once and never again. A new instruction becomes
> twenty minutes of swapping photographs instead of an evening of designing.
>
> And it compounds in a second way, which is that your listings start to look
> like a set. Any agent can produce one nice flyer. Very few produce forty that
> obviously belong together, and that consistency is what a vendor is actually
> buying when they choose between two agencies whose fees are identical. The
> kit is not really about design. It is about never having to make the same
> decision twice while you are busy.

[VISUAL: one template forks into six finished listings with different photos and
prices, arranged as a grid that reads as one brand.]
