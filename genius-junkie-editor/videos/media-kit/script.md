# How to Make a Media Kit for Sponsorships in Canva (And How to Price Them)

Target ~8.8 min · 14 sections · audience: creators who have started getting
sponsor emails and have no idea what to charge. Slate 2 row #3, bumped ahead of
rows 1–2 as an editorial call. Two DESIGNED AD BREAKS after S05 and S08.
Product: Media Kit + Rate Card (£4.99/Club). Voice: Kokoro bm_fable.

**Facts verified 12 Aug 2026:**
- The standard sponsorship formula is **(average views ÷ 1,000) × CPM**, i.e.
  priced on **delivered views**, not on subscribers.
- General creator CPM benchmark for 2026 is roughly **fifteen to twenty-five
  dollars**, with big variation by niche (tech/SaaS runs higher, gaming lower,
  finance highest). The script gives the general band and says it varies rather
  than quoting a niche table as fact.
- **Format multipliers**: dedicated video **1.3–1.5×**, short integration
  **0.7–0.9×**, Shorts **0.5×**.
- **Geography moves the price 2–3×**: a US/UK-weighted audience is worth two to
  three times a developing-market audience *in the same niche*. This is the
  trap — it is the number most creators leave out of the kit entirely.
- ⚠️ Not asserted: any specific niche CPM as a hard number, or that any given
  brand pays a given rate. Those vary and the script says so.

## S01 — HOOK (~125 words)

> A media kit is not a design problem. It looks like one, which is why every
> tutorial about it is really a tutorial about picking fonts, and why so many
> creators end up with a beautiful document that gets them lowballed anyway. A
> media kit is a pricing document. Its only job is to make a specific number
> feel obviously fair. And the number almost everybody puts at the top of theirs
> — in the biggest type, right under their name — is the one number a sponsor
> is not buying. In this video we build the whole thing: the five numbers you
> actually pull, the three pages they go on, and the arithmetic that turns them
> into a price you can say out loud without flinching. Here's what we're
> building.

## S02 — WHAT A SPONSOR IS ACTUALLY BUYING (~145 words)

> Start here, because it changes every decision after it. A sponsor is not
> buying your subscriber count. They are buying delivered views — how many
> humans will see the thing they paid for. Those two numbers come apart much
> further than people expect. A channel with a hundred thousand subscribers
> getting two thousand views a video is worth less to a sponsor than a channel
> with eight thousand subscribers getting five thousand views a video, and the
> second one usually charges less because the subscriber number embarrassed
> them. So your kit answers four questions, in this order. How many people will
> see it. Who are they and where are they. What do they do after they watch.
> And what does it cost. Everything else — your logo, your bio, your brand
> colours — is packaging around those four answers.

[VISUAL: two channel cards side by side, 100K subs / 2K views vs 8K subs / 5K
views; the "worth more" badge lands on the smaller one. Then the four questions
stack as numbered cards.]

## S03 — THE FIVE NUMBERS (~150 words)

> Open YouTube Studio, Analytics, and set the date range to the last twenty
> eight days. You need exactly five things. One: views in that window. Two:
> watch time in hours, because it shows people stay. Three: subscribers gained,
> which shows the channel is alive rather than coasting. Four: your traffic
> sources — specifically what percentage arrives from search, because search
> traffic keeps arriving for years and feed traffic does not. And five, the one
> almost nobody includes: your top countries, under the Audience tab. Write all
> five down before you open Canva, because the design decisions come from the
> numbers, not the other way round. And one warning while you're in there: if
> you have ever run ads to your videos, exclude that period or your view count
> is describing money you spent, not an audience you have.

[VISUAL: Studio analytics mock; each of the five numbers highlights in turn and
flies into a notepad; the ads warning as a struck-through inflated number.]

## S04 — CANVAS SETUP (~135 words)

> Now build it. Create a design, custom size, A4 portrait — two hundred and ten
> by two hundred and ninety seven millimetres. Three pages. Not eight. A media
> kit is read by someone with forty other emails open, and every page past
> three is a page they will not reach. Margins: two centimetres, dragged in as
> guides before anything else goes down. Then lock two fonts and two colours,
> and here is the specific version of that advice — use your actual channel
> colours, the ones on your thumbnails. A sponsor who has just watched one of
> your videos should recognise the document as belonging to the same person.
> That sounds cosmetic. It is actually the cheapest credibility signal in the
> whole file.

[VISUAL: custom size dialog typing 210 × 297; three page thumbnails appear and
a fourth gets binned; guides drop; a thumbnail's colours get eyedropped into the
brand strip.]

## S05 — PAGE ONE, THE COVER (~185 words)

> Page one, and it does three jobs. Top left: your channel name, your handle,
> and one line saying what the channel is. Not your life story. "Canva and
> design tutorials" is enough — they already clicked, they know roughly.
> Then the headline. Mine says "media kit for sponsors", which sounds boring,
> and boring is correct, because this document is going to be forwarded to
> somebody's manager and it needs to survive that. Then the part that does the
> actual work: four numbers in four cards, across the page. Subscribers, views
> per month, watch time per month, and percentage from search. Big type,
> generous space, nothing else competing. Four cards is the right number. Six
> looks like you are hiding a weak one among the strong ones, and a sponsor who
> reads media kits all day will notice. And underneath them, one short
> paragraph that frames who this audience is. Not a boast — a fact they can act
> on. We'll come back to that paragraph, because for most creators it is the
> single most valuable sentence in the file.

## S06 — THE ARITHMETIC (~195 words)

> Now the part every other media kit tutorial skips, which is the part you
> actually needed. Sponsorship pricing has a formula. Take your average
> delivered views, divide by one thousand, and multiply by a CPM — a cost per
> thousand. For most creators in twenty twenty six that CPM sits somewhere
> around fifteen to twenty five dollars, though it swings hard by subject:
> software and finance run higher, entertainment runs lower. Then multiply by
> the format. A dedicated video is about one point three to one point five
> times. A short integration inside a bigger video is about zero point seven to
> zero point nine. A Short is about half. So: five thousand views, a twenty
> dollar CPM, a dedicated video — five times twenty is a hundred, times one
> point four, is a hundred and forty dollars. That is the arithmetic. And now
> the trap. There is one more multiplier and it is the biggest of the lot.
> Where your audience lives moves that number by two to three times, for the
> exact same views in the exact same niche. Which is why the next thing we do
> is put your countries in the document on purpose.

[VISUAL: motion-graphics maths — the formula assembles term by term, numbers
tick, the 140 slams. Then a 2–3× multiplier bar swings the total and the trap
card lands.]

## S07 — PAGE TWO, THE AUDIENCE (~170 words)

> Page two is where your countries go, and how you handle this depends entirely
> on what they say. If your audience is mostly United States, United Kingdom,
> Canada, Australia — put it in enormous type, because it doubles or triples
> what you can charge and a sponsor will not assume it. If your audience is
> mostly somewhere else, and for a lot of us it is, put it in anyway. Here is
> why. Leaving it out does not hide it — the sponsor asks for it in the second
> email, and now you are negotiating from the back foot. Putting it in lets you
> frame it: this is a large, engaged audience in a specific region, and if that
> region is where you are growing, this channel is priced well below a
> comparable one elsewhere. That is not spin. It is true, and for the right
> sponsor it is the reason they say yes. Under the countries: age, gender, and
> device. Keep them as small horizontal bars — they are supporting evidence,
> not the headline.

[VISUAL: two versions of page two — a tier-1 audience in huge type, then a
tier-3 one with the framing paragraph; the "asked for it in email two" beat as
a chat thread; the small bar charts assemble.]

## S08 — PAGE THREE, THE RATES (~155 words)

> Page three: what it costs. Three rows — a link placement in an existing
> video, an integrated segment, a dedicated video. Each with one line saying
> what the sponsor actually gets, and a number. Put real numbers on the page.
> Not "rates on request", which just adds an email to the chain, and not
> "negotiable", which tells them your first number was fiction. Two things
> people forget here. Say how long a placement lasts — a description link with
> no end date is permanent, and permanent should not cost the same as six
> months. And say what you will not do, in one line, politely: no undisclosed
> ads, nothing you have not used. That single line raises your price, because
> it tells a serious brand you will not be the creator who embarrasses them
> next quarter.

[VISUAL: the three rate rows build; "rates on request" and "negotiable" get
struck through; a duration chip snaps onto the link row; the standards line
lands with a subtle stamp.]

[⏸ AD BREAK 2 — 1.4s silence · hard cut to section card "PART 3 · SENDING IT"]

## S09 — THE SENTENCE THAT SETS THE PRICE (~145 words)

> The kit is built. Now the bit that decides what you are paid, and it is one
> sentence in one email. When a brand asks what your rates are, do not ask what
> their budget is. Whoever says a number first sets the range, and every
> negotiation after that is an argument about how far from that number you both
> move. If you say a number, the conversation is about your number. If they say
> a number, the conversation is about theirs, and theirs will be lower. So:
> attach the kit, state the price, and add one line of reasoning — this is
> based on my last twenty eight days at this many views. Reasoning does
> something specific. It moves the discussion from whether you are worth it to
> whether the arithmetic is right, and the arithmetic is right, because you did
> it three minutes ago on page three.

[VISUAL: two negotiation timelines — "what's your budget?" collapsing downward
vs a stated number holding; the email assembles with the reasoning line
highlighted.]

## S10 — SENDING IT (~145 words)

> Export it as PDF standard, not PDF print — a media kit is read on a screen
> and print gives you a file several times the size for no visible difference.
> Then name the file properly. Not "media kit final version two". Your channel
> name, the words media kit, and the month: it is going to sit in a folder with
> forty others and it should be findable in three months when they come back.
> And they do come back. Attach it — this is the one email where attaching is
> right, because it is a single small document going to one human, not a file
> going out to a mailing list. Then update the numbers once a month. A kit
> quoting figures from last November tells a sponsor the channel has been quiet
> since November, whether or not that is true.

[VISUAL: export dialog; a file rename from "mediakit_final_v2" to a proper name;
a calendar chip pulsing "monthly"; a stale-dated kit greying out.]

## S11 — THE MISTAKES (~135 words)

> Five that cost money. Leading with subscribers, when the sponsor is buying
> views. Leaving your countries out, which does not hide them and costs you the
> framing. Eight pages, when they read three. "Rates on request", which is an
> extra email and a signal you have not decided. And quoting a permanent
> placement at a six month price, which is the one that keeps costing you after
> the deal closes. Notice that only one of those five is a design mistake. The
> other four are decisions you make before you open Canva, which is why we
> spent half this video on numbers. You can have the best looking media kit in
> your niche and still be the cheapest person in the folder.

[VISUAL: five ✕ cards flipping to ✓; four get tagged "not design".]

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~100 words)

> Before you send it, six checks. One: delivered views are on page one, bigger
> than your subscriber count. Two: your countries are in there, framed either
> way. Three: three pages, not eight. Four: real numbers on the rates page.
> Five: every placement has a duration next to it. Six, the one everybody
> skips: read it as though you were the sponsor, with forty other emails open,
> and see whether the price feels obvious by the time you reach it. If it does,
> the document is finished. If it doesn't, the problem is upstream of the
> design.

[VISUAL: 6 checks tick; check 6 shows the kit being skim-read at speed; KIT
