# How to Make a Digital Scrapbook in Canva (Print-Ready, and Actually Private)

## Facts verified 2026-08-19 — all from Canva's own Help Centre

**The trap — cropping is not deleting, and a scrapbook is full of people:**

- Verbatim, from "Choose the right download file type for your Canva design":
  > "If you download a design containing cropped images to a PDF file, the PDF
  > format will include the original image. This means someone could use a
  > third-party tool (for example, a PDF editor) to get the full, uncropped
  > image from your PDF export."
- Canva's own stated remedy in that same passage: "delete any content you don't
  wish to be viewed or recreate the image within your design before downloading
  and sharing."
- **The fix that actually scales: Flatten PDF.** From "Download designs as
  flattened PDF": flattening "converts your design with multiple layers into
  one", and Canva's own FAQ on that page says it "converts the file into a
  static image, preventing editing in software like Adobe Acrobat."
  Caveat stated on the same page: "Flattening your PDF may increase its file
  size", and accessibility tagging is not supported for flattened PDFs.
- This was covered in the pitch-deck video as a business risk. Here it is a
  **privacy** risk, which is a different video: a family scrapbook is the exact
  document where you crop somebody out.

**The one-way door — a PDF cannot come back:**

- From "Check file upload formats and requirements for Canva", on importing PDFs:
  > "When importing a PDF document to Canva, the pages become individual images
  > in an A4 document. It doesn't become a page of editable text like in Docs."
- Same page: PDF import is capped at **300 MB** and a **maximum of 500 pages**.
- So the Canva design is the master copy, permanently. If the page size is wrong
  on page one, there is no route back from the exported file.

**Hard limits (same Help Centre page unless noted):**

- Image uploads: **under 50 MB**, and **"not more than 250 million total pixels
  (width x height)"**. Formats JPEG, PNG, HEIC/HEIF, WebP.
- Storage: Canva Free **5 GB**, Canva Pro **100 GB**.
- Folders: Canva Free folders hold **up to 200 items**; Pro, Teams, Education
  and Nonprofits hold **up to 1000**. (From "Upload takes too long or doesn't
  finish".)
- Pages per design: **"Designs can have up to 350 pages."** Canva's own stated
  workaround, verbatim: "you can create a new design as a workaround. Download
  both designs and merge them using any file merger." (From "Add, duplicate,
  and delete pages".)
- Custom size fields: minimum **40 x 40 px**, maximum **8000 x 3125 px** — these
  are the stated *resize* limits, and the fields turn red outside them. (From
  "Resize designs and size limits".) NB: this is the Resize dialog. No claim is
  made here about the maximum size of a brand-new design.

**Export (from "Choose the right download file type"):**

- **PDF Print** — "for printing with a resolution of 300 dpi, with bleed, crop
  marks, and color profile (RGB or CMYK) selection option".
- **PDF Standard** — 96 dpi.
- **Download pages as separate files** — a toggle under Advanced settings that
  returns a zip of one PDF per page. Canva Pro, Teams, Nonprofits and Education
  only; on Free the toggle appears but cannot be turned on.
- Canva's own advice when a print shop says your file is blurry, verbatim:
  "Download the design as a flattened PDF", "Double the dimensions of your
  design for a bigger and clearer print." (From "Can't use design at an online
  print shop".)

**Not claimed in this video:** anything about Canva Print's own scrapbook
products, page-size availability by region, or third-party photo-book services.
None of that is documented well enough to state.

## S01 — HOOK (~130 words)

> You spend a weekend on this. Forty photos, twenty pages, the whole year of
> somebody's life laid out properly. You export the PDF, you send it to the
> family, and there are two things in that file you did not mean to put there.
> The first is every photo you cropped, still whole, still recoverable by
> anybody who opens it in a PDF editor. Canva documents this. It is not a bug.
> The second problem is quieter. That PDF cannot come back. If you got the page
> size wrong on page one, you cannot import it and fix it, because Canva turns
> an imported PDF into flat pictures, not editable pages. So this is a job you
> want to get right the first time. Let us build one properly, and I will show
> you the one checkbox that closes the first hole.

## S02 — THE TWO RULES (~150 words)

> Two rules, and the rest of this video is just carrying them out. Rule one: a
> scrapbook is a print object, even if nobody ever prints it. That means you
> decide the page size before you place a single photo, because everything you
> lay out afterwards is measured against it. Get that wrong and every page is
> wrong. Rule two: the Canva design is the master copy, forever. Not the PDF.
> The PDF is the thing you hand out. Canva's own help centre says that when you
> import a PDF, the pages become individual images in an A4 document. It does
> not become editable text or editable layouts. So an exported scrapbook is a
> photograph of your work, not your work. Keep the design. Name it something you
> will still recognise in five years, because you will come back to it when
> somebody asks for another copy.

## S03 — THE PAGE SIZE DECISION (~150 words)

> Start with a custom size, and start in inches, not pixels. The scrapbook
> standard is twelve inches by twelve inches. It is square, it is what physical
> scrapbook albums and page protectors are built around, and it is what a photo
> print shop expects when you say scrapbook. If this one is only ever going to
> live on a screen, or get printed on a home printer, use eight and a half by
> eleven or A four instead, because a twelve inch square page will not come out
> of a desktop printer without being shrunk. Type the numbers in with the unit
> set to inches and Canva does the pixel maths for you. One thing worth knowing
> before you go hunting for something enormous: in the resize dialog the custom
> size fields turn red outside forty by forty pixels at the small end and eight
> thousand by three thousand one hundred and twenty five at the large end.

## S04 — GETTING THE PHOTOS IN (~155 words)

> Now the photos, and this is where people lose an hour. Do not upload them one
> at a time from the editor. Make a folder first, put everything in it, and
> upload in one go, because you want them together and findable when you are
> twelve pages deep. Four numbers are worth knowing. Each image has to be under
> fifty megabytes, and not more than two hundred and fifty million total pixels,
> which is width times height. Phone photos are nowhere near that. Big flatbed
> scans of old album pages can get close, so if a scan refuses to upload, that is
> usually why. On storage, a free account gets five gigabytes and Pro gets a
> hundred. And folders themselves have a cap: a free account holds two hundred
> items in a folder, Pro and Teams and Education hold a thousand. A twenty year
> family archive will hit two hundred long before it hits five gigabytes.

## S05 — BUILDING A SPREAD (~195 words)

> Here is the actual build, and I am going to do one spread properly rather than
> twenty badly, because every other page is this page with different pictures.
> Start with a hero. One photo, large, off centre, taking about two thirds of
> the page. Not centred, because centred is where scrapbook pages go to look
> like certificates. Drag it in, then drag the corner handle out until it runs
> past the edge of the page on two sides. Now the supporting photos. Pull in
> three smaller ones and line them down the remaining third. Hold shift, select
> all three, and use the align tools so the left edges agree, then the spacing
> tool so the gaps agree. That single step is most of the difference between a
> page that looks made and a page that looks arranged. Now the frame. Elements,
> search frames, and drop a plain square frame on the page. Drag a photo into it
> and it snaps inside and crops itself. And last, the words. A text box, low, in
> the empty third, and one sentence about when this actually was. Not a caption.
> A sentence.

## S06 — THE CROP IS STILL IN THERE (~180 words)

> Now the thing I promised, and it is the reason I wanted to make this video.
> When you crop a photo in Canva, you are not deleting anything. You are hiding
> it. The photo is whole, underneath, and the crop is just a window over it.
> That is fine while it lives in Canva. It stops being fine when you export.
> Canva's own documentation says it plainly: if you download a design containing
> cropped images to a PDF, the PDF will include the original image, and somebody
> could use a PDF editor to get the full uncropped image back out. Think about
> what that means in a scrapbook specifically. You cropped somebody's ex
> partner out of the holiday photo. You cropped a stranger's child out of the
> beach shot. You cropped the corner of the room you did not want in there. All
> of it is still in the file you just emailed to eleven people. Nothing on
> screen tells you. The page looks exactly the way you designed it.

## S07 — THE CHECKBOX THAT CLOSES IT (~155 words)

> Two ways to fix it, and you want the second one. The first is Canva's own
> advice: delete anything you do not want seen, or rebuild the image so the
> hidden part was never there. That works, but it means remembering every crop
> you made across twenty pages, and you will not. The second way is one
> checkbox. Share, download, choose PDF Standard or PDF Print, and tick Flatten
> PDF. Flattening merges every layer into one. Canva's own words for what that
> gives you: it converts the file into a static image, and it prevents editing
> in software like Adobe Acrobat. So there are no separate photos left in the
> file to pull apart. Two things to know before you make it a habit. It makes
> the file bigger, sometimes a lot bigger. And it removes accessibility tagging,
> so if this document needs to work with a screen reader, leave it unticked and
> clean the crops by hand instead.

## S08 — HOW BIG CAN THIS GET (~135 words)

> Scrapbooks grow. So, the ceiling: a Canva design holds up to three hundred and
> fifty pages. That is a lot of scrapbook, but a wedding album with a spread per
> hour of the day, or twenty years of one child, will get there. Canva's stated
> workaround is exactly what you would guess and worth hearing anyway: make a
> second design, download both, and join them with any file merger. Which is
> another reason to fix the page size early, because the two halves have to
> match. There is also a limit coming the other way. If you ever import a PDF
> into Canva, that caps at three hundred megabytes and five hundred pages. And
> remember what you get back is pictures of pages, not pages.

## S09 — EXPORTING IT PROPERLY (~175 words)

> Export depends entirely on where it is going, and the two settings are not
> close. If it is going to a print shop or a photo book service, PDF Print. That
> is three hundred dpi, and it gives you bleed, crop marks and the colour
> profile choice. Tick bleed if anything on your page runs to the edge, which in
> a scrapbook is almost always yes, because the hero photo runs off the page.
> If it is going to family, on a screen, PDF Standard is ninety six dpi and the
> file is far smaller. Either way, flatten it. And here is one more that saves
> an argument: if you are on Pro, open advanced settings and turn on download
> pages as separate files. You get a zip with one PDF per page, which is what
> most print shops actually want. On Free that toggle is visible but will not
> turn on. And if a printer tells you your file is blurry, Canva's own advice is
> flatten it, and double the dimensions of the design.

→ **AD BREAK 2**

## S10 — MAKE THE SECOND ONE TAKE AN EVENING (~145 words)

> The first scrapbook takes a weekend. The second one should take an evening,
> and the difference is whether you built layouts or built pages. Take the
> spread we made. Delete the photos out of it, keep the frames, the alignment,
> the text box and the spacing, and duplicate that empty page five or six times
> with different arrangements. One hero. Two up. A four photo grid. A page that
> is mostly writing. A page that is one photo and nothing else, because a
> scrapbook needs to breathe. That set of empty pages is your kit. Next time you
> are not designing, you are pouring photos into slots, and the whole thing
> stays visually consistent because the geometry never moved. This is also the
> honest answer to why these take people so long: they design every page from
> scratch, twenty times, and the pages do not match.

## S11 — IF YOU ARE MAKING THESE FOR OTHER PEOPLE (~135 words)

> Two things change if you are doing this for somebody else, either as a gift or
> for money. First, you need their photos at full size, not sent over a chat app,
> because messaging apps compress pictures hard and you will not find out until
> it is printed. Ask for the originals, in a shared folder. Second, decide up
> front what they are getting: a print ready PDF, a home printable PDF, or the
> editable Canva template. Those are three different products at three different
> prices, and being vague about it is where these jobs go wrong. And if you are
> handing over anything that goes near a stranger's family photos, flatten it.
> That is not a design choice at that point. That is you being careful with
> somebody else's private pictures.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~115 words)

> Before you send it anywhere. Page size decided before page one, and the same
> on every page. Photos uploaded from a folder, each one under fifty megabytes.
> Alignment and spacing tools used on every group, not eyeballed. Every crop
> either flattened out of existence or rebuilt. Flatten PDF ticked, unless you
> need screen reader support. PDF Print with bleed for a print shop, PDF
> Standard for screens. Pages as separate files if the printer asked for that.
> Under three hundred and fifty pages, or split into two designs. And the Canva
> design itself named properly and kept, because that is the only version of
> this you can ever edit again.

> The design part of a scrapbook is the part people worry about, and it is the
> part that matters least. Consistent geometry and honest exports are what make
> it look made rather than assembled. And the crop thing genuinely matters: it
> is the difference between sending your family a scrapbook and sending them
> every photo you decided not to show them. Flatten it. There is more Canva on
