**Facts verified 7 Aug 2026:**
- **Canva has NO built-in page-turn flipbook**, on any plan. Every "Canva
  flipbook" tutorial is really Canva → PDF → a third-party viewer
  (FlippingBook, Flipsnack, Flipbooks AI…). Say this plainly.
- **PDF Standard vs PDF Print:** Standard is the digital-viewing export —
  much smaller file, links intact. Print is optimised for physical printing,
  far larger, and is the one that gets flattened.
- ⚠️ **THE TRAP: the "Flatten PDF" checkbox.** Flattening bakes the layers
  into an image and **removes every hyperlink** — sponsor links, product
  links, and the table of contents. The file still looks perfect, so nobody
  notices. Canva's own guidance: hyperlinks work in downloaded PDFs *except
  flattened ones*.
- **Internal page links exist**: link any element to "pages in this document"
  → a contents page that jumps. Works in the downloaded PDF (unflattened).
- ⚠️ Known Canva issue: **hyperlinks do not work on text with a text effect**
  applied (Lift, Neon, that family) in downloaded PDFs. Canva's own
  workaround is a duplicate, effect-free text layer carrying the link.
- **Canva Websites:** every site gets a free `my.canva.site` domain; Canva
  Free allows **up to five live websites**. A custom/own domain requires
  Pro, Teams or Education.

## S02 — WHAT A DIGITAL MAGAZINE ACTUALLY IS (~145 words)

> Before we design anything: "digital magazine" means three different files,
> and picking the wrong one is why so many of these feel dead on arrival.
> Route one, an interactive PDF — the thing people download, keep and
> forward. Route two, published as a Canva website, which your reader scrolls
> in a browser. Route three, a true page-turn flipbook, with the corner
> lifting as it flips. Now the part no tutorial will tell you: Canva does not
> have a page turn. Not on the free plan, not on Pro, not anywhere. Every
> Canva flipbook video you've seen is Canva, then a PDF, then a completely
> separate tool that reads that PDF. So route three is really route one plus
> a step. We're going to build route one properly, because it's free, it's
> yours, and it feeds the other two without a single change.

[VISUAL: three route cards; the flipbook card gets a pink "not in Canva"
stamp then a bridge arrow: Canva → PDF → external viewer.]

## S03 — CANVAS SETUP FOR SCREENS (~140 words)

> Create a design, Custom size, and switch the units to millimetres. Two
> hundred and ten by two hundred and ninety-seven — A four, portrait. Here's
> why portrait, and why single pages. A magazine on paper is designed as a
> spread: two pages side by side, one wide canvas. On a phone, held upright,
> that spread renders as a strip of text roughly the height of a postage
> stamp. Nobody zooms. They leave. So: single pages, portrait, always, unless
> you genuinely know your readers are on laptops. Then the frame you build
> inside — File, Settings, turn on Show rulers and Show margins, and drag
> guides fifteen millimetres from each edge. Everything you place lives
> inside those guides. That single habit is most of the difference between a
> page that looks designed and a page that looks typed.

[VISUAL: custom size dialog with mm typed; a landscape spread squeezed onto a
phone frame and rejected; guides drop at 15mm; the live area highlights.]

## S04 — THE GRID AND THE TYPE SCALE (~145 words)

> Now the system, because a magazine is not one page, it's forty. Inside your
> margins, drag two more guides to split the page into three even columns.
> Three columns is the workhorse: body text sits in one or two of them, an
> image takes the third, and pull quotes break across two. Then your type
> scale — four sizes, decided once. A headline, a standfirst, which is the
> larger paragraph directly under the headline, your body text, and a
> caption. Set them, then style one of each on a blank page and leave that
> page in the file. That's your parts bin. From now on you copy a heading
> from there rather than restyling one from scratch, which is how issue two
> takes an afternoon instead of a weekend. Two fonts, maximum. One for
> headlines, one for everything else.

[VISUAL: three-column guides drop; four text specimens build with size labels;
a "PARTS BIN" page flagged; a second font gets deleted with ✕.]

## S05 — THE COVER AND THE FEATURE PAGE (~185 words)

> Let's build the two pages everything else copies. The cover first. Drag one
> photograph in and let it fill the whole page — edge to edge, no margin,
> because the cover is the one page that ignores your guides. Now the
> masthead: your magazine's name, across the top, in the biggest type you'll
> ever use. Under it, an issue line — month and number. Then three cover
> lines down one side, each one a promise, not a description. "Nine pages you
> can steal" beats "Design tips". And a dark gradient behind the text if the
> photo is busy — Elements, search gradient, drop it under the type.
> Now the feature page. Headline across the top two columns. Standfirst
> underneath. Body text in two columns, image in the third. Then a pull
> quote — take a sentence from the body, make it three times the size, break
> it across two columns, and give it space above and below. That single
> element does more for how professional a spread reads than anything else on
> the page.

[VISUAL: full say-it-show-it — image dragged to bleed, masthead types,
cover lines pop in, gradient drops behind; then the feature page assembles
column by column; the pull quote scales up and pushes the body aside.]

## S06 — THE CHECKBOX THAT KILLS YOUR LINKS (~175 words)

> Now the export, and this is the section that decides whether your magazine
> earns anything at all. Share, Download, and open the file type list. You'll
> see P D F Standard and P D F Print. For anything read on a screen you want
> Standard — a much smaller file, so it opens on a phone instead of stalling,
> and on a screen the quality difference is genuinely invisible. Print is for
> paper. But that's not the trap. Underneath the file type, there's a
> checkbox: Flatten P D F. It sounds like housekeeping. What it actually does
> is bake every layer down into a flat image — and in the process it strips
> every hyperlink out of the document. Your sponsor links, your product
> links, your entire contents page: dead. And the file still looks completely
> perfect, so you send it, and you find out weeks later, if you find out at
> all. Leave Flatten unticked. Every single time. That one checkbox is the
> whole difference between a magazine that earns and a slideshow.

[VISUAL: the download panel opens; Standard vs Print with file sizes; camera
pushes onto the Flatten checkbox; ticked → every link in a page-thumbnail
snaps to grey and a chain icon breaks; unticked → links glow back.]

## S07 — THE LINKS THAT MAKE IT A MAGAZINE (~165 words)

> So what do you actually link? Select anything — text, an image, a shape, a
> button you drew — and there's a link field. It takes two kinds. A web
> address, which is your sponsors and your products. And "pages in this
> document", which is the one almost nobody uses. That's how you build a
> contents page that genuinely jumps: select the line that says Feature, hit
> link, choose page seven. Do that for every entry and your reader navigates
> a forty-page magazine on a phone with one thumb. Now a gotcha that will
> waste an evening if nobody tells you. Hyperlinks do not work on text that
> has a text effect applied — Lift, Neon, that family. The link attaches
> perfectly in the editor and it is simply dead in the exported file. The fix
> is inelegant and it works: duplicate the text, take the effect off the
> copy, put the link on the copy, and sit it exactly on top of the styled
> original.

[VISUAL: link panel with the two tabs; a contents line fires an arrow to page
7; then a Lift-effect headline with a dead link ✕, and the duplicate-layer fix
animating into place.]

## S08 — PUBLISHING IT AS A WEBSITE (~140 words)

> Second delivery route, and it costs you no extra work at all. Same design,
> Share, then Publish as a website. Canva hands you a free address — a dot
> canva dot site domain — and on the free plan you can keep up to five live
> websites at once. Your pages become something a reader scrolls in a
> browser, every link you just built still works, and here's the part that
> matters: you can edit it after publishing. A P D F is frozen the second
> somebody downloads it. Spot a typo on page nine and you're re-sending the
> file to everyone. A website you just fix. If you want your own domain
> rather than the Canva one, that's a paid plan — Pro, Teams or Education.
> My advice is do both. The P D F is the thing people keep. The website is
> the thing you can correct.

[VISUAL: Share menu → Publish as website; the my.canva.site address types
out; a browser scrolls the pages; a typo gets fixed and the live site updates
while a PDF icon stays frozen with a lock.]

## S09 — IF YOU REALLY WANT THE PAGE TURN (~130 words)

> Third route, honestly. If you specifically want the page turn — the corner
> lifting, the little paper sound — you need a flipbook viewer, and it lives
> outside Canva. The workflow never changes: download P D F Standard with
> Flatten unticked, upload that P D F to the flipbook tool, get a link back.
> Which tool you pick matters far less than what you feed it, and what you
> feed it is exactly the file we just made. Two warnings before you go
> shopping. Almost all of them are monthly subscriptions. And almost all of
> them host your magazine on their servers, which means the day you stop
> paying, your link stops working. For most people the P D F plus the Canva
> website covers it completely, and the page turn is a nice-to-have you can
> add any time.

[VISUAL: the PDF hands off to an external viewer with a page-turn animation;
then a subscription card and a "link expires if you stop paying" warning.]

## S10 — ONE DESIGN, EVERY ISSUE (~140 words)

> Here's what turns this into something monthly instead of a thing you did
> once. Everything you've built — the guides, the three columns, the type
> scale, the cover layout, the contents page, the parts bin — is one Canva
> design. For issue two, you duplicate the whole design, swap the photographs
> and the words, and the layout is already solved. Rename it, change the
> issue line, done. Nobody who publishes regularly starts from a blank page;
> they start from last issue. And there's a second thing that compounds:
> because every issue uses the same grid and the same two fonts, back issues
> start to look like a publication rather than a folder of unrelated PDFs.
> That's what makes a sponsor take you seriously — not the first issue, the
> fourth one that clearly matches it.

## S11 — THE MISTAKES THAT LOSE READERS (~130 words)

> Four I see constantly. Landscape spreads for something people read on a
> phone — beautiful on your monitor, unusable in the only place it'll
> actually be opened. Body text under about eleven point, which is fine while
> you're leaning into a screen and unreadable held at arm's length. Full
> page photographs sitting directly behind body text — that's where
> readability goes to die; put the words on a solid block over the photo
> instead. And exporting P D F Print for a digital file, which hands you a
> twenty megabyte magazine that half of email will refuse and that looks
> identical on screen to the two megabyte version. Every one of those is free
> to fix, and none of them need a better designer.

[VISUAL: four ✕ cards flipping to ✓ with the fix; a 20 MB file bouncing out
of an inbox.]
