# How to Make a Photo Album in Canva — the binding that eats your best spread

## Facts verified 2026-09-01 — Canva's own photo books page

**THE TRAP — Canva photo books are not layflat, and every double-page spread
pays for it.**

From Canva's photo books page, verbatim, in their own FAQ:

> "No, we don't offer layflat binding at this time. We do, however, offer PUR
> binding using a strong bookbinding adhesive."

This matters more for a photo album than for any other print product on the
channel, because the double-page spread — one photo running across both pages —
is the entire reason people make albums instead of framing prints. On a layflat
book the pages open to a true flat plane and the spread survives. On a PUR-bound
book they do not. PUR is a *good* adhesive and opens flatter than ordinary
perfect binding, so be accurate about this: the book is not badly made. It is
simply not layflat, and the centre of every spread curves down into the gutter.

Put a face, a horizon, or a line of text across that centre and it is bent,
shadowed, or swallowed. Nobody tells you before you order.

**THE SECOND TRAP — there is a minimum page count, and it is high.**

Same page, verbatim:

> "Design for a front cover, back cover and from 20 to 98 inside pages."

Twenty inside pages is a floor, not a suggestion. People design a twelve-page
album of a holiday, go to order it, and discover they are eight pages short.
Plan the page count before the first photo goes down, not after.

**THE THIRD — you cannot design the inside covers.**

Same page, verbatim:

> "Inside faces of the covers and endpapers (hardcover only) will be left blank."

So the dedication page people always want on the inside front cover has to be
inside page one instead.

**Paper**, for the record: "Inside pages are printed with quality 148gsm paper
in a gloss finish." Gloss is why over-saturated photos look harsher in print
than on screen — worth one line.

**What this video is NOT.** It is not the scrapbook video (Aug 2026) — that one
is twelve-by-twelve, print-at-home, decorative-layers. This is a bound book with
a spine, and the whole argument is that binding is physical and your layout has
to respect it. Do not re-explain bleed at length; it was covered in the lookbook.
One sentence, then move on.

## S01 — HOOK (~115 words)

> You pick the best photo you have. The one where everyone is actually looking.
> You run it right across both pages, edge to edge, because that is what a photo
> album is for. Three weeks later the book arrives, you open it to that page —
> and her face is folded into the middle. Half of it curving away into the spine,
> the other half in shadow. The photo is fine. The file was fine. The binding ate
> it. There is a line on Canva's own photo book page that tells you this will
> happen, and almost nobody reads it. In the next few minutes we are going to
> build a proper album, and I will show you exactly where you are allowed to put
> the things that matter.

[VISUAL: hero spread with a face dead-centre; the page curls at the gutter and

## S02 — TWO THINGS THAT MAKE THE REST MAKE SENSE (~125 words)

> Two ideas, and everything after this follows from them. The first: a photo
> album is not a slideshow. On a screen, two pages side by side are just one
> wide rectangle. In your hands they are two sheets of paper glued to a spine,
> and the middle is not a place. It is a fold. The second: your page count is
> not your decision. Canva's photo books run from twenty to ninety-eight inside
> pages, and twenty is the floor. If you have twelve pages of holiday, you are
> not making a twelve-page book — you are making a twenty-page book, and you get
> to choose whether those extra eight pages are considered or padded. Decide the
> number first and the whole build gets easier.

[VISUAL: split screen — left, a flat screen spread; right, the same spread as a
physical book curving at the gutter. Then a page-count dial snapping to twenty.]

## S03 — SET THE SIZE BEFORE YOU DESIGN (~130 words)

> Start from Canva's photo book product, not a blank presentation, because the
> size and the bleed are already correct and you cannot cleanly change them
> later. The default is eight by six inches, landscape, and landscape is the
> right instinct for an album because most of the photos you are putting in it
> are landscape. If you want square or portrait, pick it now. Changing the page
> size after you have laid out forty pages does not scale your work — it reflows
> it, and every photo you carefully positioned moves. This is the same rule as
> the lookbook video and it costs people the most time of anything in this
> tutorial. Set it once, at the start, and then never touch it again.

[VISUAL: Canva photo book product page → size picker → 8×6 landscape selected;
then a fast, ugly demo of a resize wrecking a laid-out page.]

## S04 — PLAN TWENTY PAGES BEFORE YOU PLACE ONE PHOTO (~135 words)

> Here is the part everyone skips. Open a notes app and write your twenty pages
> down as a list before you open Canva properly. Not the photos — the sections.
> Arrival. The first evening. The long walk. The bad weather day. The people.
> The last morning. Six sections across twenty pages is roughly three pages
> each, and now every page has a job. This is the difference between an album
> somebody actually looks through and a folder of pictures with a cover on it.
> It also solves the minimum for you. Instead of hunting for eight more photos
> to pad out the end, you are choosing which of your six stories deserves an
> extra spread. Same page count. Completely different book.

[VISUAL: a written 20-page list building line by line, sections colour-blocked;
then that list becoming the page thumbnails panel in Canva.]

## S05 — BUILD THE FIRST SPREAD, PROPERLY (~185 words)

> Now we build. Open the page thumbnails so you are always looking at two pages
> at once, because that is how the book will be read — nobody sees page seven on
> its own. Drag your photo in and let it fill one page rather than straddling
> both, and immediately this looks better than most albums people make. Then add
> the second photo on the facing page, smaller, with white space around it. That
> size difference is doing the work: one photo is the subject and one is the
> context, and your eye knows which to look at first. Pull up the ruler and drag
> a guide about half an inch in from every outside edge, then a wider one — call
> it three quarters of an inch — down each side of the centre fold. Keep faces,
> text and horizons outside those guides. Everything else in this video is
> detail. That gutter guide is the video. Set it up once on page one and copy it
> across every spread.

[VISUAL: two-page thumbnail view; photo fills left page; smaller photo lands
right with generous margin; rulers drag out; the centre gutter guides land as a
wide pink band; a face gets dragged into the band and the band flashes red.]

## S06 — THE BINDING, AND WHERE YOU MAY NOT PUT A FACE (~205 words)

> So why the wide guide down the middle? Because of one sentence on Canva's
> photo book page. Asked whether they offer layflat binding, Canva's answer is,
> quote: "No, we don't offer layflat binding at this time. We do, however, offer
> PUR binding using a strong bookbinding adhesive." Layflat is the binding where
> the pages open to a genuinely flat plane and a spread reads as one unbroken
> image. Canva does not offer it. PUR is a good adhesive — it opens flatter than
> ordinary perfect binding and the book will not fall apart — but it is not
> layflat, and Canva is telling you so on their own product page. What that means
> in your hands is simple. The centre of every spread curves away. Anything you
> put there is bent, and it catches a shadow along the fold. So: no faces across
> the gutter. No text across the gutter. No horizon line across the gutter,
> because a bent horizon looks broken in a way a bent sky does not. Landscapes,
> skies, water, blurred backgrounds — those cross the middle happily, because
> nobody can tell where a cloud was supposed to be.
>
> And if you want to be certain rather than hopeful, there is a test that takes
> two minutes. Print any spread at home on ordinary paper, fold it down the
> centre, and hold it like a book. That is what the binding is going to do to
> it. If the fold lands on somebody's eye, you have just saved yourself three
> weeks and the price of a hardback. I do this on the first spread of every
> album and almost never need to do it again, because by then you have trained
> your eye to see the fold before it exists.

[VISUAL: the Canva FAQ line on screen, quoted, source visible. Then a physical
book mock: face across the gutter → bent and shadowed. Swap to sky across the
gutter → reads perfectly. Then the rule as three ticks: faces no, text no,
horizons no; sky yes.]

## S07 — THE PHOTOS THEMSELVES (~145 words)

> Two things about the pictures. First, resolution. A photo from a phone is
> usually fine at the size it appears on a page — the trouble starts when you
> take a small photo and blow it up to fill a full page, and Canva will warn you
> with a little low resolution flag. Believe it. Print is unforgiving in a way
> screens are not: three hundred dots per inch is the standard, and a picture
> that looks acceptable on your laptop can land soft and mushy on gloss paper.
> Second, brightness. Those inside pages are one hundred and forty-eight gram
> gloss stock, and gloss deepens contrast. Photos that are already dark print
> darker. If a picture is moody on screen, lift the shadows slightly before you
> place it, or you will lose the detail entirely.

[VISUAL: small photo scaled up until the low-res warning appears; side-by-side
sharp vs soft print sim; then a dark photo with shadows lifted, before/after.]

## S08 — THE COVER, AND THE PAGE YOU CANNOT HAVE (~130 words)

> The cover is one photo, one line of text, and nothing else — this is the one
> place restraint always wins. Pick the photo that means the most rather than the
> most spectacular one. Then a detail that catches people out: Canva says, quote,
> "Inside faces of the covers and endpapers (hardcover only) will be left blank."
> So the dedication everyone wants on the inside front cover cannot go there. It
> is printed blank and there is nothing you can do about it. Put your dedication
> on inside page one instead — a single line, small, lots of space around it.
> Honestly it reads better there anyway, but you want to know that now rather
> than when the book arrives.

[VISUAL: cover build — photo, title, done. Then the inside-front-cover shown
greyed out with the quoted line; the dedication slides to page one.]

## S09 — GETTING IT OUT: PRINT WITH CANVA, OR TAKE THE FILE ELSEWHERE (~150 words)

> Two ways out. Order it through Canva and the sizing, bleed and binding are
> handled for you, which is the reason to start from the photo book product in
> the first place. Or export and take it to a printer you already like. If you
> do that, use PDF Print, tick crop marks and bleed, and run your background
> photos past the edge of the page — we covered why in the lookbook video, and
> the same guillotine drift applies here. Ask your printer one question before
> you send anything: is this layflat or perfect bound? If they say layflat, you
> can relax the gutter rule and design proper full-spread images. If they say
> perfect bound or PUR, everything in this video stands. That one question
> changes how you lay out the whole book.
>
> One more decision at this point: softcover or hardcover. Hardcover feels like
> the thing you keep and it sits flatter under its own weight, which helps you
> slightly with everything we just talked about. Softcover is cheaper and travels
> better. For a gift, hardcover. For four copies for the family, softcover, and
> spend the difference on making four instead of one.

[VISUAL: Canva Print order flow; then export panel with PDF Print, crop marks
and bleed ticked; then the printer question as a card with two branches.]

## S10 — THE SAME ALBUM, AS SOMETHING YOU CAN SEND (~130 words)

> Before you close the file, make the second version. Duplicate the design and
> export it as a standard PDF — not print, just PDF — at a smaller file size,
> and now you have something you can email to family who are never going to get
> a physical copy. The layout already works, because everything important is
> already clear of the middle, and on a screen there is no middle to worry
> about. This costs you about ninety seconds and it is the version most people
> will actually see. If you are making albums for someone else — a client, a
> wedding, a family — the digital copy is often the thing they share, and the
> printed one is the thing they keep.

[VISUAL: duplicate design; export as PDF standard; the file opening on a phone
and being shared in a message thread.]

## S11 — WHY THIS ONE COMPOUNDS (~140 words)

> Here is what makes albums worth the setup. Every album you will ever make has
> the same skeleton: a cover, a dedication page, sections of three, gutter guides
> on every spread. Build it once as a template and the next album is an
> afternoon of choosing photos rather than a week of designing pages. That is
> true whether you are making one a year for your own family or selling them.
> And there is a real market in the selling — people will happily pay someone
> else to turn eight hundred phone photos into a book they are not embarrassed
> by, and the reason they pay is precisely the thing we spent this video on. They
> do not know about the gutter. You do now. The design is the easy half; knowing
> where the fold is, is the part worth money.

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~95 words)

> Quick recap, and this is the order to do it in. Start from the photo book
> product so the size and bleed are right. Choose your size before you design
> anything. Plan twenty pages as sections before you place a single photo. Set a
> gutter guide about three quarters of an inch either side of the fold, and keep
> faces, text and horizons out of it. Only skies and backgrounds cross the
> middle. Check the low resolution warning on every full-page photo. Dedication
> goes on inside page one, never the inside cover. And ask your printer whether
> they are layflat.

[VISUAL: the eight checklist items ticking on in sequence, full width.]
