# Baby shower invitations in Canva — the two things your printer does to them

## Facts verified 2026-09-01

**THE TRAP — it is not Canva. It is the twelve inches between your laptop and
your printer.** Two separate things happen there, and between them they are why
home-printed invitations come out looking homemade.

**One: your printer cannot print to the edge of the paper.** Consumer inkjets
and lasers have a hard non-printable margin — commonly around three to six
millimetres on each edge, and often deeper on the trailing edge. Some printers
offer borderless, but usually only on photo paper and only at specific sizes.
So a full-bleed invitation, designed edge to edge, comes out with a white
rectangle round it.

**Two: the print dialog silently shrinks your design.** Every print dialog —
browser, Preview, Acrobat — defaults to a page-sizing option called "Fit",
"Shrink oversized pages" or "Fit to printable area". That option scales the
document down so it fits inside the printer's non-printable margins. The
reduction is small, a few percent, and it is enough that a five by seven inch
card comes out at roughly four point eight by six point seven — and no longer
fits the five by seven envelopes you bought.

The fix is three things and none of them cost money:
1. **Design inside a margin.** Keep everything important at least ten
   millimetres from the trim, and do not rely on a background reaching the edge.
2. **Print at ACTUAL SIZE.** In the print dialog choose "Actual size", "100%",
   or "Custom scale: 100%". Never "Fit".
3. **Lay up four cards on one A4 sheet with crop marks and trim them.** This is
   the one that solves everything at once — the design sits in the middle of the
   sheet, well inside the printer's margins, and because you cut through the
   artwork you get a genuine edge-to-edge card with no white border.

**Why this is NOT another licence video.** The last two videos both ended on a
Canva licence clause. This one is deliberately about the printer, because a
channel that answers every question with "check the licence" gets boring and
starts to sound like it has one trick.

**The consolidation.** The channel already has six party videos from one week in
October 2024 — favours, baby shower, decorations, event invitations, birthday
cards, wedding invitations — splitting about eleven hundred views between them.
This video is titled on the strongest single query, but the method must be said
out loud to transfer: birthday cards, favour tags, table numbers and thank-you
cards are all the same job at a different size. The pack covers all of them, so
one product replaces the six-way split rather than adding a seventh.

**Sizes worth knowing** (say them, they are the practical core):
- Five by seven inches — the standard invitation, fits A7 envelopes.
- A6, one hundred and five by one hundred and forty-eight millimetres — the
  European equivalent, fits C6 envelopes, and four fit an A4 sheet exactly.
- Favour tags and place cards are usually around five by nine centimetres.

**A6 is the quiet recommendation** because four of them tile an A4 sheet with no
waste, and C6 envelopes are sold everywhere.

## S03 — DESIGN INSIDE A MARGIN (~135 words)

> Now set the card up. Custom size, A six, millimetres. Then the part people
> skip: pull a guide about ten millimetres in from every edge and treat it as a
> wall. Every piece of text, the baby's name, the date, the address, the RSVP —
> all of it lives inside that wall.
>
> This is not the same as bleed. Bleed is about the background running past the
> edge so a trimmer cannot leave a white sliver. This is about the opposite
> edge of the same problem: your home printer physically cannot put ink in the
> outer few millimetres of the sheet, and if you have text out there it either
> vanishes or gets clipped. Ten millimetres is generous, it looks better anyway,
> and it makes the card printable absolutely anywhere.

[VISUAL: A6 canvas; ruler guides dragging in to 10 mm; text elements snapping

## S05 — THE FOUR-UP SHEET (~185 words)

> Here is the layout that solves the printing problem before it happens. Make a
> new design at A four, and place four copies of your finished A six card on it,
> two across and two down. They fit exactly — that is not a coincidence, A six
> is defined as a quarter of A four.
>
> Now, the important part. Do not butt them right up to the edge of the A four.
> Bring the whole block in slightly so there is a few millimetres of white all
> the way round the outside of the group. That white margin is the printer's
> dead zone, and now it is empty, so it does not matter.
>
> Between the cards, though, let the artwork touch. If two cards share an edge
> and both have a coloured background, they meet in a single line, and when you
> cut down that line with a guillotine or a craft knife and a steel rule, you
> get a card with colour running right to the edge. You have just given yourself
> full bleed on a printer that cannot do full bleed.

[VISUAL: four A6 cards tiling into A4; the outer margin highlighted as SAFE
DEAD ZONE; the inner shared edges highlighted; a blade running down the centre
line and lifting away a genuinely edge-to-edge card.]

## S06 — THE SETTING THAT SHRINKS EVERYTHING (~215 words)

> So you download the sheet — PDF Print, crop marks on — you open it, you press
> print, and this is where it goes wrong. Every print dialog on every machine
> has a page-sizing setting. In Acrobat it is called Page Sizing and Handling.
> In your browser it is under More settings, and it is usually called Scale. On
> a Mac it is often a tick box that says "Scale to fit".
>
> And whatever it is called, the default is some version of **Fit**. Fit means:
> shrink this document until it sits inside the area my printer can actually
> reach. It is trying to help. What it does is scale your design down by a few
> percent — and a few percent is the difference between a card that slides into
> its envelope and a card that rattles around in it.
>
> So change it. Find the setting and choose **Actual size**, or **one hundred
> percent**, or **Custom scale: one hundred**. Never Fit, and never Shrink
> oversized pages.
>
> Then check it before you cut anything: print one sheet, take a ruler, and
> measure a card. It should be one hundred and five millimetres wide. If it is
> one hundred and one, your dialog is still scaling and no amount of redesigning
> will fix it.

[VISUAL: three print dialogs — Acrobat, Chrome, macOS — with the offending
setting circled in each. Then ACTUAL SIZE selected. Then a physical ruler
against a printed card reading 105 mm, and a wrong version reading 101 mm.]

## S07 — PAPER, AND WHY IT MATTERS MORE THAN THE DESIGN (~150 words)

> Paper is the single biggest jump in perceived quality available to you, and it
> costs almost nothing. Ordinary copier paper is about eighty grams per square
> metre. An invitation wants something in the region of two hundred to three
> hundred — card, not paper. It holds its shape in the hand, the ink sits on top
> instead of soaking in, and the whole thing stops feeling like a printout.
>
> Check your printer takes it before you buy a ream. Most home printers handle
> up to about two hundred grams through the main tray and heavier through the
> rear or manual feed, one sheet at a time. It is in the manual under media
> weight.
>
> Matt or silk over gloss for anything with text on it — gloss fingerprints, and
> a baby shower invitation gets handled.
>
> And print the whole run in one sitting, from the same pack, on the same day.
> Card from two different packs can differ slightly in whiteness, and once you
> have seen it side by side in a stack you cannot unsee it. It costs nothing to
> avoid and it is the kind of detail that separates a set from a pile.

[VISUAL: three paper weights fanned and flexed — 80, 160, 300 gsm; then the rear
manual feed tray on a printer; then a fingerprint appearing on gloss and not on
matt.]

## S09 — CUTTING THEM NEATLY (~155 words)

> Cutting is where home-made invitations are usually given away, and it is
> entirely fixable. Do not use scissors. A steel rule and a craft knife on a
> cutting mat will beat scissors every time, because the blade follows the metal
> and your hand cannot wobble.
>
> Line the rule up on the crop marks, hold it down harder than feels necessary,
> and make three light passes rather than one heavy one. One heavy pass drags
> the card and tears the last centimetre; three light ones cut clean through.
>
> Cut the long lines first, all the way across the sheet, then the short ones —
> fewer setups and the pieces stay square. And if you have a guillotine, check
> it against a set square before you trust it, because a cheap guillotine that is
> half a degree out will taper twenty cards identically and you will not notice
> until they are stacked.
>
> A corner rounder, if you own one, is the last five percent.

[VISUAL: steel rule and blade on a mat, three light passes shown; long cuts then
short cuts numbered; a tapered stack from a misaligned guillotine; a corner
rounder finishing a card.]
