**Facts verified 10 Aug 2026:**
- **Mailchimp hosts files for you rather than attaching them**, and says so
  plainly: attachments "can slow or stop bulk email delivery, or cause security
  software to block" the message. Its file hosting caps non-image files at
  **10 MB**.
- **PDF attachments are a known spam-filter trigger** across deliverability
  guidance; the standard advice is to link to a hosted file instead.
- Mailchimp Transactional allows attachments but caps the **whole message at
  25 MB** — a different product from a broadcast, and still not how you deliver
  a lead magnet.
- ⚠️ **Canva CAN now collect emails** — a form dropped into a design writes
  addresses into a Canva Sheet, and forms can be shared by public view link.
  So the old "Canva can't capture emails" line is out of date and the script
  does NOT say it. What a Sheet still doesn't do is send the file or follow up.
- Working recommendation across the genre: a PDF lead magnet is **1–5 pages**,
  not thirty.

## S01 — HOOK (~120 words)

> Almost everything written about lead magnets is about the wrong half. People
> spend a weekend designing a beautiful thirty page ebook, export it, and get
> almost no subscribers, and they conclude the design wasn't good enough. It
> wasn't the design. A lead magnet is four things, and only one of them is the
> file. The other three decide whether it works, and there's one of them that
> people get so consistently wrong that it silently kills the whole thing —
> the email that delivers it. In this video we build the whole machine in

## S02 — THE FOUR PARTS (~145 words)

> So, the machine. Part one is the magnet — the file, and it matters least.
> Part two is the form, where somebody types their email address. Part three
> is the delivery, the email that actually puts the file in their hands. And
> part four is the thank you page, which is the most wasted page on the
> internet. Now here's something worth knowing, because most tutorials are out
> of date on it. Canva can capture an email now. You drop a form into a design
> and the addresses land in a Canva Sheet. That is genuinely useful and it's
> free. But understand exactly what you've got: a Sheet is a list of
> addresses, it is not an email list. It doesn't send the file. It doesn't
> follow anybody up. Nothing happens until you connect something that sends.

[VISUAL: the four cards; then a Canva form → a Sheet filling with rows → the
Sheet sits there doing nothing while a "send?" arrow pulses and fails.]

## S03 — PICK ONE PROMISE (~140 words)

> Before you open a canvas, the part that decides everything. Your lead magnet
> solves one specific problem for one specific person, and it can be described
> in a single sentence. Not "my free guide to social media". Instead: "the
> thirty captions I rotate for a bakery". Not "a nutrition ebook". Instead:
> "the seven breakfasts I make in under four minutes". The narrower it is, the
> better it converts, and this is counter-intuitive enough that most people
> get it backwards — they widen the promise to appeal to more people and it
> stops appealing to anybody. And keep it short. The working number across
> this whole genre is one to five pages, not thirty. A thing somebody can
> finish in four minutes gets finished, and a thing that gets finished is a
> thing that builds trust. A thirty page ebook mostly gets downloaded and
> never opened.

[VISUAL: vague titles struck through, replaced with narrow ones; a page-count
slider dragging from 30 down to 4 with a "finished ✓" meter rising.]

## S04 — CANVAS SETUP (~140 words)

> Now build it. Create a design, custom size, and here you have a genuine
> choice. If there's any chance somebody prints this — a checklist, a planner,
> a worksheet — use US Letter, eight point five by eleven inches, because
> that's what most of your readers have in their printer. If it's read on a
> screen and never printed, you can go wider, but portrait still wins, because
> the thing it will be opened on is a phone. Then margins. Two centimetres on
> every edge, dragged in as guides before you place a single element. And one
> more thing people skip: set your two fonts and your two colours now, in the
> Brand tab or just on a scrap page, because the fastest way to make a free
> download look free is three fonts and five colours.

[VISUAL: custom size dialog typing 8.5 × 11 in; guides drop at 2cm; a brand
strip of two fonts + two colours locks in; a "3 fonts, 5 colours" version
appears and gets binned.]

## S05 — BUILD THE PAGES (~185 words)

> Four page types, and that's the whole document. Page one, the cover: the
> promise as a headline, your name small underneath, and nothing else. Resist
> putting your logo enormous at the top — nobody downloaded this for your
> logo. Page two is the one almost everybody skips, and it's the page that
> makes people read the rest. One short paragraph: here's what this is, here's
> what you'll be able to do at the end of it, here's roughly how long it
> takes. Say four minutes and people give you four minutes. Then the content
> pages. One idea per page, a heading, a short paragraph, and white space —
> and genuinely resist filling the space, because a page that looks quick to
> read gets read. If it's a checklist, put real checkboxes on it, because
> people print those. And the last page is not a goodbye. The last page is the
> single next step: work with me, book a call, buy the thing, read this next.
> One button, one link, one sentence explaining why. Nobody is ever more warm
> towards you than the moment they finish something useful you gave them.

[VISUAL: full say-it-show-it — cover assembles, logo shrinks with ✕, the
"what you'll get" page builds, content page fills then has half its text
deleted with a "quick to read ✓", checkboxes drop in, CTA page with one button.]

## S06 — THE DELIVERY TRAP (~180 words)

> Now export it and hand it over, and this is the section that decides whether
> any of this works. Share, download, PDF standard — not PDF print, because
> print is for paper and gives you a file several times the size for a
> difference nobody can see on a screen. And now the trap. The obvious thing
> to do next is to attach that PDF to the welcome email. Do not attach it.
> Ever. Attachments are one of the most reliable ways to land in spam. Mail
> providers treat an attachment from a bulk sender as a risk, security
> software blocks them, and Mailchimp says so in its own documentation — that
> attachments can slow or stop bulk delivery, or get the message blocked
> outright. Which is why Mailchimp hosts your file for you instead, and caps
> hosted files at ten megabytes. So you upload the PDF somewhere it lives — your
> email tool's own file hosting, your website, a cloud drive with the link set
> to anyone-with-the-link — and the email contains a button that points at it.
> The file never travels inside the email. That single change is the difference
> between a lead magnet that arrives and one that quietly doesn't.

[VISUAL: export dialog PDF Standard; then an email with a paper-clip attachment
flying into a SPAM folder with a thud; rebuild as email → button → hosted file;
the 10 MB cap chip.]

## S07 — THE FORM (~160 words)

> Part two, the form, and you have two honest options. Option one, entirely
> inside Canva: drop a form into your design, wire the fields, and share it by
> public link. Addresses land in a Canva Sheet. It's free, it takes five
> minutes, and for someone collecting their first fifty emails it is genuinely
> fine. Option two is a proper email tool, and the reason to move is not
> features, it's that the sending and the following up happen automatically.
> Whichever you pick, the form itself follows three rules. Ask for the email
> and nothing else — every extra field costs you sign-ups, and you do not need
> their phone number for a PDF. Put the promise directly above the field, in
> the same words as the cover, because a person who clicked from somewhere
> else needs to recognise where they've landed. And label the button with what
> they get: send me the templates, not submit.

[VISUAL: Canva form building, fields wired, share-by-link; Sheet fills. Then a
form with five fields getting fields deleted one by one and a conversion bar
rising; button relabelled from "Submit" to "Send me the templates".]

## S08 — THE DELIVERY EMAIL (~150 words)

> The delivery email is the one everybody writes badly, and it is the easiest
> one to get right, because it should be almost empty. Subject line: the name
> of the thing they asked for. Not "welcome to my newsletter" — they didn't
> ask for your newsletter, they asked for the thing. First line: here it is.
> Then the button. Then one sentence telling them what to do first — open page
> three and do the first item — because a person who uses it in the first five
> minutes is a person who remembers you existed. And that's the whole email.
> No life story, no seven other links, no social icons. Every extra thing you
> put in there is another chance to click something that isn't the file. Send
> it immediately, not on a delay, because they are sitting there waiting for it
> right now with the tab still open.

[VISUAL: a bloated email with 7 links and a bio being stripped down to
subject → line → button → one instruction; a timer showing "immediately".]

## S09 — THE THANK-YOU PAGE (~140 words)

> When somebody submits the form they get sent somewhere, and that somewhere is
> almost always a page that says "thanks, check your email". That page is
> real estate you already paid for and it's doing one job when it could do
> three. Keep the reassurance — tell them it's on its way and to check the
> promotions tab, because that genuinely reduces the number of people who
> never find it. Then add the two things. One: a single next step, the same
> one that's on the last page of the magnet, while their attention is still on
> you. Two: something that makes the wait feel short — a two minute video, a
> quick win they can do before the email lands. This is the highest intent
> moment you will ever get with this person. They have just raised their hand.
> A page that says "thanks" and nothing else throws that away.

[VISUAL: a bare "Thanks! Check your email." page, then two blocks slot in
underneath; an intent meter spiking at the moment of submit.]

## S10 — WHAT HAPPENS NEXT (~135 words)

> One more piece and the machine is finished. A lead magnet with no follow-up
> is a download, not a list. Three emails, written once, sent automatically.
> Email one is the delivery, immediately. Email two, a day or two later: did
> you use it, here's the one thing people get stuck on, here's how to fix it.
> That email does more for how people feel about you than the magnet did,
> because it's the only one that proves a human is on the other end. Email
> three, a few days after that, is the offer — and it's allowed to be an
> offer, because you've given them two useful things first. Write these once,
> and every single person who ever downloads the file from now on goes through
> them without you doing anything. That's the difference between a file and a
> machine.

[VISUAL: three email cards on a timeline with day markers; then a stream of
avatars flowing through the same three cards automatically.]

## S11 — THE MISTAKES (~130 words)

> Five that cost sign-ups. Attaching the file to the email — the one we just
> covered, and the most expensive, because the failure is invisible to you.
> Asking for a name, a phone number and a company on a form that delivers a
> PDF. A promise so broad that nobody feels it's for them. A thirty page ebook
> when a two page checklist would have been finished and used. And no
> follow-up at all, which is the one that costs the most over a year, because
> every person who ever downloaded it just sits in a list, cooling off. Notice
> that four of those five have nothing to do with the design. You can have the
> best looking lead magnet on the internet and no list, and the reason will be
> in this list, not in the file.

[VISUAL: five ✕ cards flipping to ✓; then four of them highlighted with "not

## S12 — THE CHECKLIST (~100 words)

> Before you turn it on, six checks. One: the promise is one sentence and one
> person. Two: it can be finished in under five minutes. Three: the last page
> has exactly one next step. Four: exported PDF standard, and small. Five: the
> file is hosted, and the email links to it — not attached, never attached.
> Six, and this is the one everybody skips: sign up to your own form, with a
> real address, on a phone, and go all the way through. Did it arrive? Did it
> land in promotions? Did the button work? Six ticks and the machine runs
> without you.
